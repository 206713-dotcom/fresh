1. Understanding business activity

Purpose of business, sectors, enterprise, legal forms, objectives and stakeholders.

1/6
One-page sheet

1.1 Business activity

  • Need = essential to survive. Want = not essential. Unlimited wants + limited resources = scarcity, so choices must be made.
  • Opportunity cost = the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
  • Factors of production: land (rent), labour (wages), capital (interest), enterprise (profit). Specialisation raises output but can cause boredom and dependence.
  • Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials. Raise it by quality, branding, packaging, convenience or a USP.

1.2 Classification of businesses

  • Primary: extract natural resources. Secondary: manufacture. Tertiary: services. Chain of production links all three.
  • De-industrialisation: secondary sector shrinks and tertiary grows as a country develops.
  • Private sector is owned by individuals and aims at profit. Public sector is owned by government and aims at service.
  • Mixed economy uses both. Sector change matters because it changes jobs, growth and what the country sells.

1.3 Enterprise, growth and size

  • Entrepreneur: risk-taker, innovative, organised, decisive. A business plan covers summary, objectives, market, marketing, finance, people and operations. It wins finance and keeps the firm focused.
  • Governments support start-ups (jobs, competition, new ideas) with grants, advice, cheap loans and training.
  • Measure size by employees, value or volume of output/sales, or capital employed. Profit is not a measure of size. Methods can rank the same firm differently.
  • Grow internally (new products or markets) or externally: merger (agreed) or takeover. Horizontal = same stage. Vertical forward/backward = another stage. Conglomerate = unrelated.
  • Why grow: profit, share, economies of scale, lower risk. Why stay small: niche, owner choice, small market, lack of finance. Failures: weak management, no finance, poor product, falling demand, competition, the economy.

1.4 Types of business organisation

  • Unlimited liability: personal assets can be taken. Limited liability: owners lose only what they invested. Companies have a separate legal identity and continuity.
  • Sole trader: easy, keeps all profit, full control; unlimited liability, hard to raise finance, no continuity.
  • Partnership: more capital and skills; unlimited liability, disputes, shared profit. A deed sets rules.
  • Private limited (Ltd): limited liability, shares sold privately; accounts partly public, harder to set up, shares less easy to sell.
  • Public limited (plc): can sell shares to the public and raise large capital; loss of control, public accounts, costly, divorce of ownership and control.
  • Franchise: franchisee gets a known brand and support but pays fees and follows rules. Franchisor expands fast but risks its reputation.
  • Joint venture: share cost, risk and knowledge for a project; possible conflict. Social enterprise trades to meet a social aim.

1.5 Objectives and stakeholders

  • Objectives: survival, profit, growth, market share, service to the community / CSR. They set direction, motivate and let success be measured. They change with size, competition and the economy.
  • Internal stakeholders: owners, shareholders, managers, employees. External: customers, suppliers, lenders, government, local community.
  • Each group has its own aim (owners: profit; employees: pay and security; customers: low price and quality; community: jobs and low pollution).
  • Conflicts: higher wages cut profit; growth can pollute; lower prices squeeze suppliers; managers may want growth while owners want dividends.

2. People in business

Motivation, organisation, leadership, recruitment, training and communication.

2/6
One-page sheet

2.1 Motivating employees

  • Motivation raises output, quality and retention, and cuts absenteeism and labour turnover.
  • Taylor: money is the main motivator; piece rate and scientific management. Maslow: needs rise from physical → security → social → esteem → self-actualisation; a met need no longer motivates.
  • Herzberg: hygiene factors (pay, conditions, supervision) prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate. Motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, the work itself) do.
  • Financial: time rate, piece rate, salary, commission, bonus, profit sharing, fringe benefits. Piece rate speeds output but can hurt quality.
  • Non-financial: job rotation, enlargement, enrichment, teamworking, praise, promotion chances. Use the method that fits the worker and the job.

2.2 Organisation and management

  • Hierarchy = levels of authority. Chain of command = route orders take. Span of control = people one manager directly supervises.
  • Tall structure: narrow span, many levels, close control, slow decisions. Flat: wide span, few levels, faster, more delegation.
  • Delayering removes levels: cheaper and faster, but managers are overloaded and staff may feel insecure.
  • Delegation: a manager passes authority for a task but keeps responsibility. It develops staff and frees managers; it fails if the task or the person is wrong.
  • Leadership: autocratic (manager decides — fast, but demotivating), democratic (workers join in — slower, more ideas and motivation), laissez-faire (workers decide — good for experts, weak if they lack skill).
  • Trade unions bargain on pay, conditions and security. They can raise wages and protect members, or cause strikes and higher costs.

2.3 Recruitment, selection and training

  • Job analysis → job description (duties) and person specification (qualities). Then advertise, apply, select, appoint.
  • Internal recruitment: cheap, known person, motivates staff; small choice, leaves a gap. External: wider skills and new ideas; slower, costlier, unknown.
  • Selection: application forms, CVs, interviews, tests, references. Interviews show communication but can be biased.
  • Induction: settling-in training. On-the-job: learn while working (cheap, specific; trainer’s output falls, may pass on bad habits). Off-the-job: courses away from work (experts, but costly and less specific).
  • Dismissal = worker is at fault (misconduct, incompetence). Redundancy = job no longer exists. Both are controlled by employment law (contracts, discrimination, health and safety, unfair dismissal).
  • Workforce may shrink because of falling demand, automation, relocation or a merger.

2.4 Internal and external communication

  • Effective communication is a message sent, received and understood, with feedback. One-way has no reply. Two-way allows questions and feedback.
  • Internal = inside the firm. External = customers, suppliers, banks, government.
  • Verbal (meetings, phone): fast, feedback, no record. Written (email, letter, report): record, can be re-read; slow, may be ignored. Visual (charts, videos): clear for data; can oversimplify.
  • Barriers: jargon, language, noise, long chain of command, wrong medium, no feedback, attitude. Fix with short messages, the right medium, feedback, training and fewer layers.

3. Marketing

Customers, research, the 4Ps and a legal, joined-up marketing strategy.

3/6
One-page sheet

3.1 Marketing, competition, customer

  • Marketing finds customer needs and meets them profitably. Market-oriented firms start from the customer. Product-oriented firms start from the product.
  • Market share = firm’s sales ÷ total market sales × 100. A rising share can mean the firm is beating rivals.
  • Mass marketing: one product for the whole market — high sales, high cost, heavy competition. Niche: a small segment — less competition, can charge more, but the market is small.
  • Segment by age, gender, income, location or lifestyle so the mix fits the target.

3.2 Market research

  • Research cuts the risk of launching a product nobody wants. Primary (field): questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observation — up to date and specific, but slow and costly. Secondary (desk): internal records, government stats, reports, internet — cheap and fast, but may be old or not exact.
  • Quantitative = numbers. Qualitative = opinions and reasons. A sample must be large enough and representative or the result is biased.

3.3 Product

  • USP = what makes the product different. Branding builds loyalty and can support a higher price. Packaging protects, informs and promotes.
  • Product life cycle: introduction (low sales, high promo) → growth (sales rise) → maturity (peak, competition) → decline (sales fall).
  • Extension strategies delay decline: new markets, new uses, updated design, more promotion, lower price.
  • Boston Matrix: Star (high growth, high share — invest), Cash cow (low growth, high share — harvest cash), Problem child / question mark (high growth, low share — decide), Dog (low and low — often drop).

3.4 Price

  • Cost-plus: cost + markup — covers costs, ignores demand. Penetration: low price to enter, then maybe rise. Skimming: high price for a new product, then fall. Competitive: match rivals. Promotional: short-term cut. Dynamic: price changes with demand (often online).
  • PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price. Elastic (>1): a price rise cuts revenue. Inelastic (<1): a price rise raises revenue. PED guides whether to raise or cut price.

3.5 Place

  • Channel: producer → consumer (direct); or via retailer; or wholesaler then retailer; or an agent. More stages mean wider reach but less control and a lower share of the price.
  • E-commerce: open all hours, low rent, wide market; delivery cost, no try-before-buy, website cost, more competition.

3.6 Promotion

  • Aim: inform, persuade, remind. Above the line = advertising (TV, radio, press, online) — wide reach, costly. Below the line = sales promotion (BOGOF, coupons, point of sale), PR, sponsorship, direct mail — more targeted.
  • Choose the method that reaches the target at a cost the firm can afford. Promotion must match the brand.

3.7 Marketing strategy

  • The 4Ps must work together for the chosen segment, and change as the product, competition and economy change.
  • Entering a foreign market: different income, culture, law and competition — adapt product, price, place and promotion.
  • Legal controls protect consumers: honest description, safe products, no misleading ads, weights and measures, rights to refunds. Breaking them brings fines and a damaged brand.

4. Operations management

How goods are made, what they cost, quality, and where to locate.

4/6
One-page sheet

4.1 Production of goods and services

  • Productivity = output per worker (or per hour). Raise it with training, better machinery, motivation and improved methods.
  • Job: one-off, skilled, high price, slow (bridges, tailored suits). Batch: groups of the same item, then the line switches (bakery). Flow / mass: continuous identical output, low unit cost, high setup cost, little variety.
  • Technology and automation raise speed and consistency and cut labour cost, but are expensive and can cause redundancy.
  • Lean production cuts waste. Kaizen = small continuous improvements from workers. Just-in-time: stock arrives as needed — less storage cost and cash tied in stock, but no buffer if a supplier fails. Cell production: teams make a whole part — more responsibility.

4.2 Costs, scale and break-even

  • Fixed costs do not change with output (rent). Variable costs do (materials). Total cost = FC + VC. Average cost = total cost ÷ output.
  • Economies of scale: average cost falls as the firm grows. Purchasing (bulk discounts), technical (better machines), financial (cheaper loans), managerial (specialist managers), marketing. Diseconomies: average cost rises — poor communication, slow decisions, low morale in a large firm.
  • Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution. Contribution = selling price − variable cost per unit. Margin of safety = current output − break-even output.
  • Revenue = price × quantity. Profit = revenue − total cost. A higher price or lower costs move break-even down. Break-even assumes one product and that all output is sold at a constant price.

4.3 Achieving quality production

  • Quality means meeting customer expectations. Poor quality causes returns, complaints, a bad reputation and waste.
  • Quality control: inspect at the end. Finds faults late; some faulty goods still get through; inspectors add cost.
  • Quality assurance: check at every stage, including suppliers. Faults are caught earlier. Workers are responsible for their own quality.
  • TQM: quality is everyone’s job, all the time, aiming at zero defects. It cuts waste and builds a quality culture, but needs training and commitment.

4.4 Location decisions

  • Manufacturing looks at raw materials, transport links, labour (cost and skill), rent, power, government grants and nearness to the market.
  • Services look at customers passing by, parking, competition, rent and whether the work can be done online.
  • Relocating abroad can mean cheaper labour, new markets and grants, but also language, culture, transport cost, legal risk and a longer supply chain.
  • Legal controls (planning permission, pollution rules) can block or raise the cost of a site.

5. Financial information and decisions

Where money comes from, cash versus profit, the accounts, and ratios.

5/6
One-page sheet

5.1 Needs and sources of finance

  • Start-up capital launches the firm. Expansion capital grows it. Capital expenditure buys non-current assets. Revenue expenditure is day-to-day cost.
  • Internal: retained profit (no interest, but may be too small), sale of surplus assets, owners’ savings. External: bank overdraft (short-term, flexible, interest), trade credit, bank loan, leasing, hire purchase, share issue, debentures, venture capital, government grants.
  • Choose by amount, how long it is needed, interest and fees, whether security is required, gearing risk, and whether owners lose control (shares do; loans do not).
  • Limited companies can sell shares. Sole traders and partnerships cannot. A new firm often cannot use retained profit.

5.2 Cash flow and working capital

  • Cash is money in the bank now. Profit is revenue minus costs over a period. A firm can be profitable and still run out of cash.
  • Net cash flow = cash in − cash out. Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow. A cash-flow forecast shows future shortages so the firm can act early.
  • Working capital = current assets − current liabilities. It pays day-to-day bills. Too little → insolvency. Too much → cash sitting idle in stock or receivables.
  • Short of cash: arrange an overdraft, chase receivables, delay payables, cut stock, factor debts, postpone capital spending, or inject owner funds.

5.3 Income statement

  • Revenue − cost of sales = gross profit. Gross profit − expenses = profit for the year. Some of that is kept as retained profit; the rest may be paid as drawings or dividends.
  • Stakeholders use it to judge whether the firm is profitable and whether profit is rising. It does not show cash or the value of assets.

5.4 Statement of financial position

  • A snapshot on one date. Assets = liabilities + equity.
  • Non-current assets: kept over a year (premises, machines; also intangibles such as brand). Current assets: inventory, trade receivables, cash.
  • Current liabilities: due within a year (trade payables, overdraft). Non-current liabilities: longer loans. Equity: share capital + retained profit.
  • Capital employed is the long-term capital in the business (equity + non-current liabilities). Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities.

5.5 Ratio analysis

  • Gross profit margin = gross profit ÷ revenue × 100. Profit margin = profit for the year ÷ revenue × 100. ROCE = profit for the year ÷ capital employed × 100. These measure profitability.
  • Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities. Acid test = (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities. These measure liquidity. A current ratio near 1.5–2 is often safer; below 1 means current liabilities exceed current assets.
  • Compare with last year and with similar firms. A ratio alone does not explain the cause.
  • Limits: accounts are historical, can be window-dressed, ignore qualitative factors, and different firms use different accounting policies.

6. External influences on business activity

The economy, ethics and the environment, and trading across borders.

6/6
One-page sheet

6.1 Economic issues

  • Business cycle: growth → boom → recession → slump, then recovery. In a boom, sales and prices rise and unemployment is low, but costs and interest rates may rise. In a recession, demand, profit and jobs fall; firms may cut output and prices.
  • Inflation: a general rise in prices. Costs rise, real incomes may fall, and exporting gets harder if home prices rise faster than abroad. Unemployment: more people seeking work can lower wage pressure, but also cuts demand for many products.
  • Economic growth (rising GDP) usually means higher sales, but also a tighter labour market.
  • Fiscal policy: government changes tax and its own spending. Higher income tax cuts consumer spending. Higher corporation tax cuts profit left for investment. More government spending can raise demand.
  • Monetary policy: interest rates. A rise makes borrowing dearer, so firms invest less and consumers with loans spend less; saving becomes more attractive. A cut does the opposite.
  • Supply-side policies (training, less regulation, more competition) aim to raise the economy’s productive capacity.

6.2 Environmental and ethical issues

  • A business activity can create externalities. External costs fall on others (pollution, congestion). External benefits help others (jobs, training) without them paying the full cost.
  • Pressure groups and boycotts can force cleaner or fairer behaviour. Ignoring them risks lost sales and new laws.
  • Ethical issues include child labour, low wages, unsafe conditions, animal testing, misleading ads and waste. Acting ethically can raise costs in the short run and protect the brand and sales in the long run.
  • Sustainable development meets today’s needs without stopping future generations meeting theirs. Responses: recycling, renewable energy, greener packaging, less waste. Weigh the extra cost against reputation, new customers and possible legal penalties.
  • Social responsibility means considering employees, customers, suppliers and the community, not only owners’ profit.

6.3 Business and the international economy

  • Globalisation: firms buy, make and sell across more countries because of freer trade, cheaper transport and the internet. Opportunities: larger markets, cheaper supplies, new ideas. Threats: more competition, and dependence on foreign economies.
  • A multinational produces in more than one country. For the host: jobs, tax, skills and exports, but also profits sent home, local firms squeezed, and possible pollution or political influence.
  • Free trade has no barriers. Protectionism limits imports: tariffs (a tax on imports), quotas (a quantity limit), subsidies to home firms. Barriers protect jobs at home but raise prices and can start retaliation.
  • Exchange rates. Appreciation (currency stronger): imports cheaper, exports dearer — good for firms that buy foreign materials, harder for exporters. Depreciation (weaker): imports dearer, exports cheaper — the reverse. SPICED: Stronger currency, Imports Cheaper, Exports Dearer.
