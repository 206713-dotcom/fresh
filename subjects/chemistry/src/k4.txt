## The evidence
The order comes from experiments. For a fair comparison, use the same size of metal piece and the same volume and concentration of acid, then compare:

- how **fast bubbles** of hydrogen are produced;
- how much the **temperature rises**.

## Displacement
A more reactive metal **displaces** a less reactive metal from its compound.

- zinc + copper sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper. Zinc is higher, so it reacts.
- copper + zinc sulfate: no reaction. Copper is lower.

## Why carbon and hydrogen are in the list
- **Carbon** can take the oxygen from the oxides of metals **below** it. This is how those metals are extracted.
- Metals **above hydrogen** react with dilute acids to give hydrogen. Metals below it do not.

## Reactivity and discovery
Unreactive metals such as gold are found as the metal itself, so people have used them for thousands of years. Very reactive metals such as potassium were only isolated about two hundred years ago.

S: Most metals are found in the ground joined to oxygen, in rocks called ores. To get the metal you have to take the oxygen away. For metals below carbon in the reactivity series, heating the oxide with carbon does the job.
<<<
## Metals in the Earth
!def Ore | A rock that contains enough of a metal compound to make it worth extracting the metal.

- **Unreactive** metals such as gold and platinum are found **native**: as the metal itself.
- Most other metals are found as **compounds**, usually oxides.

## Extraction with carbon
Carbon is more reactive than the metals below it in the series, so it **takes the oxygen** from their oxides. This is a displacement reaction.

metal oxide + carbon → metal + carbon dioxide

| Metal | Reaction |
| iron | iron oxide + carbon → iron + carbon dioxide |
| zinc | zinc oxide + carbon → zinc + carbon dioxide |
| copper | copper oxide + carbon → copper + carbon dioxide |

$$\mathrm{2CuO + C \rightarrow 2Cu + CO_2}$$

!def Reduction | A reaction in which a substance loses oxygen.

The metal oxide is **reduced** to the metal. The carbon gains oxygen, so it is **oxidised**.

### In the laboratory
Mix black copper oxide with charcoal powder and heat strongly in a test tube. Pink-brown copper appears.

### In industry
Iron is extracted in a **blast furnace**. Iron ore is heated with coke, which is nearly pure carbon. Molten iron runs out at the bottom.

## Which method for which metal?
| Position in the reactivity series | Method | Examples |
| above carbon | **electrolysis**: using electricity to split the compound | potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium |
| below carbon | **heating with carbon** | zinc, iron, lead, copper |
| very unreactive | found **native**: only needs separating from the rock | silver, gold |

Electrolysis uses a great deal of electricity, which is why aluminium costs more to produce than iron.
>>>
W: Carbon cannot extract aluminium. Aluminium is more reactive than carbon, so the carbon cannot take its oxygen.
W: Reduction is loss of oxygen. Oxidation is gain of oxygen. They happen together.
M: Define ore :: a rock containing **enough metal (compound)** to be **worth extracting**
M: How iron is extracted from iron oxide :: **heating with carbon**
M: Define reduction :: **loss of oxygen**
M: Why carbon can extract iron :: carbon is **more reactive** than iron
M: Method for extracting aluminium :: **electrolysis**
M: Why gold needs no chemical extraction :: it is found **native** (uncombined) because it is **unreactive**

S: The Earth is built in layers: a thin rocky crust, a thick mantle, and a core of iron and nickel. Nearly all of the crust is made of just eight elements, and almost half of it, by mass, is oxygen locked up in rock.
<<<
## The layers of the Earth
| Layer | State | What it is like |
| **crust** | solid | thin and rocky: about 5 to 70 km thick. We live on it |
| **mantle** | solid, but it can flow very slowly | by far the thickest layer of rock, about 2900 km |
| **outer core** | liquid | molten iron and nickel |
| **inner core** | solid | iron and nickel, hotter than 5000 °C but kept solid by enormous pressure |

Compared with the whole Earth, the crust is about as thin as the skin on an apple.

The crust and the top of the mantle are broken into huge pieces called **tectonic plates**. They move a few centimetres a year, which causes earthquakes and volcanoes.

| silicon | about 28% |
| aluminium | about 8% |
| iron | about 5% |
| calcium, sodium, potassium, magnesium | about 11% between them |
| all the other elements | about 2% |

Most of these elements are found as **compounds**. Oxygen and silicon are joined to metals in the minerals that make up rock.

## Rocks and minerals
- A **mineral** is a naturally occurring element or compound with its own crystal structure, such as quartz.
- A **rock** is a mixture of minerals. Granite contains quartz, feldspar and mica.

## How do we know?
Nobody has drilled further than about 12 km. Our picture of the inside comes from the way earthquake waves travel through the Earth and change speed and direction at each layer.

== k3-8-2
S: Rocks are slowly being made, worn down and remade. Igneous rock forms when molten rock cools, sedimentary rock when layers of sediment are squeezed and stuck together, and metamorphic rock when heat and pressure change a rock without melting it. The rock cycle links all three.
<<<
## The three types of rock
| | Igneous | Sedimentary | Metamorphic |
| How it forms | **molten rock cools** and solidifies | layers of **sediment** are compacted and cemented | existing rock is changed by **heat and pressure** |
| What it looks like | interlocking **crystals** | **grains** in **layers** | crystals, often in **bands** |
| Fossils? | never | often | rarely, and distorted |
| Porous? | no | usually | no |
| Examples | granite, basalt | sandstone, limestone, mudstone | marble, slate |

## Igneous rocks
Molten rock is called **magma** underground and **lava** when it reaches the surface. The size of the crystals tells you how fast it cooled.

| Where it cooled | Speed of cooling | Crystals | Example |
| underground (intrusive) | slow | **large** | granite |
| at the surface (extrusive) | fast | **small** | basalt |

## Sedimentary rocks
- **Weathering** breaks rock into pieces. Freeze-thaw is physical weathering; acidic rain on limestone is chemical weathering; plant roots cause biological weathering.
- **Erosion and transport:** rivers, wind and ice carry the pieces away, rounding them as they go.
- **Deposition:** the pieces settle as **sediment**, in layers, usually on a sea bed.
- **Compaction:** the weight of new layers squeezes the water out of the layers below.
- **Cementation:** minerals crystallise between the grains and glue them together.

The oldest layers are at the bottom. Remains of plants and animals trapped in the sediment may become fossils.

## Metamorphic rocks
Deep in the crust, heat and pressure change the crystals of a rock **without melting it**.

| Original rock | Metamorphic rock |
| limestone | marble |
| mudstone | slate |

## The rock cycle
- Any rock at the surface is **weathered and eroded**, and its sediment forms **sedimentary** rock.
- Any rock that is buried and heated under pressure becomes **metamorphic** rock.
- Any rock that gets hot enough **melts** to form magma, which cools into **igneous** rock.
- **Uplift** pushes buried rock back to the surface, and the cycle continues.

The cycle takes millions of years. The material of the Earth is recycled again and again.
>>>

W: Large crystals mean slow cooling, not fast.

M: Why granite has large crystals :: it **cooled slowly**, underground
M: How sedimentary rock forms :: sediment is **deposited in layers**, then **compacted** and **cemented**
M: How metamorphic rock forms :: existing rock is changed by **heat and pressure** without melting
M: Which rock type contains fossils :: **sedimentary**
M: Metamorphic rock formed from limestone :: **marble**

== k3-8-3
S: Everything we make comes from the Earth: metals from ores, plastics and fuels from crude oil, glass from sand. These resources are limited and will not be replaced in our lifetimes. Recycling makes them last longer, and for some materials it works far better than for others.
<<<
## A limited supply
!def Finite resource | A resource that is used up faster than it is replaced, so it will eventually run out.

| Resource | What we get from it |
| metal ores | iron, aluminium, copper |
| crude oil | petrol, diesel and most plastics |
| natural gas and coal | fuels |
| sand and limestone | glass, cement and concrete |

Extracting them has a cost. Mines and quarries scar the land, processing ores uses a great deal of energy, and burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide.

## Recycling
Recycling means collecting used materials and processing them into new products.

### What recycling saves
- **Raw materials:** the ore stays in the ground.
- **Energy:** melting an old drinks can takes about 5% of the energy needed to extract the same aluminium from its ore.
- **Land:** less mining and quarrying, and less waste going to landfill.
- **Carbon dioxide:** less energy used usually means less fuel burned.

### What limits it
- Collecting, transporting, sorting and cleaning the waste all use energy and money.
- Some materials are hard to separate, such as mixed plastics or drinks cartons with layers of card, plastic and foil.
- Some materials get worse each time. Paper fibres become shorter, and recycled plastic is often lower in quality.

## How well does it work?
| Material | How well it recycles |
| aluminium, steel | very well: can be recycled again and again with no loss of quality |
| glass | very well, if the colours are sorted |
| paper and card | well, but only a limited number of times |
| plastics | poorly unless they are sorted by type |

## Better than recycling
Recycling still uses energy. It is better to **reduce** what you use, then **reuse** things, and **recycle** what is left.
>>>

W: Finite does not mean nearly gone. It means the supply cannot be replaced once it is used.
M: Define finite resource :: one that will **run out** because it is **not replaced** as fast as it is used
M: Two benefits of recycling a metal :: **saves ore (raw material)**, **saves energy**, **less mining**, **less landfill** (any two)
M: Why recycling aluminium is worthwhile :: it uses **much less energy** than extracting it from its ore
M: A problem with recycling plastics :: they must be **sorted by type**, and the **quality falls**
M: The order of the three Rs :: **reduce**, **reuse**, **recycle**

== k3-8-4
S: Carbon atoms are constantly on the move between the air, living things, the oceans and the rocks. Photosynthesis takes carbon dioxide out of the air. Respiration, decay and burning put it back. This endless loop is the carbon cycle.
<<<
## Where the carbon is
| Store | Form of the carbon |
| atmosphere | carbon dioxide gas |
| living things | carbohydrates, fats and proteins |
| oceans | dissolved carbon dioxide |
| fossil fuels | coal, oil and natural gas |
| rocks | carbonates, such as limestone (calcium carbonate) |

## Processes that move carbon
| Process | What happens | Effect on carbon dioxide in the air |
| **photosynthesis** | plants use carbon dioxide and water to make glucose | **removes** it |
| **dissolving** | carbon dioxide dissolves in the oceans | **removes** it |
| **respiration** | plants, animals and microorganisms release energy from food | **adds** it |
| **decomposition** | microorganisms break down dead plants and animals, and respire | **adds** it |
| **combustion** | burning wood or fossil fuels | **adds** it |

carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (photosynthesis)

glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (respiration)

## Following a carbon atom
- A plant takes in carbon dioxide and builds the carbon into its leaves.
- An animal eats the plant, and the carbon becomes part of the animal: this is **feeding**.
- The animal respires, and some of the carbon returns to the air as carbon dioxide.
- When the plant or animal dies, decomposers break it down and release the rest.

## The slow part of the cycle
- Over millions of years, dead plants and sea creatures that were buried without decaying turned into **fossil fuels**.
- Shells of sea creatures built up on the sea bed and became **limestone**.
- Carbon stored like this stays locked away until the fuel is burned or the rock is heated.

## A cycle in balance
For thousands of years the carbon dioxide removed from the air roughly equalled the carbon dioxide added. Burning fossil fuels returns, in a few decades, carbon that took millions of years to store.
>>>
W: Plants respire as well as photosynthesise. They give out carbon dioxide too, all day and all night.
W: Carbon atoms are not used up. The cycle moves the same atoms round and round.
M: Process that removes carbon dioxide from the air :: **photosynthesis**
M: Processes that add carbon dioxide to the air :: **respiration**, **combustion**, **decomposition**
M: How carbon passes from plants to animals :: by **feeding**
M: Role of decomposers :: break down dead organisms and release carbon dioxide by **respiration**
M: How fossil fuels formed :: from **dead organisms** buried for **millions of years**

C: [1] How does the carbon in a dead leaf return to the air? | by photosynthesis | by evaporation | *by the respiration of decomposers | by condensation | Microorganisms feed on the leaf and release carbon dioxide as they respire.
C: [1] Which is a long-term store of carbon? | a cloud | *limestone rock | a river | the wind | Limestone is calcium carbonate formed from the shells of sea creatures.

S: Dry air is about four-fifths nitrogen and one-fifth oxygen, with a little argon and a trace of carbon dioxide. That trace matters: carbon dioxide traps heat, and human activity has raised its level by half, which is warming the planet and changing the climate.
<<<

Air also contains **water vapour**. The amount varies from place to place and day to day.

## The greenhouse effect
- Energy from the Sun warms the Earth's surface.
- The warm surface gives out heat, as infrared radiation.
- **Greenhouse gases**, mainly carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour, absorb some of this heat and stop it escaping to space.

This natural effect keeps the Earth warm enough for life. Without it the average temperature would be about −18 °C.

## How people add carbon dioxide
| Activity | Why it raises carbon dioxide |
| **burning fossil fuels** in power stations, vehicles, factories and homes | combustion releases carbon stored for millions of years |
| **deforestation** | fewer trees to take in carbon dioxide; burning or rotting wood releases more |
| **making cement** | limestone is thermally decomposed, giving off carbon dioxide |

Farming adds **methane**, another greenhouse gas, from cattle and rice fields.

## The evidence
- Before about 1750 the air contained about 280 parts per million of carbon dioxide. It is now more than 420 parts per million.
- The Earth's average surface temperature has risen by more than 1 °C over the same period.

More greenhouse gas means more heat is trapped. This extra warming is called **global warming**, and it is causing **climate change**.

## The impact on climate
- Ice sheets and glaciers melt, and sea water expands as it warms, so **sea levels rise** and low-lying land floods.
- **Extreme weather** becomes more common: heatwaves, droughts, storms and floods.
- Habitats change, and some species cannot adapt or move fast enough.
- Growing food becomes harder in some regions.

## What can be done
- Use **renewable energy**, such as solar and wind, instead of fossil fuels.
- Use less energy: insulate buildings, use efficient machines, travel by public transport.
- Protect forests and plant trees.
