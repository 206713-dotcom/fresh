## The rate equation
For a reaction between A and B:

$$\text{rate} = k[\mathrm{A}]^m[\mathrm{B}]^n$$

| Term | Meaning |
| **rate equation** | an equation showing how the rate depends on the concentrations of the reactants |
| **order of reaction** (with respect to a reactant) | the **power** to which the concentration of that reactant is raised in the rate equation: m or n |
| **overall order** | the **sum** of the orders: m + n |
| **rate constant, k** | the constant of proportionality in the rate equation; it changes only with temperature (or a catalyst) |
| **half-life, t½** | the **time taken for the concentration of a reactant to fall to half** its initial value |
| **rate-determining step** | the **slowest step** in a reaction mechanism |
| **intermediate** | a species **formed in one step and used up in a later step**; it is not in the overall equation |

!key Orders must be found **by experiment**. They cannot be read from the balanced equation.

## What each order means
| Order | Effect of doubling the concentration | Rate–concentration graph | Concentration–time graph |
| **zero** | **no change** in rate | **horizontal** straight line | **straight line** sloping down (constant gradient) |
| **first** | rate **doubles** | **straight line through the origin** | a curve with a **constant half-life** |
| **second** | rate goes up **four times** | an **upward curve** | a steeper curve; the **half-life gets longer** each time |

## Finding orders from initial rates
Compare two experiments in which only **one** concentration changes.

| Experiment | [A] / mol dm⁻³ | [B] / mol dm⁻³ | Initial rate / mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
| 1 | 0.10 | 0.10 | 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ |
| 2 | 0.20 | 0.10 | 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ |
| 3 | 0.20 | 0.20 | 1.6 × 10⁻³ |

- Experiments 1 and 2: [A] doubles, [B] constant; the rate doubles. **First order** with respect to A.
- Experiments 2 and 3: [B] doubles, [A] constant; the rate goes up four times. **Second order** with respect to B.

$$\text{rate} = k[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}]^2 \qquad \text{overall order} = 3$$

The **initial rate** is found from the gradient of the tangent at t = 0 on a concentration–time graph.

## The rate constant
Rearrange the rate equation and substitute one experiment.

$$k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}]^2} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-4}}{0.10 \times 0.10^2} = 0.20\ \text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{dm}^6\,\text{s}^{-1}$$

### Units of k
| Overall order | Units of k |
| 0 | mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
| 1 | s⁻¹ |
| 2 | mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹ |
| 3 | mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹ |

Work them out each time: units of rate ÷ units of the concentration terms.

## Half-life
For a **first-order** reaction, the **half-life is constant**: it is **independent of the concentration**. On a concentration–time graph, the time for the concentration to fall from 0.80 to 0.40 is the same as from 0.40 to 0.20.

$$k = \frac{0.693}{t_{1/2}}$$

**Example.** A first-order reaction has a half-life of 150 s.

- k = 0.693 ÷ 150 = 4.62 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹
- After 450 s (three half-lives), the fraction remaining is ½ × ½ × ½ = ⅛.

To test for first order: measure two or three successive half-lives from the graph. If they are equal, the reaction is first order.

## Mechanisms
Most reactions happen in several steps. The rate depends on the **slowest step**, the **rate-determining step** (RDS).

**The link:** the rate equation contains the species that take part in the rate-determining step (and in any step before it). **The order with respect to a species is the number of molecules of it involved up to and including the rate-determining step.** A reactant that appears only after the RDS is zero order: it is not in the rate equation.

### Example 1
$$\mathrm{NO_2 + CO \rightarrow NO + CO_2} \qquad \text{rate} = k[\mathrm{NO_2}]^2$$

CO is not in the rate equation, so it is not involved in the RDS. Two NO₂ molecules are.

- Step 1 (slow): $\mathrm{NO_2 + NO_2 \rightarrow NO + NO_3}$
- Step 2 (fast): $\mathrm{NO_3 + CO \rightarrow NO_2 + CO_2}$

The steps add up to the overall equation. NO₃ is an **intermediate**.

### Example 2: hydrolysis of halogenoalkanes
| Halogenoalkane | Rate equation | Mechanism |
| (CH₃)₃CBr, tertiary | rate = k[(CH₃)₃CBr] | **SN1**: only the halogenoalkane is in the slow step (it ionises) |
| CH₃CH₂Br, primary | rate = k[CH₃CH₂Br][OH⁻] | **SN2**: both species collide in the single step |

### Checking a proposed mechanism
- The steps must **add up** to the overall equation.
- The species in the **slow step** (and before it) must **match the rate equation**.
- An **intermediate** is made in one step and used in a later one.
- A **catalyst** is used in an early step and **regenerated** in a later one. It can appear in the rate equation even though it is not in the overall equation.

## Temperature and the rate constant
Raising the temperature **increases the rate constant, k**, and so increases the rate. A greater proportion of molecules have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy, so more collisions are effective. The increase is steep: k roughly doubles for each 10 K rise in many reactions.

Changing a concentration changes the rate but **not** k.

## Two kinds of catalyst
| | Phase | Example |
| **homogeneous** | the **same phase** as the reactants | Fe²⁺(aq) in the reaction of I⁻(aq) with S₂O₈²⁻(aq) |
| **heterogeneous** | a **different phase** from the reactants | solid iron with gaseous N₂ and H₂ |

Both provide an alternative route with a **lower activation energy**.

## Heterogeneous catalysis
The reaction takes place on the **surface** of the solid catalyst, at active sites.

- **Adsorption:** reactant molecules **form bonds to the surface** of the catalyst.
- **Bond weakening:** the bonds **within the reactant molecules are weakened** (and the molecules are held close together, in a favourable orientation). The activation energy is lowered.
- **Reaction:** new bonds form between the adsorbed atoms or molecules.
- **Desorption:** the **product molecules leave the surface**, freeing the active sites for more reactants.

### Iron in the Haber process
$$\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g)}$$

- Nitrogen and hydrogen molecules are **adsorbed** onto the surface of the iron.
- The **N≡N and H–H bonds are weakened** and break.
- Nitrogen and hydrogen atoms on the surface join to form NH₃.
- The **ammonia desorbs** from the surface.

### Palladium, platinum and rhodium in catalytic converters
$$\mathrm{2NO(g) + 2CO(g) \rightarrow N_2(g) + 2CO_2(g)}$$

- NO and CO molecules are **adsorbed** onto the metal surface.
- Their **bonds are weakened**.
- The atoms rearrange to form N₂ and CO₂.
- The products **desorb**.

The metals are spread thinly over a ceramic honeycomb to give a **large surface area**.

### How strong should adsorption be?
- Too **weak**: few molecules are adsorbed, and their bonds are not weakened enough.
- Too **strong**: the products cannot leave, and the surface stays blocked.

A **poison** is a substance that is adsorbed strongly and blocks the active sites: lead compounds poison catalytic converters.

## Homogeneous catalysis
The catalyst **takes part in one step and is regenerated in a later step**. Often it works by changing oxidation state and changing back.

### Oxides of nitrogen in the oxidation of sulfur dioxide
In the atmosphere, all the species are gases.

$$\mathrm{SO_2 + NO_2 \rightarrow SO_3 + NO}$$

$$\mathrm{NO + \tfrac{1}{2}O_2 \rightarrow NO_2}$$

Overall: $\mathrm{SO_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}O_2 \rightarrow SO_3}$. The NO₂ is used and then **reformed**. The SO₃ goes on to form sulfuric acid in acid rain.

### Iron ions in the reaction of iodide with peroxodisulfate
$$\mathrm{S_2O_8^{2-}(aq) + 2I^-(aq) \rightarrow 2SO_4^{2-}(aq) + I_2(aq)}$$

Without a catalyst this is **slow**: both reactants are **negative ions, which repel** each other, so the activation energy is high.

**With Fe²⁺ ions:**

$$\mathrm{S_2O_8^{2-} + 2Fe^{2+} \rightarrow 2SO_4^{2-} + 2Fe^{3+}}$$

$$\mathrm{2Fe^{3+} + 2I^- \rightarrow 2Fe^{2+} + I_2}$$

- Each step is between a **positive ion and a negative ion**, which attract, so both steps have a lower activation energy.
- Fe²⁺ is oxidised to Fe³⁺ in the first step and **regenerated** in the second.
- **Fe³⁺ works too**: the two steps simply happen in the other order.

This is possible because iron, a transition element, has two stable oxidation states that interconvert easily. The E⦵ value of Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ (+0.77 V) lies between those of S₂O₈²⁻/SO₄²⁻ (+2.01 V) and I₂/I⁻ (+0.54 V), so both steps are feasible.

## Thermal stability of the nitrates and carbonates
**Trend:** thermal stability **increases down the group**. A higher temperature is needed to decompose the compounds of the lower elements.

$$\mathrm{MCO_3 \rightarrow MO + CO_2} \qquad \mathrm{2M(NO_3)_2 \rightarrow 2MO + 4NO_2 + O_2}$$

### Explanation: polarisation of the anion
- The carbonate and nitrate ions are **large anions**, with electron clouds that are easily distorted.
- A Group 2 cation, with its 2+ charge, attracts the electrons of the anion and **polarises** it: it distorts the electron cloud, pulling electron density towards itself.
- This **weakens the C–O (or N–O) bonds** within the anion, so the anion breaks up more easily, giving the oxide.
- **Down the group, the cation radius increases** while the charge stays 2+. The **charge density decreases**, so the cation has **less polarising power**.
- The anion is **polarised (distorted) less**, its bonds are weakened less, and **more energy is needed** to decompose it.

| Cation | Mg²⁺ | Ca²⁺ | Sr²⁺ | Ba²⁺ |
| Ionic radius / nm | 0.072 | 0.100 | 0.113 | 0.136 |
| Polarising power | highest | | | lowest |
| Stability of carbonate and nitrate | lowest | | | highest |

The same idea explains why Group 1 carbonates (1+ cations, lower charge density) are more stable than Group 2 carbonates.

## Solubility of the hydroxides and sulfates
| | Trend down the group | ΔH⦵sol |
| **hydroxides** | solubility **increases** | becomes **more exothermic** (less endothermic) |
| **sulfates** | solubility **decreases** | becomes **more endothermic** (less exothermic) |

### The two competing enthalpy changes
$$\Delta H_{sol} = -\Delta H_{latt} + \sum \Delta H_{hyd}$$

Down the group the cation gets larger, so:

- the **lattice energy becomes less exothermic** (the ions are further apart)
- the **enthalpy change of hydration of the cation becomes less exothermic** (water molecules are attracted less strongly).

Solubility depends on **which of the two changes more**.

### Hydroxides: small anion
The hydroxide ion is small, so the **size of the cation makes a big difference** to the distance between the ions. Down the group the **lattice energy becomes less exothermic by more** than the hydration enthalpy does. Less energy is needed to break the lattice, compared with the energy returned by hydration, so **ΔHsol becomes more exothermic** and the hydroxides become **more soluble**.

### Sulfates: large anion
The sulfate ion is much larger than any of the cations, so the distance between the ions is dominated by the anion. Changing the cation makes **little difference to the lattice energy**. But the **hydration enthalpy of the cation still becomes much less exothermic**. Hydration returns less and less energy while the lattice needs about the same, so **ΔHsol becomes more endothermic** and the sulfates become **less soluble**.

| hydroxides | much less exothermic | less exothermic | **lattice energy** | increases |
| sulfates | only slightly less exothermic | less exothermic | **hydration enthalpy** | decreases |
