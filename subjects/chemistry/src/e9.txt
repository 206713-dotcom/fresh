S: Thin-layer chromatography separates a mixture on a plate coated with a solid such as aluminium oxide. A solvent creeps up the plate, and each substance travels a distance set by how strongly it sticks to the solid and how well it dissolves in the solvent. The Rf value identifies it.
<<<
## The parts of a TLC experiment
| Term | Meaning |
| **stationary phase** | the phase that **does not move**: a thin layer of a **solid** such as **aluminium oxide** (alumina, Al₂O₃) or silica (SiO₂), coated on a glass, metal or plastic plate |
| **mobile phase** | the phase that **moves**: a **polar or non-polar liquid solvent** that travels up the plate |
| **baseline** | the line (drawn in pencil) on which the samples are spotted at the start |
| **solvent front** | the **furthest point reached by the solvent** |
| **Rf value** | distance travelled by the substance ÷ distance travelled by the solvent front |

## Method
- Draw a pencil **baseline** near the bottom of the plate and put a small spot of the mixture on it, with spots of known substances alongside if wanted.
- Stand the plate in a little **solvent** in a covered tank. The solvent level must be **below the baseline**.
- Let the solvent rise up the plate. Remove the plate before the solvent reaches the top, and **mark the solvent front** at once.
- Dry the plate. **Locate** colourless spots with ultraviolet light, iodine vapour, or a locating agent such as ninhydrin for amino acids.

## Rf values
$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance travelled by the substance}}{\text{distance travelled by the solvent front}}$$

- Both distances are measured **from the baseline**; measure to the **centre** of the spot.
- $R_f$ has **no units** and lies **between 0 and 1**.

**Example.** A spot is 3.4 cm from the baseline and the solvent front is 8.5 cm from the baseline: $R_f$ = 3.4 ÷ 8.5 = 0.40.

### Interpreting Rf values
- A substance is **identified** by comparing its $R_f$ with those of known substances run **under the same conditions** (same stationary phase, solvent and temperature).
- The **number of spots** shows the number of components: one spot suggests a pure substance.
- Two substances with the same $R_f$ in one solvent may be separated by using a different solvent, or by two-way chromatography.

## Why substances have different Rf values
The separation depends on two competing processes.

| | Effect on the substance |
| **interaction with the stationary phase** (adsorption onto the surface of the solid) | holds it back: it travels **less far**; **smaller $R_f$** |
| **solubility in the mobile phase** | carries it along: it travels **further**; **larger $R_f$** |

Aluminium oxide and silica are **polar**. So, with a less polar solvent:

- a **polar** substance (one that can form hydrogen bonds or strong dipole attractions) is **adsorbed strongly** onto the stationary phase and is less soluble in the solvent: it moves slowly and has a **small $R_f$**
- a **non-polar** substance is adsorbed weakly and dissolves well in the solvent: it moves quickly and has a **large $R_f$**.

Changing to a more polar solvent increases the $R_f$ values of polar substances, because they dissolve better in the mobile phase.

## Uses
Checking the purity of a product; following the progress of a reaction; identifying the amino acids from a hydrolysed peptide; analysing dyes, drugs and food additives. TLC is faster and more sensitive than paper chromatography.

S: Gas–liquid chromatography separates volatile substances by carrying them in a stream of unreactive gas over a liquid coated on a solid. Each substance emerges after its own retention time, and the area of its peak shows how much of it is present.
<<<
## The parts of a GLC experiment
| Term | Meaning |
| **stationary phase** | a **high boiling point, non-polar liquid** held on a **solid support** inside a long, coiled column |
| **mobile phase** | an **unreactive gas** (the carrier gas), such as nitrogen or helium |
| **retention time** | the **time between injecting the sample and a component reaching the detector** |

## How it works
- A small sample is **injected** and vaporised.
- The **carrier gas** sweeps the vapour through the column, which is kept in an oven at a constant temperature.
- Each component is **partitioned** between the moving gas and the stationary liquid: it dissolves in the liquid, comes out again, and is carried further along.
- A component that spends more time dissolved in the stationary phase moves through the column more slowly.
- A **detector** at the end records each component as it leaves.

The result is a **gas chromatogram**: a graph of detector signal against time, with one **peak** for each component.

## Retention times
A substance is identified by comparing its retention time with those of known substances **under the same conditions** (same column, temperature and carrier gas flow rate).

### Why retention times differ
Retention time depends on the **interaction of the substance with the stationary phase**.

- A substance that is **more soluble in** (interacts more strongly with) the **non-polar stationary liquid** is held back more: a **longer retention time**.
- **Non-polar** substances interact strongly with the non-polar liquid, through instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces. Larger molecules, with more electrons, interact more strongly.
- A substance with a **higher boiling point** (lower volatility) spends less time in the gas phase, so it also has a longer retention time.
- **Polar** substances interact less with the non-polar liquid and tend to leave the column sooner than non-polar substances of similar boiling point.

For a homologous series such as the alkanes, retention time increases with chain length.

## Percentage composition
The **area under each peak is proportional to the amount** of that component in the mixture.

$$\%\ \text{of component} = \frac{\text{area of its peak}}{\text{total area of all the peaks}} \times 100$$

For a peak that is a triangle: area = ½ × base × height. If the peaks all have the same width, their heights can be compared instead.

**Example.** A chromatogram has three peaks, with areas 12, 30 and 18 units.

- total = 60
- composition: 20%, 50% and 30%

## Uses and limits
- Analysing mixtures of volatile liquids and gases: fuels, perfumes, pollutants in air, alcohol in blood, and drugs in urine.
- It needs only a tiny sample, and it is fast and sensitive.
- Limits: the substances must be volatile and stable at the oven temperature; and two different substances can have the same retention time. Linking the column to a mass spectrometer (GC–MS) identifies each component with certainty.

S: A carbon-13 NMR spectrum has one peak for every different carbon environment in a molecule. Counting the peaks tells you how symmetrical the molecule is, and the chemical shift of each peak tells you what kind of carbon it is.
<<<
## What the spectrum shows
- **Each peak corresponds to one carbon environment.**
- **The number of peaks is the number of different carbon environments.**
- The **chemical shift, δ** (in ppm), shows the **type** of carbon atom: what it is bonded to.
- Peaks are single lines: there is no splitting to interpret.
- The heights of the peaks are **not** a reliable guide to the number of carbon atoms.

Carbon atoms are in the **same environment** if they are bonded to exactly the same atoms and groups: they are **equivalent**, usually by symmetry.

## Chemical shifts
Use the Data Booklet for the exact ranges.

| Type of carbon | Example | δ / ppm (approximate) |
| alkyl carbon bonded only to C and H | CH₃–, –CH₂– | 0–50 |
| carbon next to C=O | –**C**–C=O | 20–50 |
| carbon bonded to a halogen | **C**–Cl, **C**–Br | 30–65 |
| carbon bonded to oxygen (alcohol, ester, ether) | **C**–O | 50–90 |
| alkene carbon | **C**=**C** | 110–160 |
| aromatic carbon | benzene ring | 110–160 |
| nitrile carbon | –**C**≡N | 100–125 |
| carboxylic acid, ester or amide carbon | –**C**OOH, –**C**OOR, –**C**ONH₂ | 160–185 |
| aldehyde or ketone carbon | –**C**HO, >**C**=O | 190–220 |

An electronegative atom draws electron density away from the carbon, which moves its peak to a higher δ. Tetramethylsilane, TMS, is the standard, at δ = 0.

## Counting carbon environments
Look for symmetry. Carbons that are mirror images of each other in the molecule are equivalent.

| Compound | Structure | Number of peaks | Reason |
| ethanol | CH₃CH₂OH | **2** | CH₃ and CH₂ are different |
| propan-1-ol | CH₃CH₂CH₂OH | **3** | all three carbons differ |
| propan-2-ol | CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ | **2** | the two CH₃ groups are equivalent |
| propanone | CH₃COCH₃ | **2** | two equivalent CH₃; one C=O |
| propanal | CH₃CH₂CHO | **3** | |
| butane | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ | **2** | symmetrical about the middle |
| 2-methylpropane | (CH₃)₃CH | **2** | three equivalent CH₃; one CH |
| ethyl ethanoate | CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ | **4** | all four differ |
| benzene | C₆H₆ | **1** | all six carbons are equivalent |
| methylbenzene | C₆H₅CH₃ | **5** | CH₃; C1; C2 and C6; C3 and C5; C4 |
| 1,4-dimethylbenzene | CH₃C₆H₄CH₃ | **3** | very symmetrical |

## Using a spectrum to choose between structures
**Example.** A compound C₃H₈O gives a spectrum with **two** peaks, at δ 25 and δ 64.

- Propan-1-ol would give three peaks. Propan-2-ol gives two.
- The peak at δ 64 is a carbon bonded to oxygen; the peak at δ 25 is the two equivalent CH₃ carbons.
- The compound is **propan-2-ol**.

**Example.** A compound C₃H₆O gives three peaks, one at δ 203. A peak at 190–220 is an aldehyde or ketone C=O. Propanone would give only two peaks, so the compound is **propanal**.

### Method
- **Count the peaks:** the number of carbon environments. Compare with the number of carbon atoms in the formula: fewer peaks means symmetry.
- **Read the shifts:** look for C=O above 160, C=C or a benzene ring at 110–160, C–O at 50–90.
- **Draw the possible isomers** and count the environments in each.
- **Choose** the structure that fits.

S: A proton NMR spectrum gives four pieces of information: the number of peaks is the number of hydrogen environments, the chemical shift identifies each type, the peak area counts the hydrogens, and the splitting pattern shows how many hydrogens are on the neighbouring carbon.

## The four kinds of information
| Feature of the spectrum | What it tells you |
| **number of peaks** | the number of different **proton environments** |
| **chemical shift, δ** | the **type of proton** (what it is near) |
| **relative peak areas** (integration) | the **relative number of protons** in each environment |
| **splitting pattern** | the number of protons on the **adjacent carbon** atom(s) |

## Chemical shifts
Use the Data Booklet for the exact ranges.

| Type of proton | Example | δ / ppm (approximate) |
| alkane | R–C**H**₃, R–C**H**₂–R | 0.9–1.7 |
| next to C=O | C**H**₃–C=O | 2.2–3.0 |
| next to a benzene ring | Ar–C**H**₃ | 2.3–3.0 |
| next to N | C**H**–N | 2.3–3.0 |
| next to O or a halogen | C**H**–O, C**H**–Cl | 3.2–4.0 |
| alkene | =C**H** | 4.5–6.0 |
| aromatic | benzene ring **H** | 6.0–9.0 |
| aldehyde | –C**H**O | 9.3–10.5 |
| alcohol | –O**H** | 0.5–6.0 |
| phenol | Ar–O**H** | 4.5–7.0 |
| carboxylic acid | –COO**H** | 9.0–13.0 |
| amine | –N**H**₂ | 1.0–5.0 |
| amide | –CON**H**– | 5.0–12.0 |

## Peak areas
The **area under each peak is proportional to the number of protons** causing it. The spectrometer gives the areas as an integration trace or as numbers. They give a **ratio**, which is scaled to match the molecular formula.

## Splitting: the n + 1 rule
A peak is **split by the protons on the adjacent carbon atom**.

!key If there are **n equivalent protons on the adjacent carbon**, the peak is split into **n + 1** lines.

| Protons on the adjacent carbon | Splitting | Name |
| 0 | 1 line | **singlet** |
| 1 | 2 lines | **doublet** |
| 2 | 3 lines | **triplet** |
| 3 | 4 lines | **quartet** |
| several, on more than one neighbour | many lines | **multiplet** |

- **Equivalent protons do not split each other.**
- Protons on **–OH and –NH** groups usually appear as **singlets** and do **not** split the peaks of neighbouring protons.
- Splitting is by protons on the **neighbouring** carbon, not on the same carbon.

Common patterns: a **triplet (3H) with a quartet (2H)** is an **ethyl group**, CH₃CH₂–. A **doublet (6H) with a multiplet (1H)** is (CH₃)₂CH–. A **singlet (3H)** near δ 2.1 is CH₃CO–.

| 3.7 | 2 | quartet | CH₂ (next to CH₃: 3 + 1; next to O) |

### Propanone, CH₃COCH₃
One singlet at δ 2.2: all six protons are equivalent, with no protons on the adjacent carbon.

## The standard: TMS
**Tetramethylsilane, TMS, Si(CH₃)₄**, is added as the **standard for chemical shift**. Its peak is defined as **δ = 0**.

- It gives a **single, sharp peak**: its 12 protons are all equivalent.
- Its peak lies away from those of most organic compounds.
- It is **unreactive**, **non-toxic**, and **volatile**, so it is easy to remove.

## Deuterated solvents
The sample is dissolved in a **deuterated solvent** such as **CDCl₃**.

An ordinary solvent containing ¹H would give a large signal that would swamp the peaks of the sample. Deuterium (²H) does **not** give a signal in the proton NMR spectrum.

## Identifying O–H and N–H protons: D₂O
The protons of –OH and –NH groups can be identified by **proton exchange with D₂O**.

- Run the spectrum, then add a little **D₂O**, shake, and run it again.
- The –OH and –NH protons are **labile**: they exchange with deuterium.

$$\mathrm{ROH + D_2O \rightleftharpoons ROD + HOD}$$

- Deuterium gives no signal, so **the peak due to the O–H or N–H proton disappears**.

This matters because the chemical shifts of these protons vary widely.

## Method for an unknown
- **Count the peaks:** proton environments.
- **Use the areas:** how many protons in each.
- **Use the shifts:** what each group is next to.
- **Use the splitting:** how many protons are on the adjacent carbon, and so how the groups are joined.
- **Assemble** the pieces, and check against the molecular formula.

**Example.** C₄H₈O₂: singlet at δ 3.7 (3H), quartet at δ 2.3 (2H), triplet at δ 1.1 (3H).

- δ 3.7 singlet, 3H: CH₃–O, with no neighbouring protons.
- δ 2.3 quartet, 2H, with δ 1.1 triplet, 3H: an ethyl group next to C=O.
- The compound is **methyl propanoate**, CH₃CH₂COOCH₃.

## Predicting a spectrum
For a given structure: find the proton environments (use symmetry); note the number of protons in each; estimate each shift from what the protons are next to; and apply the n + 1 rule to each.

Q: [4] State the four kinds of information that a proton NMR spectrum gives about a molecule.
+ the number of peaks gives the number of proton environments :: environment
+ the chemical shift gives the type of proton :: shift
+ the peak area gives the relative number of protons :: area|integrat
+ the splitting pattern gives the number of protons on the adjacent carbon :: split|adjacent|neighbour
A: The number of peaks gives the number of proton environments. The chemical shift gives the type of proton. The area of each peak gives the relative number of protons. The splitting pattern gives the number of protons on the adjacent carbon atom.
Q: [3] Describe and explain the proton NMR spectrum of ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH, in terms of splitting.
+ the CH₃ peak is a triplet, because the adjacent carbon has two protons :: triplet
+ the CH₂ peak is a quartet, because the adjacent carbon has three protons :: quartet
+ the OH peak is a singlet :: singlet
A: The CH₃ peak is a triplet, because there are two protons on the adjacent carbon. The CH₂ peak is a quartet, because there are three protons on the adjacent carbon. The OH peak is a singlet.
Q: [3] Explain why TMS and CDCl₃ are used in proton NMR spectroscopy.
+ TMS is the standard for chemical shift (δ = 0) :: standard|reference|zero
+ it gives a single sharp peak (it is also unreactive and volatile) :: single|sharp|peak|unreactive|inert|volatile
+ CDCl₃ is the solvent: it gives no signal in the proton spectrum :: no signal|no peak|not detect|not absorb|no proton|no 1H
A: TMS is the standard for chemical shift, set at zero. It gives a single sharp peak and is unreactive. CDCl₃ is used as the solvent because it gives no signal in the proton spectrum.
Q: [2] Explain how D₂O is used to identify the peak due to an –OH proton.
+ add D₂O to the sample and run the spectrum again :: add|shake
+ the –OH peak disappears (the proton exchanges with deuterium) :: disappear|vanish|exchang|no longer
A: Add D₂O to the sample, shake, and run the spectrum again. The –OH proton exchanges with deuterium, so its peak disappears.
