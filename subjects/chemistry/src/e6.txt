## Esters from alcohols and acyl chlorides
$$\text{alcohol} + \text{acyl chloride} \rightarrow \text{ester} + \text{HCl}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH + CH_3COCl \rightarrow CH_3COOCH_2CH_3 + HCl}$$

ethanol + ethanoyl chloride → ethyl ethanoate + hydrogen chloride

- **Conditions: room temperature.** No catalyst and no heating are needed.
- The reaction is **vigorous** and exothermic. **Steamy fumes** of hydrogen chloride are given off.
- It **goes to completion**: it is not reversible.
- The mechanism is **addition–elimination**: the lone pair on the oxygen of the alcohol attacks the δ+ carbon of the –COCl group, and HCl is eliminated.

   |   ||      |   |
 H-C - C - O - C - C - H    +   H - Cl
   |           |   |
   H           H   H
!end

## Two routes to an ester
| | Alcohol + carboxylic acid | Alcohol + acyl chloride |
| Conditions | heat under reflux; **concentrated H₂SO₄ catalyst** | **room temperature**; no catalyst |
| Rate | slow | **fast** |
| Extent | **reversible**: an equilibrium mixture | **goes to completion** |
| Other product | water | hydrogen chloride (toxic, acidic fumes) |
| Yield of ester | lower | **higher** |

The acyl chloride is more reactive because its carbonyl carbon is more strongly δ+: it is bonded to two electronegative atoms, oxygen and chlorine.

The acyl chloride route is essential for **phenols**, which do not react readily with carboxylic acids.

## Making phenol
From **phenylamine**, in two steps.

- **Step 1:** react phenylamine with **HNO₂** (made in the flask from **NaNO₂ and dilute acid**) at a temperature **below 10 °C**. This gives the **diazonium salt**.
- **Step 2:** **warm** the diazonium salt **with H₂O**. Nitrogen gas is given off and phenol forms.

$$\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2 + HNO_2 + HCl \rightarrow C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^- + 2H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^- + H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_5OH + N_2 + HCl}$$

## Phenol as an acid
Phenol is a **weak acid**.

$$\mathrm{C_6H_5OH \rightleftharpoons C_6H_5O^- + H^+}$$

| Reagent | Reaction | Products |
| **NaOH(aq)** | acid–base | **sodium phenoxide** + water |
| **Na(s)** | redox | **sodium phenoxide** + **hydrogen** |

$$\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + NaOH \rightarrow C_6H_5O^-Na^+ + H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{2C_6H_5OH + 2Na \rightarrow 2C_6H_5O^-Na^+ + H_2}$$

Phenol is only slightly soluble in water, but it dissolves in aqueous sodium hydroxide, because the ionic sodium phenoxide is soluble. Phenol is **not** acidic enough to react with carbonates: there is **no effervescence** with sodium carbonate. This distinguishes it from a carboxylic acid.

### Why phenol is acidic
In the **phenoxide ion**, a **lone pair on the oxygen overlaps with the delocalised π system of the ring**. The **negative charge is delocalised** over the ring, so it is spread out. This **stabilises the phenoxide ion**, so phenol loses a proton more readily.

## Acidities of water, phenol and ethanol
$$\text{phenol} > \text{water} > \text{ethanol}$$

| Compound | p$K_a$ | Anion | Stability of the anion |
| **phenol** | 10.0 | C₆H₅O⁻ | charge **delocalised into the ring**: most stable |
| **water** | 14.0 | OH⁻ | charge stays on the oxygen |
| **ethanol** | 16 | C₂H₅O⁻ | the **electron-donating ethyl group** pushes electron density onto the oxygen, **concentrating** the charge: least stable |

The more stable the anion, the further the equilibrium lies to the right, and the stronger the acid.

Evidence: all three react with sodium; only phenol reacts with sodium hydroxide.

## Reactions of the ring
### Bromination
**Reagent: Br₂(aq), at room temperature.** No catalyst.

$$\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + 3Br_2 \rightarrow C_6H_2Br_3OH + 3HBr}$$

- The orange bromine water is **decolourised**.
- A **white precipitate** of **2,4,6-tribromophenol** forms.

This is a test for phenol.

### Nitration
**Reagent: dilute HNO₃(aq), at room temperature.**

$$\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + HNO_3 \rightarrow O_2NC_6H_4OH + H_2O}$$

A mixture of **2-nitrophenol and 4-nitrophenol** forms.

### Why phenol reacts more easily than benzene
| | Benzene | Phenol |
| Bromination | liquid Br₂ and an AlBr₃ catalyst; one bromine enters | **aqueous** Br₂, **no catalyst**; **three** bromines enter |
| Nitration | **concentrated** HNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄, 25–60 °C | **dilute** HNO₃, room temperature |

**Explanation:** a **lone pair on the oxygen atom is delocalised into the ring**. This **increases the electron density of the π system**, so the ring is **activated**: it attracts electrophiles more strongly and can polarise a bromine molecule without a halogen carrier.

### Directing effect
The **–OH group directs incoming electrophiles to the 2-, 4- and 6-positions**. That is why bromine gives the 2,4,6-tribromo product and nitration gives the 2- and 4-nitro products.

## Coupling with diazonium salts
Phenol, **dissolved in NaOH(aq)**, reacts with a **diazonium salt** (kept cold) to form an **azo compound**: a brightly coloured (orange or yellow) solid, used as a dye.

$$\mathrm{C_6H_5N_2^+ + C_6H_5O^- \rightarrow C_6H_5{-}N{=}N{-}C_6H_4O^-}$$

The alkali converts phenol to the phenoxide ion, whose ring is even more electron-rich. The diazonium ion acts as an electrophile and attacks the 4-position.

## Other phenolic compounds
Any compound with an –OH group bonded directly to an aromatic ring behaves like phenol: it dissolves in NaOH(aq), reacts with sodium, decolourises bromine water, and couples with diazonium salts. **Naphthol** (an –OH group on a naphthalene ring system) couples with benzenediazonium chloride to give a red azo dye.

## Esters of phenol
Phenol does not react readily with carboxylic acids. It forms esters with **acyl chlorides**: phenol + benzoyl chloride → phenyl benzoate + HCl.

## Chlorine-substituted acids
| Acid | Formula | p$K_a$ |
| ethanoic | CH₃COOH | 4.76 |
| chloroethanoic | CH₂ClCOOH | 2.86 |
| dichloroethanoic | CHCl₂COOH | 1.29 |
| trichloroethanoic | CCl₃COOH | 0.65 |

**The more chlorine atoms, the stronger the acid.**

**Explanation:** chlorine is **electronegative**, so it is **electron-withdrawing** (a negative inductive effect). It pulls electron density away from the –COO⁻ group:

- this **spreads out the negative charge** on the carboxylate ion, **stabilising** it
- it also **weakens the O–H bond** in the acid.

So the acid dissociates more. More chlorine atoms withdraw more electron density. A chlorine atom **closer to the –COOH group** has a greater effect: 2-chloropropanoic acid is stronger than 3-chloropropanoic acid.

The opposite effect: **alkyl groups are electron-donating**, so they destabilise the anion. Methanoic acid (p$K_a$ 3.75) is stronger than ethanoic acid (4.76).

## Why phenols need acyl chlorides
Phenols **do not react readily with carboxylic acids**. The lone pair on the oxygen of phenol is partly delocalised into the ring, so phenol is a weaker nucleophile than an alcohol. The more reactive acyl chloride, with its strongly δ+ carbonyl carbon, is needed.

## Making acyl chlorides
From a **carboxylic acid** with **PCl₃ and heat**, **PCl₅**, or **SOCl₂**.

$$\mathrm{CH_3COOH + SOCl_2 \rightarrow CH_3COCl + SO_2 + HCl}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_3COOH + PCl_5 \rightarrow CH_3COCl + POCl_3 + HCl}$$

$$\mathrm{3CH_3COOH + PCl_3 \rightarrow 3CH_3COCl + H_3PO_3}$$

## Reactions
All happen at **room temperature**, and all give **HCl** (seen as steamy fumes).

| Reagent | Organic product | Equation for ethanoyl chloride |
| **water** (hydrolysis) | **carboxylic acid** | $\mathrm{CH_3COCl + H_2O \rightarrow CH_3COOH + HCl}$ |
| an **alcohol** | **ester** | $\mathrm{CH_3COCl + C_2H_5OH \rightarrow CH_3COOC_2H_5 + HCl}$ |
| a **phenol** | **ester** | $\mathrm{CH_3COCl + C_6H_5OH \rightarrow CH_3COOC_6H_5 + HCl}$ |
| **ammonia** | **amide** | $\mathrm{CH_3COCl + NH_3 \rightarrow CH_3CONH_2 + HCl}$ |
| a **primary amine** | **N-substituted amide** | $\mathrm{CH_3COCl + CH_3NH_2 \rightarrow CH_3CONHCH_3 + HCl}$ |
| a **secondary amine** | N,N-disubstituted amide | $\mathrm{CH_3COCl + (CH_3)_2NH \rightarrow CH_3CON(CH_3)_2 + HCl}$ |

- The hydrolysis is **violent** with cold water.
- With **excess** ammonia or amine, the HCl is neutralised: white smoke of ammonium chloride forms.
- The amide products are ethanamide, N-methylethanamide and N,N-dimethylethanamide.

$$\mathrm{CH_3COCl + 2NH_3 \rightarrow CH_3CONH_2 + NH_4Cl}$$

All these are **condensation** reactions: two molecules join with the loss of a small molecule, HCl.

## The addition–elimination mechanism
The carbonyl carbon is strongly **δ+**, because it is bonded to two electronegative atoms, **oxygen and chlorine**.

**Addition**

- A **lone pair** on the nucleophile (the O of water, an alcohol or a phenol; the N of ammonia or an amine) attacks the **δ+ carbon**.
- The **π bond of the C=O breaks**, and its electron pair moves onto the oxygen, which becomes **O⁻**. The intermediate has four groups on the carbon.

**Elimination**

- A lone pair on the O⁻ **re-forms the C=O double bond**.
- The **C–Cl bond breaks**, and a **chloride ion** leaves.
- A **proton is lost** from the atom that attacked (it had become positively charged), giving **HCl**.

!mol Addition-elimination: ethanoyl chloride + water
      O d-            O-                O
      ||              |                 ||
H3C - C d+  ->  H3C - C - Cl  ->  H3C - C
      |               |                 |
      Cl           H - O+ - H           O - H
H2O: attacks C    intermediate      + HCl
 arrows: O lone pair to C; C=O pi bond to O;
 then O- lone pair back; C-Cl bond to Cl
!end

The same steps apply with an alcohol, a phenol, ammonia or an amine: only the nucleophile changes.

## Ease of hydrolysis
$$\text{acyl chlorides} > \text{alkyl chlorides} > \text{halogenoarenes (aryl chlorides)}$$

| Compound | Conditions for hydrolysis | Explanation |
| **acyl chloride**, CH₃COCl | **cold water**: rapid | the carbon is bonded to **two electronegative atoms (O and Cl)**, so it is **strongly δ+** and readily attacked by nucleophiles |
| **alkyl chloride**, CH₃CH₂Cl | **heat with NaOH(aq)**: slow | the carbon is bonded to only **one** electronegative atom: less δ+ |
| **aryl chloride**, C₆H₅Cl | **no hydrolysis** under normal conditions | a **lone pair on Cl is delocalised into the ring**, giving the C–Cl bond partial double-bond character: it is **stronger**; the ring also repels nucleophiles |

A quick test: add each to aqueous silver nitrate. The acyl chloride gives a white precipitate of AgCl at once; the chloroalkane only slowly on warming; chlorobenzene gives none.
