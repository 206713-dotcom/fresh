## Electrophilic substitution
!def Electrophilic substitution | A reaction in which an electrophile attacks an electron-rich benzene ring and replaces a hydrogen atom.

- The benzene ring has a region of **high electron density**: its delocalised π electrons, above and below the ring. This attracts **electrophiles**, which are electron-pair acceptors.
- The electrophile is usually a **positive ion**, made in the reaction mixture: NO₂⁺, Br⁺, CH₃⁺, CH₃CO⁺.
- A hydrogen atom on the ring is **replaced**: the delocalised ring is restored at the end.

$$\mathrm{C_6H_6 + NO_2^+ \rightarrow C_6H_5NO_2 + H^+}$$

Compare alkenes, which also attract electrophiles but undergo **addition**. Benzene substitutes because that keeps its stable delocalised ring.

## Addition–elimination
!def Addition–elimination | A reaction in which a nucleophile adds to a C=O carbon, and a small molecule is then eliminated.

- The carbon of the –COCl group is strongly **δ+**: both the oxygen and the chlorine withdraw electrons from it.
- **Addition:** a nucleophile (H₂O, an alcohol, NH₃, an amine) uses its lone pair to bond to this carbon; the π bond of the C=O breaks.
- **Elimination:** the C=O bond re-forms, and a **chloride ion** leaves. A proton is also lost, so overall **HCl** is eliminated.

$$\mathrm{CH_3COCl + H_2O \rightarrow CH_3COOH + HCl}$$

The overall result is substitution of –Cl by the nucleophile, but it happens in two stages, so it is also called a **condensation** when two molecules join with loss of HCl.

## The structure of benzene
- Benzene, C₆H₆, is a **planar**, regular **hexagon**.
- Each carbon atom is **sp² hybridised**.
- Each carbon forms **three σ bonds**: two to the neighbouring carbon atoms and one to a hydrogen atom.
- The three σ bonds lie in one plane at **120°**: trigonal planar around each carbon.
- Each carbon has **one p orbital** left, containing **one electron**, at right angles to the plane of the ring.
- Each p orbital **overlaps sideways with the p orbitals on both sides**.
- This forms a **delocalised π system**: a ring of electron density **above and below** the plane of the carbon atoms, containing **six delocalised electrons**.

!mol Benzene: sigma framework and delocalised ring
        H
        |
        C
     /     \
 H - C       C - H
     |  ( )  |         ( ) = delocalised
 H - C       C - H         pi electrons
     \     /
        C
        |
        H
!end

## Evidence for delocalisation
| Evidence | Explanation |
| all six **carbon–carbon bonds are the same length**, 0.139 nm | between a C–C single bond (0.154 nm) and a C=C double bond (0.134 nm): there are no alternating single and double bonds |
| benzene is **more stable** than expected | the enthalpy change of hydrogenation of benzene (−208 kJ mol⁻¹) is much less exothermic than three times that of cyclohexene (3 × −120 = −360 kJ mol⁻¹) |
| benzene **does not decolourise aqueous bromine** | it does not undergo the addition reactions of alkenes |

The extra stability, about 150 kJ mol⁻¹, comes from the delocalisation of the π electrons.

## Consequences
- The delocalised electrons are spread over six carbon atoms, so the **electron density between any two carbons is lower** than in the C=C bond of an alkene. Benzene cannot polarise a bromine molecule enough to react without a catalyst.
- Benzene reacts by **substitution**, which keeps the delocalised ring, and not by addition, which would destroy it.
- The ring is drawn as a hexagon with a circle to show the delocalised system.

## Other aromatic molecules
In methylbenzene, phenol and phenylamine, the six ring carbons are still sp² hybridised and planar with a delocalised π system. The carbon of a methyl side chain is sp³ and tetrahedral. In phenol and phenylamine, a lone pair on the oxygen or nitrogen atom overlaps with the ring's π system, which increases the electron density of the ring.

## Enantiomers
A molecule with a **chiral centre** (a carbon atom bonded to four different groups) exists as two **enantiomers**: non-superimposable mirror images.

Enantiomers have **identical physical and chemical properties**, with two exceptions:

- their ability to **rotate plane-polarised light**
- their potential **biological activity**.

They have the same melting point, boiling point, solubility and density, and react in the same way with reagents that are not chiral.

## Optical activity
**Plane-polarised light** vibrates in one plane only.

!def Optically active | Able to rotate the plane of plane-polarised light.

- One enantiomer rotates the plane of polarised light **clockwise**.
- The other rotates it **anticlockwise**, by the **same angle**.

!def Racemic mixture | A mixture containing equal amounts of the two enantiomers.

A racemic mixture is **not optically active**: the two rotations **cancel**.

### Why reactions often give racemic mixtures
When a chiral centre is made in a reaction from non-chiral starting materials, both enantiomers form in equal amounts. For example, CN⁻ can attack the **planar** C=O group of ethanal from **either side** with equal probability, so 2-hydroxypropanenitrile is formed as a racemic mixture. An SN1 reaction goes through a planar carbocation and gives the same result.

## Chirality and drugs
Enzymes and receptors in the body are themselves chiral: they are built from single enantiomers of amino acids. A drug molecule has to **fit the shape** of a receptor, like a hand in a glove. So the two enantiomers of a chiral drug can have **different biological activity**.

| Possibility | Consequence |
| one enantiomer is active; the other is inactive | half of a racemic dose is wasted |
| one enantiomer is active; the other is **harmful** | serious side effects. Thalidomide: one enantiomer relieved morning sickness; the other caused birth defects |
| the two have different useful effects | each must be used separately |

### Making a single enantiomer
| Method | Notes |
| **separate the racemic mixture** (resolution) | difficult and expensive, because the enantiomers have identical physical properties; the unwanted half is waste |
| use a **chiral catalyst** | the catalyst, often an enzyme or a chiral transition metal complex, produces **only one** enantiomer |
| start from a naturally occurring single enantiomer | uses the "chiral pool" |

### Benefits of a single-enantiomer drug
- **smaller doses**: all of the drug is active
- **fewer side effects**
- higher activity for the same mass.

### Benefits of chiral catalysts
- only the wanted isomer is made, so there is **no need to separate** a racemic mixture
- **less waste** and a higher useful yield
- enzymes work at low temperatures and pressures, saving energy.

## Reactions of arenes
| Reaction | Reagents and conditions | Product from benzene |
| **halogenation** | **Cl₂ or Br₂** with **AlCl₃ or AlBr₃** catalyst | chlorobenzene or bromobenzene (+ HCl or HBr) |
| **nitration** | **concentrated HNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄**, at **25–60 °C** | nitrobenzene (+ H₂O) |
| **Friedel–Crafts alkylation** | **CH₃Cl and AlCl₃**, heat | methylbenzene (+ HCl) |
| **Friedel–Crafts acylation** | **CH₃COCl and AlCl₃**, heat | phenylethanone, C₆H₅COCH₃ (+ HCl) |
| **hydrogenation** | **H₂** with **Pt or Ni** catalyst, heat | cyclohexane |

$$\mathrm{C_6H_6 + Br_2 \rightarrow C_6H_5Br + HBr}$$

$$\mathrm{C_6H_6 + HNO_3 \rightarrow C_6H_5NO_2 + H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{C_6H_6 + CH_3Cl \rightarrow C_6H_5CH_3 + HCl}$$

$$\mathrm{C_6H_6 + CH_3COCl \rightarrow C_6H_5COCH_3 + HCl}$$

$$\mathrm{C_6H_6 + 3H_2 \rightarrow C_6H_{12}}$$

The first four are **electrophilic substitutions**. Hydrogenation is an addition (a reduction) of the ring.

Above 60 °C, nitration gives more dinitrobenzene. The Friedel–Crafts reactions are important because they form **new C–C bonds** to the ring.

### Oxidation of a side chain
An alkyl side chain on a benzene ring is **completely oxidised** to **–COOH**.

**Reagents and conditions:** **hot alkaline KMnO₄**, then **dilute acid**.

$$\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_3 + 3[O] \rightarrow C_6H_5COOH + H_2O}$$

Methylbenzene gives **benzoic acid**. Any alkyl side chain, whatever its length, gives benzoic acid: ethylbenzene gives benzoic acid too. The ring itself is not oxidised. The purple manganate(VII) is reduced (to brown MnO₂); the dilute acid converts the benzoate ion into benzoic acid.

## The mechanism of electrophilic substitution
### Step 1: making the electrophile
| Reaction | Formation of the electrophile | Electrophile |
| nitration | $\mathrm{HNO_3 + 2H_2SO_4 \rightarrow NO_2^+ + 2HSO_4^- + H_3O^+}$ | **NO₂⁺**, the nitronium ion |
| bromination | $\mathrm{Br_2 + AlBr_3 \rightarrow Br^+ + AlBr_4^-}$ | **Br⁺** |
| alkylation | $\mathrm{CH_3Cl + AlCl_3 \rightarrow CH_3^+ + AlCl_4^-}$ | CH₃⁺ |
| acylation | $\mathrm{CH_3COCl + AlCl_3 \rightarrow CH_3CO^+ + AlCl_4^-}$ | CH₃CO⁺ |

AlCl₃ and AlBr₃ are **halogen carriers**: the aluminium atom is electron-deficient and accepts a lone pair from a halogen atom.

### Step 2: attack on the ring
A **pair of electrons from the delocalised π system** forms a bond to the electrophile. This gives an **intermediate with a positive charge**. The carbon that is attacked is now sp³, so the delocalisation is **partly broken**: the remaining four π electrons are spread over five carbon atoms. In a diagram, the intermediate is drawn with a broken ring (a horseshoe) and a + sign inside it, with both the H and the electrophile on the attacked carbon.

### Step 3: loss of a proton
The **C–H bond breaks**, and its pair of electrons **returns to the ring**, **restoring the delocalised π system**. H⁺ is lost.

### Regenerating the catalyst
$$\mathrm{H^+ + AlBr_4^- \rightarrow HBr + AlBr_3} \qquad \mathrm{H^+ + HSO_4^- \rightarrow H_2SO_4}$$

!mol Nitration of benzene: the three stages

## Why substitution and not addition
- The **delocalised π system makes benzene unusually stable**.
- **Addition** would permanently **destroy the delocalisation**, so the product would be much less stable: a lot of energy would be needed.
- **Substitution restores the delocalised ring**, so the stability is kept.

## Halogenating methylbenzene: ring or side chain?
The **conditions** decide.

| Conditions | Where the halogen goes | Mechanism | Product with Cl₂ |
| **AlCl₃ catalyst**, room temperature, no UV light | the **ring** (positions 2 and 4) | electrophilic substitution | 2-chloromethylbenzene and 4-chloromethylbenzene |
| **ultraviolet light** (or boiling), no catalyst | the **side chain** | free-radical substitution | (chloromethyl)benzene, C₆H₅CH₂Cl |

## Directing effects
A group already on the ring decides where a second substituent goes.

| Group already present | Directs to positions | Effect on the ring |
| **–NH₂, –OH, –R** (alkyl) | **2, 4 (and 6)** | **electron-donating**: activate the ring, so it reacts faster than benzene |
| **–NO₂, –COOH, –COR** | **3 (and 5)** | **electron-withdrawing**: deactivate the ring, so it reacts more slowly |

| Starting compound | Reaction | Main products |
| methylbenzene | nitration | 2-nitromethylbenzene and 4-nitromethylbenzene |
| nitrobenzene | chlorination with AlCl₃ | 3-chloronitrobenzene |
| benzoic acid | nitration | 3-nitrobenzoic acid |
| phenol | bromination | 2,4,6-tribromophenol |

In a synthesis, the **order of the steps** matters. To make 3-nitrobenzoic acid from methylbenzene, oxidise the methyl group first (to the 3-directing –COOH), then nitrate. Nitrating first would put the nitro group at positions 2 and 4.

## Making halogenoarenes
An arene reacts with **Cl₂ or Br₂** in the presence of an **AlCl₃ or AlBr₃ catalyst**, by **electrophilic substitution**.

$$\mathrm{C_6H_6 + Cl_2 \rightarrow C_6H_5Cl + HCl}$$

## Chloroethane compared with chlorobenzene
| | Chloroethane, CH₃CH₂Cl | Chlorobenzene, C₆H₅Cl |
| with NaOH(aq), heat | **hydrolysed** to ethanol | **no reaction** |
| with NH₃ in ethanol | forms ethylamine | no reaction |
| with KCN in ethanol | forms propanenitrile | no reaction |
| with aqueous AgNO₃ in ethanol | white precipitate forms slowly | no precipitate |

**Chlorobenzene is much less reactive towards nucleophilic substitution.** It reacts only under extreme conditions.

### Explanation
- A **lone pair** of electrons on the chlorine atom, in a p orbital, **overlaps with the delocalised π system** of the benzene ring.
- The lone pair is **partly delocalised into the ring**, which gives the **C–Cl bond some double-bond character**.
- The C–Cl bond is therefore **stronger** (and shorter) than in chloroethane, and **harder to break**.
- In addition, the **electron-rich ring repels nucleophiles**, and the ring blocks attack from the side opposite the chlorine.

In chloroethane there is no π system for the lone pair to interact with: the C–Cl bond is an ordinary polar single bond, and the δ+ carbon is open to attack.

## Order of ease of hydrolysis
$$\text{acyl chloride} > \text{halogenoalkane} > \text{halogenoarene}$$

Ethanoyl chloride reacts violently with cold water; chloroethane needs hot aqueous alkali; chlorobenzene does not react.

A halogenoarene still has a benzene ring, so it can undergo further **electrophilic substitution** (nitration, for example). Halogens direct to the 2- and 4-positions.
