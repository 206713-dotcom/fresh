## Homologous series
!def Homologous series | A family of similar compounds with similar chemical properties due to the presence of the same functional group.

!def Functional group | An atom or group of atoms that determines the chemical properties of a homologous series.

### The five characteristics of a homologous series
The members of a homologous series:

- have the **same functional group**
- have the **same general formula**
- **differ from one member to the next by a –CH₂– unit**
- display a **trend in physical properties**: boiling point, for example, rises as the chain gets longer
- **share similar chemical properties**.

## Saturated and unsaturated
!def Saturated compound | A compound whose molecules have all carbon–carbon bonds as single bonds.

!def Unsaturated compound | A compound whose molecules have one or more carbon–carbon bonds that are not single bonds.

Alkanes are saturated. Alkenes, with a C=C double bond, are unsaturated.

## Fossil fuels
The fossil fuels are **coal**, **natural gas** and **petroleum** (crude oil).

- **Methane**, CH₄, is the main constituent of **natural gas**.
- **Petroleum** is a **mixture of hydrocarbons**.

!def Hydrocarbon | A compound that contains hydrogen and carbon only.

## Fractional distillation of petroleum
Petroleum is separated into useful **fractions**. A fraction is a mixture of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points and similar chain lengths.

- The petroleum is **heated** until most of it has **vaporised**.
- The vapour enters a **fractionating column**, which is **hot at the bottom and cooler at the top**.
- The vapours **rise** up the column and cool.
- Each hydrocarbon **condenses** at the level where the temperature is just below its **boiling point**, and is run off as a liquid.
- The smallest molecules have the lowest boiling points. They reach the top without condensing and leave as gases.
- The largest molecules do not vaporise at all and are collected at the bottom.

Why it works: larger molecules have stronger forces of attraction between them, so more energy is needed to separate them and their boiling points are higher.

## Trends up the column
From the **bottom to the top** of the fractionating column, the fractions have:

| Property | Trend from bottom to top |
| **chain length** | **decreasing** |
| **volatility** | **higher** (they evaporate more easily) |
| **boiling point** | **lower** |
| **viscosity** | **lower** (they are runnier) |

Fractions from the top also burn more easily and with a cleaner flame.

## The fractions and their uses
From the top of the column to the bottom:

| Fraction | Use |
| **refinery gas** | gas used in **heating and cooking** |
| **gasoline / petrol** | **fuel in cars** |
| **naphtha** | **chemical feedstock**: the starting material for making other chemicals |
| **kerosene / paraffin** | **jet fuel** |
| **diesel oil / gas oil** | fuel in **diesel engines** |
| **fuel oil** | fuel in **ships** and **home heating systems** |
| **lubricating oil** | **lubricants, waxes and polishes** |
| **bitumen** | **making roads** |

## Burning hydrocarbons

With too little air, combustion is incomplete, and carbon monoxide and carbon (soot) are formed.

## Properties
Alkanes are **generally unreactive**. The C–C and C–H bonds are strong and hard to break. They have only two kinds of reaction: **combustion** and **substitution by chlorine**.

### Combustion
Alkanes burn in air, releasing a lot of energy, which is why they are used as fuels.

**Complete combustion**, in plenty of oxygen, gives carbon dioxide and water.

$$\mathrm{CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{C_3H_8 + 5O_2 \rightarrow 3CO_2 + 4H_2O}$$

To balance: carbons first, then hydrogens, then count the oxygens needed.

**Incomplete combustion**, in a limited supply of oxygen, gives carbon monoxide, carbon (soot) and water.

### Substitution with chlorine
!def Substitution reaction | A reaction in which one atom or group of atoms is replaced by another atom or group of atoms.

In **ultraviolet light**, a chlorine atom replaces a hydrogen atom in the alkane.

$$\mathrm{CH_4 + Cl_2 \rightarrow CH_3Cl + HCl}$$

methane + chlorine → chloromethane + hydrogen chloride

!key This is a **photochemical reaction**: the **ultraviolet light provides the activation energy**. In the dark, nothing happens.

!mol Chloromethane: one H has been replaced by Cl
    H
    |
H - C - Cl
    |
    H
!end

With ethane:

$$\mathrm{C_2H_6 + Cl_2 \rightarrow C_2H_5Cl + HCl}$$

The product is chloroethane, CH₃CH₂Cl. Notice that there are **two products**: the chloroalkane and hydrogen chloride.

For propane, the chlorine can replace a hydrogen on an end carbon or on the middle carbon, giving two possible products: 1-chloropropane, CH₃CH₂CH₂Cl, and 2-chloropropane, CH₃CHClCH₃.

## Making alkenes: cracking
!def Cracking | The breaking down of larger alkane molecules into smaller alkane molecules, alkenes and hydrogen.

**Conditions:** a **high temperature** and a **catalyst**.

$$\mathrm{C_{10}H_{22} \rightarrow C_8H_{18} + C_2H_4}$$

decane → octane + ethene

$$\mathrm{C_2H_6 \rightarrow C_2H_4 + H_2}$$

ethane → ethene + hydrogen

Cracking is a thermal decomposition. The atoms must balance: count the carbons and hydrogens on each side.

### Why cracking is done
- Fractional distillation gives **more of the long-chain fractions than are needed**, and **not enough of the short-chain fractions**, such as gasoline, which are in **high demand**. Cracking converts the surplus into what is wanted.
- **Shorter-chain alkanes are more useful as fuels**: they are more volatile and burn more easily.
- Cracking produces **alkenes**, which are used to make **polymers** (plastics) and ethanol.
- Cracking produces **hydrogen**, which is used to make ammonia and as a fuel.

## The test for unsaturation
Add **aqueous bromine** (bromine water) and shake.

| Compound | Result |
| an **alkene** (unsaturated) | the aqueous bromine changes from **orange to colourless** |
| an **alkane** (saturated) | **no change**: it stays orange |

## Addition reactions
!def Addition reaction | A reaction in which only one product is formed.

The double bond opens, and one atom or group joins on to each of the two carbon atoms. The product is saturated.

### With bromine
$$\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 + Br_2 \rightarrow CH_2BrCH_2Br}$$

ethene + bromine → 1,2-dibromoethane

!mol Bromine adds across the double bond

### With hydrogen
**Conditions:** a **nickel catalyst** (and heat).

$$\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 + H_2 \rightarrow CH_3CH_3}$$

ethene + hydrogen → ethane

The alkene becomes an alkane. This reaction, hydrogenation, is used to turn vegetable oils into margarine.

### With steam
**Conditions:** an **acid catalyst** (with a high temperature and pressure).

$$\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 + H_2O \rightarrow CH_3CH_2OH}$$

ethene + steam → ethanol

The alkene becomes an alcohol. H adds to one carbon and OH to the other.

## Polymers and plastics
!def Polymer | A large molecule built up from many smaller molecules called monomers.

## Addition polymerisation
Alkenes polymerise by addition. The double bond in each monomer opens, and the monomers join end to end. **The polymer is the only product.**

**Poly(ethene)** is made by the addition polymerisation of **ethene**.

### From polymer back to monomer
Take two neighbouring carbon atoms of the chain with their groups, remove the bonds at the ends, and put a double bond between the two carbons.

## Condensation polymerisation
In condensation polymerisation, each time two monomers join, a **small molecule such as water is also formed**. The monomers need **two functional groups each**, one at each end.

A polyamide is made from a **dicarboxylic acid** and a **diamine**.

- dicarboxylic acid: HOOC–▭–COOH
- diamine: H₂N–▭–NH₂

Each –COOH reacts with an –NH₂. Water is lost, and an **amide linkage**, –CO–NH–, is formed.

!mol The structure of nylon (a polyamide). [ ] stands for the rest of each monomer
  O         O   H         H
  ||        ||  |         |
- C -[   ]- C - N -[   ]- N -
!end

### Polyesters: PET
A polyester is made from a **dicarboxylic acid** and a **diol**.

- dicarboxylic acid: HOOC–▭–COOH
- diol: HO–▭–OH

Each –COOH reacts with an –OH. Water is lost, and an **ester linkage**, –COO–, is formed.

!mol The structure of PET (a polyester)
  O         O
  ||        ||
- C -[   ]- C - O -[   ]- O -
!end
