## Characteristic properties
- **variable oxidation states**
- they behave as **catalysts**
- they form **complex ions**
- they form **coloured compounds**.

## Variable oxidation states
**Explanation:** the **3d and 4s sub-shells are similar in energy**, so different numbers of electrons can be lost, or used in bonding, for similar amounts of energy.

| Element | Common oxidation states |
| Ti | +3, +4 |
| V | +2, +3, +4, +5 |
| Cr | +3, +6 |
| Mn | +2, +4, +6, +7 |
| Fe | +2, +3 |
| Co | +2, +3 |
| Ni | +2 |
| Cu | +1, +2 |

- The maximum oxidation state rises from Ti to Mn, as all the 3d and 4s electrons can be used (Mn: +7 in MnO₄⁻).
- High oxidation states occur in **oxo-ions** such as MnO₄⁻ and Cr₂O₇²⁻, which are strong oxidising agents.
- Lower oxidation states occur as simple ions such as Fe²⁺.

## Catalysts
**Explanation:** transition elements

- have **more than one stable oxidation state**, so they can accept and then give back electrons, being used in one step and regenerated in another
- have **vacant d orbitals that are energetically accessible**, so they can **form dative bonds** with reactant molecules (ligands), holding them on the surface or in a complex while their bonds are weakened.

| Catalyst | Process |
| iron | Haber process |
| vanadium(V) oxide, V₂O₅ | Contact process |
| nickel | hydrogenation of alkenes |
| Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺ | reaction of I⁻ with S₂O₈²⁻ |

## Complex ions
**Explanation:** transition element ions are **small and highly charged**, and have **vacant d orbitals that are energetically accessible**. These orbitals can **accept lone pairs** of electrons from ligands, forming **dative (coordinate) bonds**.

## Coloured compounds
Their compounds are coloured because the d sub-shell is partly filled, so electrons can move between d orbitals of different energies by absorbing visible light. Ions with **3d⁰ or 3d¹⁰** configurations, such as Sc³⁺, Zn²⁺ and Cu⁺, are **colourless**.

Ligands can swap for one another, changing the colour, and the variable oxidation states make transition elements central to redox titrations.

## Ligands and complexes
!def Ligand | A species that contains a lone pair of electrons that forms a dative covalent bond to a central metal atom or ion.

!def Complex | A molecule or ion formed by a central metal atom or ion surrounded by one or more ligands.

!def Coordination number | The number of coordinate (dative) bonds formed to the central metal atom or ion.

| Type of ligand | Dative bonds per ligand | Examples |
| **monodentate** | one | H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻, CN⁻ |
| **bidentate** | two | 1,2-diaminoethane, H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂ (written **en**); the ethanedioate ion, C₂O₄²⁻ |
| **polydentate** | more than two | EDTA⁴⁻ (six) |

## Shapes
| Coordination number | Shape | Bond angle | Example |
| 2 | **linear** | 180° | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ |
| 4 | **tetrahedral** | 109.5° | [CuCl₄]²⁻, [CoCl₄]²⁻ |
| 4 | **square planar** | 90° | [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂], [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻ |
| 6 | **octahedral** | 90° | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺, [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺, [Ni(en)₃]²⁺ |

Small ligands (H₂O, NH₃) usually give six-coordinate complexes. The larger chloride ion gives four-coordinate, tetrahedral complexes: only four fit round the metal ion.

### Formula and charge
Charge on the complex = charge on the metal ion + the sum of the charges on the ligands.

- Cu²⁺ with four Cl⁻: 2 + 4(−1) = 2−: [CuCl₄]²⁻
- Fe³⁺ with six CN⁻: 3 + 6(−1) = 3−: [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻
- Ni²⁺ with three en: 2+: [Ni(en)₃]²⁺, coordination number 6 (each en forms two bonds)

## Reactions of copper(II) and cobalt(II)
| Reagent | Copper(II) | Cobalt(II) |
| **water** | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺: **blue** solution | [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺: **pink** solution |
| **OH⁻(aq)**, or a little NH₃(aq) | [Cu(OH)₂(H₂O)₄]: **pale blue precipitate** | [Co(OH)₂(H₂O)₄]: **blue precipitate** (turns pink, then brown, on standing) |
| **excess NH₃(aq)** | [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺: **deep blue** solution | [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺: **yellow-brown** solution (darkens in air as it is oxidised) |
| **concentrated HCl** (excess Cl⁻) | [CuCl₄]²⁻: **yellow** solution (green while mixed with the blue) | [CoCl₄]²⁻: **blue** solution |

$$\mathrm{[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+} + 2OH^- \rightarrow [Cu(OH)_2(H_2O)_4] + 2H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+} + 4NH_3 \rightleftharpoons [Cu(NH_3)_4(H_2O)_2]^{2+} + 4H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+} + 4Cl^- \rightleftharpoons [CuCl_4]^{2-} + 6H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{[Co(H_2O)_6]^{2+} + 6NH_3 \rightleftharpoons [Co(NH_3)_6]^{2+} + 6H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{[Co(H_2O)_6]^{2+} + 4Cl^- \rightleftharpoons [CoCl_4]^{2-} + 6H_2O}$$

- The reaction with hydroxide ions is an **acid–base** reaction: OH⁻ removes H⁺ from two water ligands, giving an uncharged, insoluble complex.
- The reactions with ammonia and chloride are **ligand exchange**: one ligand replaces another.

## Ligand exchange
- It is **reversible**. Adding water to yellow [CuCl₄]²⁻ turns it back to blue [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺.
- The position of equilibrium depends on the **concentrations** of the ligands and on how stable each complex is.
- With NH₃ replacing H₂O (similar size, both uncharged), the coordination number stays 6. With Cl⁻ (larger, charged), the **coordination number changes** from 6 to 4, the shape from octahedral to tetrahedral, and the charge on the complex changes.

## Redox and E⦵
Whether one transition element species will oxidise or reduce another is predicted from E⦵ values: the reaction is feasible if E⦵cell is positive.

**Example.** Will acidified MnO₄⁻ (+1.52 V) oxidise Fe²⁺ (Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ +0.77 V)? E⦵cell = +1.52 − 0.77 = +0.75 V: feasible.

## Three redox reactions for titration
### Manganate(VII) with iron(II), in acid
$$\mathrm{MnO_4^- + 8H^+ + 5Fe^{2+} \rightarrow Mn^{2+} + 5Fe^{3+} + 4H_2O}$$

- Ratio MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ = **1 : 5**.
- **Self-indicating:** the purple manganate(VII) in the burette is decolourised as it is added. The end-point is the **first permanent pale pink** colour.
- Acidify with **dilute sulfuric acid**.

### Manganate(VII) with ethanedioate, in acid
$$\mathrm{2MnO_4^- + 16H^+ + 5C_2O_4^{2-} \rightarrow 2Mn^{2+} + 10CO_2 + 8H_2O}$$

- Ratio MnO₄⁻ : C₂O₄²⁻ = **2 : 5**.
- The mixture is **warmed** to about 60 °C, because the reaction is slow at first. The Mn²⁺ formed catalyses it (autocatalysis).

### Copper(II) with iodide
$$\mathrm{2Cu^{2+} + 4I^- \rightarrow 2CuI(s) + I_2}$$

- A **white precipitate** of copper(I) iodide forms in a **brown** solution of iodine.
- The iodine is then titrated with sodium thiosulfate, adding **starch** near the end-point: **blue-black to colourless**.

$$\mathrm{I_2 + 2S_2O_3^{2-} \rightarrow 2I^- + S_4O_6^{2-}}$$

- Overall, 2Cu²⁺ ≡ I₂ ≡ 2S₂O₃²⁻, so **moles of Cu²⁺ = moles of S₂O₃²⁻**.

### A worked titration
25.0 cm³ of an Fe²⁺ solution needs 22.50 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄.

- n(MnO₄⁻) = 0.0200 × 0.02250 = 4.50 × 10⁻⁴ mol
- n(Fe²⁺) = 5 × 4.50 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.25 × 10⁻³ mol
- [Fe²⁺] = 2.25 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = **0.0900 mol dm⁻³**

Other redox systems are handled in the same way: use the given half-equations to get the mole ratio.

S: Ligands split the five d orbitals into two groups with slightly different energies. An electron can jump the gap by absorbing visible light of exactly that energy, and we see the colour that is left. Change the ligand and the gap changes, so the colour changes.
<<<
## Degenerate and non-degenerate orbitals
| Term | Meaning |
| **degenerate** | orbitals with the **same energy** |
| **non-degenerate** | orbitals with **different energies** |

In an isolated transition metal ion, the five d orbitals are degenerate. When ligands bond to the ion, the lone pairs of the ligands repel the electrons in the d orbitals. d orbitals that point **towards** the ligands are repelled more and are raised in energy more than those that point **between** them. The d orbitals **split** into two non-degenerate sets, separated by an energy gap, **ΔE**.

## The splitting patterns
| Complex | Higher energy | Lower energy |
| **octahedral** | **two** orbitals | **three** orbitals |
| **tetrahedral** | **three** orbitals | **two** orbitals |

!mol d orbital splitting
   octahedral             tetrahedral

   ---  ---               ---  ---  ---
      ^                        ^
      | dE                     | dE
      v                        v
 ---  ---  ---               ---  ---
!end

In an octahedral complex the ligands lie on the x, y and z axes, so the two orbitals with lobes along the axes (d_z² and d_x²−y²) are raised most. ΔE is smaller for a tetrahedral complex than for an octahedral one.

## Where the colour comes from
- An electron in a **lower-energy d orbital absorbs energy** from visible light and is **promoted** (excited) to a **higher-energy d orbital**.
- The light absorbed has the **frequency** that corresponds to the energy gap, **ΔE** (ΔE = hf).
- The rest of the visible light is **transmitted** (or reflected). The colour we see is the **complementary colour** of the light absorbed.

| Light absorbed | Colour seen |
| red / orange | blue / green |
| yellow | violet |
| blue | orange |
| violet | yellow |

[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ absorbs in the red–orange region, so it looks blue.

### Two requirements
- The d sub-shell must be **partly filled**: there must be an electron to promote and a space for it to go into.
- So **3d⁰** ions (Sc³⁺, Ti⁴⁺) and **3d¹⁰** ions (Zn²⁺, Cu⁺) are **colourless**.

## What changes the colour
The size of ΔE depends on:

- the **ligand**: different ligands repel the d electrons to different extents
- the **oxidation state** of the metal: Fe²⁺ is pale green; Fe³⁺ is yellow-brown
- the **coordination number and shape**: octahedral or tetrahedral.

**Different ligands give a different ΔE, so light of a different frequency is absorbed, and a different colour is observed.**

## Ligand exchange and colour
| Complex | Ligands | Colour |
| [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ | H₂O | blue |
| [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ | NH₃ and H₂O | deep blue |
| [CuCl₄]²⁻ | Cl⁻ | yellow |
| [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ | H₂O | pink |
| [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺ | NH₃ | yellow-brown |
| [CoCl₄]²⁻ | Cl⁻ | blue |

Ammonia causes a larger splitting than water, so [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ absorbs light of higher frequency than [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺, and its colour is different. For [CuCl₄]²⁻ and [CoCl₄]²⁻ both the ligand and the shape change.

## Geometrical (cis/trans) isomerism
The same ligands are bonded to the metal, but in different positions in space.

- **cis:** the two identical ligands are **next to each other** (at 90°).
- **trans:** they are **opposite each other** (at 180°).

### Square planar: [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]
!mol cis- and trans-[Pt(NH3)2Cl2]

     \    /               \    /
       Pt                   Pt
     /    \               /    \
  H3N      Cl           Cl      NH3

     cis                  trans
!end

S: The stability constant, Kstab, is the equilibrium constant for forming a complex from the aqueous metal ion and its ligands. The larger it is, the more stable the complex, so a ligand that gives a larger Kstab will displace one that gives a smaller Kstab.
<<<
## Definition
!def Stability constant, Kstab | The equilibrium constant for the formation of a complex ion in a solvent, from its constituent ions or molecules.

## Writing the expression
For the formation of the complex from the aqueous ion:

$$\mathrm{[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+} + 4NH_3 \rightleftharpoons [Cu(NH_3)_4(H_2O)_2]^{2+} + 4H_2O}$$

$$K_{stab} = \frac{[\mathrm{[Cu(NH_3)_4(H_2O)_2]^{2+}}]}{[\mathrm{[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+}}][\mathrm{NH_3}]^4}$$

- **Products over reactants**, each raised to the power of its balancing number.
- **Water is not included**: it is the solvent, in large excess, so its concentration is effectively constant.
- Units here: mol⁻⁴ dm¹².

| Formation of | $K_{stab}$ expression | Units |
| [CuCl₄]²⁻ | $\dfrac{[\mathrm{CuCl_4^{2-}}]}{[\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}][\mathrm{Cl^-}]^4}$ | mol⁻⁴ dm¹² |
| [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ | $\dfrac{[\mathrm{Ag(NH_3)_2^+}]}{[\mathrm{Ag^+}][\mathrm{NH_3}]^2}$ | mol⁻² dm⁶ |
| [Fe(SCN)(H₂O)₅]²⁺ | $\dfrac{[\mathrm{Fe(SCN)^{2+}}]}{[\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}][\mathrm{SCN^-}]}$ | mol⁻¹ dm³ |

In these expressions Cu²⁺ stands for the aqueous ion, [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺.

## What the value means
**A large $K_{stab}$ means the position of equilibrium lies well to the right: the complex is stable.**

| Complex of copper(II) | $K_{stab}$ |
| [CuCl₄]²⁻ | 4.2 × 10⁵ |
| [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ | 1.2 × 10¹³ |
| [Cu(EDTA)]²⁻ | 6.3 × 10¹⁸ |

Values are often quoted as log $K_{stab}$: 5.6, 13.1 and 18.8.

## Ligand exchange
A ligand will displace another if it forms a complex with a **larger $K_{stab}$**: the new complex is more stable.

- Adding ammonia to [CuCl₄]²⁻ gives [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺: its $K_{stab}$ is larger.
- Adding EDTA⁴⁻ to [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ gives [Cu(EDTA)]²⁻.
- The reverse changes do not happen to any great extent, unless the concentration of the other ligand is made very high (the exchange is still an equilibrium).

Polydentate and bidentate ligands usually form more stable complexes than monodentate ligands: when one EDTA⁴⁻ ion replaces six water molecules, the number of particles in solution increases, so the entropy increases.

## Calculations
**Example 1.** For [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺, $K_{stab}$ = 1.7 × 10⁷ mol⁻² dm⁶. In a solution at equilibrium, [NH₃] = 0.10 mol dm⁻³ and the concentration of the complex is 0.010 mol dm⁻³. Find [Ag⁺].

$$[\mathrm{Ag^+}] = \frac{[\mathrm{Ag(NH_3)_2^+}]}{K_{stab}[\mathrm{NH_3}]^2} = \frac{0.010}{1.7 \times 10^7 \times 0.10^2} = 5.9 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$$

Almost all the silver is in the complex.

**Example 2.** At equilibrium, [Fe³⁺] = 2.0 × 10⁻³, [SCN⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻³ and [Fe(SCN)²⁺] = 2.8 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³.

$$K_{stab} = \frac{2.8 \times 10^{-4}}{2.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 1.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 140\ \text{mol}^{-1}\,\text{dm}^3$$
