This explains the properties:

- A solid keeps its shape because its particles are held in fixed positions by strong forces of attraction.
- A liquid flows because its particles can move past each other, but it cannot be compressed because the particles are already touching.
- A gas can be compressed because there is a lot of empty space between its particles.

## Changes of state
| Change | Name | Energy |
| solid → liquid | **melting** | taken in |
| liquid → gas | **boiling** or **evaporating** | taken in |
| gas → liquid | **condensing** | given out |
| liquid → solid | **freezing** | given out |

- **Melting** happens at one fixed temperature, the melting point.
- **Boiling** happens at one fixed temperature, the boiling point. Bubbles of gas form all through the liquid.
- **Evaporating** happens at any temperature below the boiling point, and only at the surface of the liquid. It is slower than boiling.

## Explaining changes of state with kinetic particle theory
- **Melting.** As a solid is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy and vibrate faster. At the melting point they have enough energy to overcome the forces of attraction holding them in fixed positions. The regular arrangement breaks down and the particles can move around: the solid has become a liquid.
- **Boiling.** As the liquid is heated, its particles move faster. At the boiling point they have enough energy to overcome the forces of attraction between them completely. They escape and move far apart: the liquid has become a gas.
- **Evaporating.** The particles in a liquid have a range of energies. The fastest ones at the surface have enough energy to escape, even below the boiling point.
- **Condensing and freezing.** As a substance is cooled, its particles lose kinetic energy and move more slowly. The forces of attraction pull them closer together (condensing) and then into fixed positions (freezing).

## Heating and cooling curves
A heating curve shows temperature against time as a solid is heated steadily.

| Part of the curve | What is happening |
| sloping up | the solid is getting hotter: particles vibrate faster |
| **flat, at the melting point** | the solid is **melting**. The temperature stays constant |
| sloping up | the liquid is getting hotter: particles move faster |
| **flat, at the boiling point** | the liquid is **boiling**. The temperature stays constant |
| sloping up | the gas is getting hotter |

!key During a change of state the temperature does not change, because the energy supplied is used to overcome the forces of attraction between the particles, not to increase their kinetic energy.

A cooling curve is the same picture in reverse. On the flat parts, energy is given out as forces of attraction form between the particles, which keeps the temperature constant while the substance condenses or freezes.

A pure substance has sharp, flat sections. An impure substance melts and boils over a range of temperatures.

## Gases: temperature, pressure and volume
The pressure of a gas is caused by its particles hitting the walls of the container.

| Change | Effect on the volume of a gas |
| temperature **increases** (pressure constant) | volume **increases** |
| pressure **increases** (temperature constant) | volume **decreases** |

### Explaining it with particles
- **Higher temperature.** The particles gain kinetic energy and move faster. They hit the walls more often and harder. If the container can expand, such as a balloon or a syringe, the particles push the walls outwards and move further apart, so the volume increases.
- **Higher pressure.** Pushing in on a gas forces the particles closer together, into a smaller volume. There is a lot of empty space between gas particles, so this is easy.
- In a sealed container that cannot expand, heating the gas increases its pressure: the faster particles collide with the walls more frequently and with more force.

## What diffusion is
Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of **higher concentration** to a region of **lower concentration**, as a result of their **random movement**.

It happens in liquids and gases, where the particles are free to move. It does not happen in solids, where the particles are fixed in position.

## Explaining it with kinetic particle theory
- The particles of a gas or liquid move randomly all the time.
- They collide with each other and with the particles of the substance they are spreading into, and bounce off in new directions.
- Over time they spread out and mix until they are evenly distributed.

No stirring or shaking is needed. The energy comes from the particles' own kinetic energy.

### Examples
- **Bromine and air.** A gas jar of red-brown bromine vapour is placed mouth to mouth with a gas jar of air. After some time, both jars are the same pale brown colour: the bromine particles and the air particles have mixed.
- **A crystal in water.** A purple crystal of potassium manganate(VII) is placed at the bottom of a beaker of water. The crystal dissolves, and the purple colour slowly spreads through all the water.
- **Smells.** You can smell perfume across a room because its particles diffuse through the air.

## What affects the rate of diffusion
| Factor | Effect | Why |
| **state** | faster in gases than in liquids | gas particles move much faster, and there is more space between them |
| **temperature** | faster at higher temperature | particles have more kinetic energy and move faster |
| **relative molecular mass** | **lower mass means faster** | lighter particles move faster than heavier ones at the same temperature |

## Molecular mass and the rate of diffusion
!key At the same temperature, gases with a lower relative molecular mass diffuse faster.

At a given temperature all gas particles have the same average kinetic energy, so lighter molecules must be moving faster.

### The ammonia and hydrogen chloride experiment
- A long glass tube is clamped horizontally.
- Cotton wool soaked in concentrated **ammonia** solution is put in one end, and cotton wool soaked in concentrated **hydrochloric acid** in the other, at the same time. The ends are sealed.
- Ammonia gas, NH₃, and hydrogen chloride gas, HCl, diffuse along the tube towards each other.
- Where they meet they react, forming a **white ring** of solid ammonium chloride.

$$\mathrm{NH_3(g) + HCl(g) \rightarrow NH_4Cl(s)}$$

The white ring forms **nearer the hydrochloric acid end**.

| Gas | $M_r$ | Speed |
| ammonia, NH₃ | 14 + 3 = **17** | faster: travels further |
| hydrogen chloride, HCl | 1 + 35.5 = **36.5** | slower |

**Conclusion:** ammonia molecules have a lower relative molecular mass, so they diffuse faster and travel further in the same time.

The ring takes several minutes to appear, even though gas particles move at hundreds of metres per second. This is because they keep colliding with the particles of the air in the tube, which change their direction.

## Three kinds of substance
!def Element | A substance made of atoms that all have the same number of protons. It cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means.

!def Compound | A substance made of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed proportion.

!def Mixture | Two or more substances (elements or compounds) that are not chemically combined.

Examples:

- **Elements:** oxygen, O₂; iron, Fe; carbon, C.
- **Compounds:** water, H₂O; carbon dioxide, CO₂; sodium chloride, NaCl.
- **Mixtures:** air; sea water; brass; petroleum.

An element can be made of single atoms (helium, He) or of molecules (oxygen, O₂). It is still an element if every atom is the same kind.

## Compounds and mixtures compared
| | Compound | Mixture |
| How the parts are joined | **chemically combined** (bonded) | **not** chemically combined |
| Composition | **fixed** proportions: water is always H₂O | **any** proportions |
| Properties | **different** from those of its elements | the **same** as those of its parts: each keeps its own properties |
| Separation | only by a **chemical reaction** | by **physical methods**: filtration, distillation, chromatography, a magnet |
| Making it | a chemical reaction; there is usually an energy change | no chemical reaction; just mixing |
| Melting and boiling point | sharp, fixed values | melts and boils over a range |

### Iron and sulfur

- **Compound.** Heat the mixture. It glows as the elements react and forms iron(II) sulfide, FeS, a black solid. It is not magnetic, and a magnet cannot separate it. A new substance with new properties has formed.

## Inside the atom
- At the centre is the **nucleus**, which contains **protons** and **neutrons**.

!def Proton number (atomic number) | The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.

## Isotopes
!def Isotopes | Different atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

Because the number of protons is the same, isotopes have the same proton number. Because the number of neutrons differs, they have different mass numbers.

| Isotope | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
| chlorine-35 | 17 | 18 | 17 |
| chlorine-37 | 17 | 20 | 17 |

## Writing an atom or ion in symbols
$$^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}$$

- **A**, at the top, is the **mass number**.
- **Z**, at the bottom, is the **proton number**.
- **X** is the symbol of the element.
- A charge, if there is one, goes at the top right.

| Symbol | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
| $^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}$ | 6 | 12 − 6 = 6 | 6 |
| $^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}$ | 6 | 14 − 6 = 8 | 6 |
| $^{35}_{17}\mathrm{Cl^-}$ | 17 | 35 − 17 = 18 | 17 + 1 = 18 |
| $^{24}_{12}\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ | 12 | 24 − 12 = 12 | 12 − 2 = 10 |

For an ion: a negative charge means electrons have been **gained**; a positive charge means electrons have been **lost**.

## Why isotopes have the same chemical properties
!key Isotopes of the same element have the same chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons and therefore the same electronic configuration.

Chemical reactions involve only the outer-shell electrons. Neutrons take no part.

Isotopes do differ slightly in physical properties that depend on mass, such as density and rate of diffusion.

## Relative atomic mass from isotopes
Most elements are a mixture of isotopes. The relative atomic mass, $A_r$, is the **average mass** of the atoms, taking into account how much of each isotope there is.

$$A_r = \frac{\sum(\text{isotope mass} \times \text{percentage abundance})}{100}$$

### Worked example
Chlorine is 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37.

$$A_r = \frac{(35 \times 75) + (37 \times 25)}{100}$$

$$A_r = \frac{2625 + 925}{100}$$

$$A_r = \frac{3550}{100} = 35.5$$

This is why the relative atomic mass of chlorine is not a whole number.

**Check:** the answer must lie between the masses of the lightest and heaviest isotopes, and nearer the more abundant one.

An ion is an atom, or group of atoms, with an electric charge.

!def Ionic bond | A strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.

== ci-2-6

## Giant covalent structures
A giant covalent structure (a macromolecule) is a huge lattice of atoms, all joined by **strong covalent bonds**. There are no separate molecules.

To melt one, the covalent bonds themselves must be broken. That takes a great deal of energy, so the melting points are **very high**.

## Diamond
- Made only of carbon atoms.
- **Each carbon atom is covalently bonded to four other carbon atoms.**
- The atoms are arranged tetrahedrally, in a rigid three-dimensional lattice.
- All four outer electrons of each atom are used in bonds, so there are **no free electrons**.

| Property | Explanation |
| very hard | every atom is held in place by four strong covalent bonds in a rigid lattice |
| very high melting point | many strong covalent bonds must be broken, which needs a lot of energy |
| does not conduct electricity | there are no free electrons and no ions |

**Use: cutting tools**, such as drill tips and glass cutters, because diamond is so hard.

## Graphite
- Also made only of carbon atoms.
- **Each carbon atom is covalently bonded to three other carbon atoms.**
- The atoms form flat **layers** of hexagons.
- There are only **weak forces between the layers**.
- The fourth outer electron of each atom is not used in a bond. It is **delocalised** and free to move along the layers.

| Property | Explanation |
| soft and slippery | the weak forces between the layers are easily overcome, so the **layers slide over each other** |
| conducts electricity | it has **delocalised electrons that are free to move** |
| very high melting point | the covalent bonds within the layers are strong, and many must be broken |

**Uses:**

- as a **lubricant**, because the layers slide over each other
- as an **electrode**, because it conducts electricity, has a high melting point and is unreactive.

## Silicon(IV) oxide, SiO₂
Silicon(IV) oxide (silicon dioxide) is found as quartz and in sand.

- **Each silicon atom is covalently bonded to four oxygen atoms.**
- **Each oxygen atom is covalently bonded to two silicon atoms.**
- The result is a rigid three-dimensional lattice with a tetrahedral arrangement, very like diamond. The ratio of atoms is one silicon to two oxygens, which gives the formula SiO₂.

## Diamond and silicon(IV) oxide compared
They have similar structures, so they have similar properties:

- both are **very hard**
- both have **very high melting points**
- both **do not conduct electricity**, because there are no free electrons.

The reason is the same for both: a giant lattice held together by many strong covalent bonds in a rigid, tetrahedral arrangement.
