S: Every reaction takes in or gives out energy as bonds break and form. The enthalpy change, ΔH, is negative when energy is released and positive when it is absorbed. It can be measured with a calorimeter using q = mcΔT, or estimated from bond energies.

## Exothermic and endothermic
| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
| Energy | released to the surroundings | absorbed from the surroundings |
| Sign of ΔH | **negative** | **positive** |
| Temperature of surroundings | rises | falls |
| Enthalpy of products | lower than reactants | higher than reactants |
| Examples | combustion, neutralisation | thermal decomposition, photosynthesis |

## Reaction pathway diagrams
Enthalpy is on the vertical axis and the progress of the reaction on the horizontal axis.

- Draw a line for the **reactants** and a line for the **products**. Products lower: exothermic. Products higher: endothermic.
- Join them with a curve that rises to a peak.
- **Activation energy, $E_a$:** the arrow from the reactants **up to the peak**.
- **Enthalpy change, ΔH:** the arrow from the reactants level to the products level. It points down for an exothermic reaction.

## Standard conditions
Standard conditions, shown by the symbol ⦵, are **298 K** and **101 kPa**. Each substance is in its **standard state**: its normal physical state under these conditions. Solutions have a concentration of 1.00 mol dm⁻³.

## Four standard enthalpy changes
!def Standard enthalpy change of reaction, ΔHr | The enthalpy change when the amounts of reactants shown in the equation react to give products, under standard conditions.

!def Standard enthalpy change of formation, ΔHf | The enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states, under standard conditions.

!def Standard enthalpy change of combustion, ΔHc | The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is burned completely in excess oxygen, under standard conditions.

!def Standard enthalpy change of neutralisation, ΔHneut | The enthalpy change when one mole of water is formed by the reaction of an acid with an alkali, under standard conditions.

| | Equation that represents it |
| ΔHf of ethanol | $\mathrm{2C(s) + 3H_2(g) + \tfrac{1}{2}O_2(g) \rightarrow C_2H_5OH(l)}$ |
| ΔHc of ethanol | $\mathrm{C_2H_5OH(l) + 3O_2(g) \rightarrow 2CO_2(g) + 3H_2O(l)}$ |
| ΔHneut | $\mathrm{H^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) \rightarrow H_2O(l)}$ |

- The ΔHf of an **element** in its standard state is **zero**.
- Fractions are allowed in these equations, so that exactly one mole is formed or burned.
- ΔHc is always negative. ΔHneut for a strong acid and a strong alkali is about −57 kJ mol⁻¹.

## Bonds and energy
Energy is transferred in reactions because bonds are broken and made.

- **Breaking bonds absorbs energy: endothermic** (ΔH positive).
- **Making bonds releases energy: exothermic** (ΔH negative).

$$\Delta H_r = \sum(\text{bond energies of bonds broken}) - \sum(\text{bond energies of bonds formed})$$

**Example.** $\mathrm{CH_4(g) + 2O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g) + 2H_2O(g)}$

Bond energies in kJ mol⁻¹: C–H 410, O=O 496, C=O 805, O–H 460.

- broken: (4 × 410) + (2 × 496) = 2632
- formed: (2 × 805) + (4 × 460) = 3450
- ΔH = 2632 − 3450 = **−818 kJ mol⁻¹**

### Exact and average bond energies
- Some bond energies are **exact**: the bond exists in only one molecule, such as H–H in H₂ or O=O in O₂.
- Others are **averages**, taken over many different molecules. The C–H bond energy is slightly different in methane, ethane and ethanol.

Because average values are used, and because bond energies apply to **gaseous** species, a value calculated from bond energies differs a little from the experimental one.

## Calorimetry
$$q = mc\Delta T$$

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
| q | thermal energy transferred | J |
| m | mass of the **water or solution** that changes temperature | g |
| c | specific heat capacity: 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ for water and dilute solutions | J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ |
| ΔT | temperature change | K (or °C) |

$$\Delta H = \frac{-mc\Delta T}{n}$$

n is the amount, in mol, of the substance that the enthalpy change refers to. Divide by 1000 for kJ mol⁻¹. The sign is negative if the temperature **rose**.

### Combustion
0.500 g of ethanol ($M$ = 46.0) burns and heats 100 g of water by 30.0 °C.

- q = 100 × 4.18 × 30.0 = 12 540 J
- n = 0.500 ÷ 46.0 = 0.01087 mol
- ΔH = −12.54 ÷ 0.01087 = **−1150 kJ mol⁻¹**

The data-book value is −1367 kJ mol⁻¹. The experimental value is less exothermic because of **heat loss to the surroundings**, **incomplete combustion**, heating of the calorimeter itself, and non-standard conditions.

### Reactions in solution
Use a polystyrene cup with a lid. 1 cm³ of solution is taken to have a mass of 1 g, and m is the **total** volume of solution.

25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl is added to 25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. The temperature rises by 6.8 °C.

- q = 50.0 × 4.18 × 6.8 = 1421 J
- n(H₂O) = 1.00 × 0.0250 = 0.0250 mol
- ΔH = −1.421 ÷ 0.0250 = **−56.8 kJ mol⁻¹**

For a slow reaction, plot temperature against time and extrapolate the cooling line back to the time of mixing to find the true ΔT.

S: Hess's law says the enthalpy change of a reaction is the same whatever route is taken. So an enthalpy change that cannot be measured directly can be calculated by going round an energy cycle built from changes that can.
<<<
## Hess's law
!def Hess's law | The enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route taken, provided the initial and final conditions are the same.

It follows from the conservation of energy. If route 1 goes directly from A to B and route 2 goes from A to C to B, then:

$$\Delta H_1 = \Delta H_2 + \Delta H_3$$

!mol An energy cycle
   A  ------ dH1 ------>  B
    \                    ^
     \ dH2              / dH3
      v                /
            C
!end

Going **against** an arrow, **reverse the sign**.

## Why it is needed
Some enthalpy changes cannot be found by direct experiment:

- the reaction is too slow, or needs conditions that cannot be used in a calorimeter
- other products form as well. Carbon and hydrogen do not react together cleanly to give only methane, so the ΔHf of methane cannot be measured directly.

## Cycles from enthalpy changes of formation
The elements go at the bottom, and both arrows point **up** from the elements.

$$\Delta H_r = \sum \Delta H_f(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta H_f(\text{reactants})$$

**Example.** $\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) \rightarrow CaO(s) + CO_2(g)}$

ΔHf in kJ mol⁻¹: CaCO₃ −1207, CaO −635, CO₂ −394.

$$\Delta H_r = (-635 - 394) - (-1207) = +178\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$$

Remember: ΔHf of an element is zero, and multiply each ΔHf by its balancing number.

## Cycles from enthalpy changes of combustion
The combustion products (CO₂ and H₂O) go at the bottom, and both arrows point **down**.

$$\Delta H_r = \sum \Delta H_c(\text{reactants}) - \sum \Delta H_c(\text{products})$$

**Example.** Find the ΔHf of methane: $\mathrm{C(s) + 2H_2(g) \rightarrow CH_4(g)}$

ΔHc in kJ mol⁻¹: C −394, H₂ −286, CH₄ −890.

$$\Delta H_f = [-394 + 2(-286)] - (-890) = -966 + 890 = -76\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$$

!key Formation data: **products − reactants**. Combustion data: **reactants − products**. If you forget, draw the cycle and follow the arrows.

## Cycles with bond energies
Break all the bonds in the reactants to give gaseous atoms, then form the bonds in the products.

$$\Delta H_r = \sum E(\text{bonds broken}) - \sum E(\text{bonds formed})$$

A bond energy can also be found from a cycle if ΔH is known.

**Example.** $\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightarrow 2NH_3(g)}$, ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹. N≡N 945, H–H 436. Find the N–H bond energy, E.

- broken: 945 + (3 × 436) = 2253
- formed: 6E
- −92 = 2253 − 6E, so 6E = 2345 and **E = 391 kJ mol⁻¹**

## Rules for oxidation numbers
| Rule | Oxidation number |
| an **uncombined element** (Na, O₂, Cl₂, S₈) | **0** |
| a **simple ion** | its **charge**: Na⁺ +1, Fe³⁺ +3, Cl⁻ −1 |
| **Group 1** metals in compounds | +1 |
| **Group 2** metals in compounds | +2 |
| **fluorine** in compounds | −1 |
| **hydrogen** in compounds | +1 (but −1 in metal hydrides, such as NaH) |
| **oxygen** in compounds | −2 (but −1 in peroxides, such as H₂O₂; +2 in F₂O) |
| chlorine in compounds | −1, unless combined with O or F |
| the sum in a **neutral compound** | **0** |
| the sum in a **compound ion** | the **charge** on the ion |

The more electronegative element takes the negative oxidation number.

### Examples
| Species | Working | Oxidation number |
| S in H₂SO₄ | 2(+1) + x + 4(−2) = 0 | **+6** |
| Mn in MnO₄⁻ | x + 4(−2) = −1 | **+7** |
| Cr in Cr₂O₇²⁻ | 2x + 7(−2) = −2 | **+6** |
| N in NH₄⁺ | x + 4(+1) = +1 | **−3** |
| N in NO₃⁻ | x + 3(−2) = −1 | **+5** |
| Cl in ClO₃⁻ | x + 3(−2) = −1 | **+5** |
| O in H₂O₂ | 2(+1) + 2x = 0 | **−1** |

| | Oxidation | Reduction |

!def Redox reaction | A reaction in which oxidation and reduction both take place.

!def Oxidising agent | A species that oxidises another species by accepting electrons; it is itself reduced.

$$\mathrm{2H_2O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O + O_2}$$

## Reversible reactions and dynamic equilibrium
A **reversible reaction** can go in both directions, shown by ⇌.

!def Dynamic equilibrium | The state in which the rates of the forward and reverse reactions are equal, and the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant.

It needs a **closed system**: nothing can enter or leave. If a product escaped, the reverse reaction could not take place.

The concentrations are constant, not necessarily equal.

## Le Chatelier's principle
!def Le Chatelier's principle | If a change is made to a system at dynamic equilibrium, the position of equilibrium moves to minimise this change.

| Change | Position of equilibrium moves |
| **increase the concentration** of a reactant | to the **right**, to use up the added reactant |
| **decrease the concentration** of a product | to the right, to replace it |
| **increase the pressure** | to the side with **fewer moles of gas** |
| **decrease the pressure** | to the side with more moles of gas |
| **increase the temperature** | in the **endothermic** direction |
| **decrease the temperature** | in the exothermic direction |
| **add a catalyst** | **no change**: both rates increase equally; equilibrium is reached **faster** |

If both sides have the same number of moles of gas, pressure has no effect on the position.

## The equilibrium constant Kc
For $\mathrm{aA + bB \rightleftharpoons cC + dD}$:

$$K_c = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]^c[\mathrm{D}]^d}{[\mathrm{A}]^a[\mathrm{B}]^b}$$

- [ ] means the **equilibrium concentration** in mol dm⁻³.
- **Products over reactants**, each raised to the power of its balancing number.
- **Solids** (and pure liquids) are left out in a heterogeneous equilibrium.
- Work out the **units** each time by cancelling.

| Equilibrium | $K_c$ | Units |
| $\mathrm{H_2 + I_2 \rightleftharpoons 2HI}$ | $\dfrac{[\mathrm{HI}]^2}{[\mathrm{H_2}][\mathrm{I_2}]}$ | none |
| $\mathrm{N_2 + 3H_2 \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3}$ | $\dfrac{[\mathrm{NH_3}]^2}{[\mathrm{N_2}][\mathrm{H_2}]^3}$ | mol⁻² dm⁶ |
| $\mathrm{2SO_2 + O_2 \rightleftharpoons 2SO_3}$ | $\dfrac{[\mathrm{SO_3}]^2}{[\mathrm{SO_2}]^2[\mathrm{O_2}]}$ | mol⁻¹ dm³ |

### Calculating equilibrium quantities
Use a table: **I**nitial moles, **C**hange, **E**quilibrium moles.

**Example.** 1.00 mol of H₂ and 1.00 mol of I₂ are mixed in a 1.00 dm³ vessel. At equilibrium there is 1.56 mol of HI.

| | H₂ | I₂ | 2HI |
| initial / mol | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0 |
| change / mol | −0.78 | −0.78 | +1.56 |
| equilibrium / mol | 0.22 | 0.22 | 1.56 |

$$K_c = \frac{1.56^2}{0.22 \times 0.22} = 50.3$$

Divide each amount by the volume to get the concentration. (Here the volumes cancel, because there are the same number of moles on each side.)

## Mole fraction and partial pressure
$$\text{mole fraction of A} = \frac{\text{moles of A}}{\text{total moles of gas}}$$

$$\text{partial pressure of A} = \text{mole fraction of A} \times \text{total pressure}$$

The partial pressures add up to the total pressure.

## The equilibrium constant Kp
For gases, $K_p$ is written in terms of **partial pressures**, p, with no square brackets.

$$K_p = \frac{p(\mathrm{NH_3})^2}{p(\mathrm{N_2}) \times p(\mathrm{H_2})^3}$$

**Example.** At equilibrium a mixture contains 0.40 mol of N₂O₄ and 1.20 mol of NO₂ at a total pressure of 200 kPa.

$$\mathrm{N_2O_4(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NO_2(g)}$$

- total moles = 1.60
- mole fractions: N₂O₄ 0.25; NO₂ 0.75
- partial pressures: N₂O₄ 50 kPa; NO₂ 150 kPa

$$K_p = \frac{p(\mathrm{NO_2})^2}{p(\mathrm{N_2O_4})} = \frac{150^2}{50} = 450\ \text{kPa}$$

## What changes the value of K
| Change | Position of equilibrium | Value of $K_c$ or $K_p$ |
| concentration | moves | **no change** |
| pressure | may move | **no change** |
| catalyst | no change | **no change** |
| **temperature** | moves | **changes** |

- Exothermic forward reaction: raising the temperature **decreases** K.
- Endothermic forward reaction: raising the temperature **increases** K.

## Rate of reaction
!def Rate of reaction | The change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time.

$$\text{rate} = \frac{\text{change in concentration}}{\text{time taken}} \qquad \text{units: mol dm}^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}$$

## Collision theory
Particles react only if they **collide**. Most collisions do not lead to a reaction.

| | |
| **effective collision** | a collision that leads to a reaction: the particles have **at least the activation energy** and the **correct orientation** |
| **non-effective collision** | the particles simply bounce apart: they have too little energy, or the wrong orientation |

## Concentration and pressure
| Change | Effect | Explanation |
| **increase the concentration** of a solution | rate increases | **more particles per unit volume**, so a **greater frequency of effective collisions** |
| **increase the pressure** of a gas | rate increases | the same number of molecules in a **smaller volume**: more particles per unit volume, so a greater frequency of effective collisions |

## Activation energy
!def Activation energy, EA | The minimum energy required for a collision to be effective.

It is the energy barrier that must be overcome: the energy needed to start breaking bonds. On a reaction pathway diagram, it is the height from the reactants to the peak.

## The Boltzmann distribution
The molecules in a gas have a range of energies. The Boltzmann distribution shows the **number of molecules** (vertical axis) with each **energy** (horizontal axis).

### How to sketch it
- The curve **starts at the origin**: no molecules have zero energy.
- It rises to a **peak**: the **most probable energy**.
- It falls away in a long tail and **never touches the energy axis**: there is no maximum energy.
- It is **not symmetrical**.
- The **area under the curve** is the **total number of molecules**.

Mark $E_A$ as a vertical line on the energy axis, to the right of the peak. The **shaded area to the right of $E_A$** is the number of molecules with energy **greater than or equal to** the activation energy: the molecules that can react.

## The effect of temperature
At a higher temperature, on the same axes:

- the **peak is lower** and **moves to the right** (to higher energy)
- the curve is **broader**
- the **area under the curve is the same**: the number of molecules has not changed
- the area to the right of $E_A$ is **much larger**.

!mol Boltzmann distribution at two temperatures (sketch)
number of
molecules
  |      _T1
  |    /    \       T2 (higher temperature)
  |   /   ___\_____
  |  /  /      \    \____
  | / /          \_______\_____
  |//_____________|______________ energy
                  EA
!end

### Why the rate increases
Two things happen when the temperature rises.

- The molecules move faster, so they **collide more frequently**. This is a small effect.
- **A greater proportion of the molecules have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy.** This is the main effect.

Together these give a much **greater frequency of effective collisions**, so the rate increases. A 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate of many reactions.

At a lower temperature the reverse is true: fewer molecules have the activation energy, and the rate falls.

## Catalysts
!def Catalyst | A substance that increases the rate of a reaction without being used up; it is chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction.

!def Catalysis | The process of increasing the rate of a reaction by using a catalyst.

### How a catalyst works
In the presence of a catalyst, the reaction has a **different mechanism**: an alternative route, which has a **lower activation energy**.

- The catalyst does **not** change ΔH.
- It does **not** change the position of equilibrium or the yield.
- It is not in the overall equation, though it takes part in the mechanism and is regenerated.

## On the Boltzmann distribution
The curve itself does **not change**: the temperature is the same.

- Mark the activation energy without a catalyst, $E_A$.
- Mark the activation energy with a catalyst, $E_A(\text{cat})$, **to the left** of it, at a lower energy.
- The area to the right of $E_A(\text{cat})$ is **much larger** than the area to the right of $E_A$.

So **a greater proportion of the molecules have energy greater than or equal to the (lower) activation energy**. There is a **greater frequency of effective collisions**, and the rate increases.

## On a reaction pathway diagram
Draw both routes on the same axes.

- The **reactants** and **products** lines are in the **same places** for both: ΔH is unchanged.
- The curve for the **catalysed** route has a **lower peak**.
- Label $E_A$ (uncatalysed) and $E_A$ (catalysed) as arrows from the reactants level to each peak.

A catalysed route often has two smaller humps, with an intermediate in the dip between them.

  |        /  ___  \   with catalyst

  |_____________________________ progress
!end

## Homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts
| Type | Meaning | Example |
| **homogeneous** | the catalyst is in the **same phase** as the reactants | H⁺(aq) catalysing the formation of an ester from an acid and an alcohol (all in solution) |
| **heterogeneous** | the catalyst is in a **different phase** from the reactants | solid **iron** in the Haber process; solid **V₂O₅** in the Contact process; solid platinum, palladium and rhodium in a catalytic converter |

A heterogeneous catalyst is usually a solid with gaseous reactants. The reaction takes place on its **surface**, so it is used as a powder, a mesh or a thin coating to give a large surface area.

## Why catalysts matter
- Reactions can be run at **lower temperatures and pressures**, which saves energy and money.
- Less fuel is burned, so less carbon dioxide is released.
- Enzymes, which are biological catalysts, let reactions happen quickly at body temperature.
