S: In everyday life "pure" means nothing has been added. In chemistry it means something stricter: a pure substance contains only one substance, a single element or a single compound. You can recognise it because it melts and boils at one exact temperature.
<<<
## What pure means in chemistry
!def Pure substance | A single element or a single compound, not mixed with any other substance.

| Sample | Pure in chemistry? | Why |
| distilled water | yes | only water, H₂O |

| "pure" orange juice | no | a mixture of water, sugars, acids and more |

## Identifying a pure substance
A pure substance has a **sharp, fixed melting point and boiling point**. Pure water melts at exactly 0 °C and boils at exactly 100 °C.

An **impure** substance:

- melts and boils **over a range** of temperatures, not at one temperature;
- usually has a **lower melting point** and a **higher boiling point** than the pure substance.

To identify an unknown pure substance, measure its melting point or boiling point and compare it with values in a data book.

## Using it
- **Gritting roads.** Salt mixed with ice makes the ice impure, so it melts below 0 °C.
- **Cooking.** Salty water boils a little above 100 °C.
- **Checking a medicine.** A chemist measures the melting point of a new batch. If it is sharp and matches the data book, the batch is pure.
>>>

M: Effect of an impurity on boiling point :: it is **raised**

<<<
## The words to know
| Word | Meaning | Example |

## Dissolving with particles
- The solvent particles collide with the solid and surround its particles.
- The solute particles break away and spread out evenly among the solvent particles.
- The solution is **transparent** (you can see through it), though it may be coloured.
- Mass is conserved: 5 g of salt in 100 g of water makes 105 g of solution.

| To dissolve a solid faster | Why it works |

## Solubility and temperature
Most solids are **more soluble in hot water** than in cold. A solubility curve is a graph of solubility against temperature. If a hot saturated solution is cooled, the solute that can no longer stay dissolved comes out as **crystals**.

## Different solvents
A substance that is insoluble in water may dissolve in another solvent. Nail varnish does not dissolve in water but it does dissolve in propanone (nail-varnish remover).

== k3-3-3
S: Diffusion is the spreading out of particles from where there are many of them to where there are few. Nobody has to stir: it happens because the particles of gases and liquids are always moving at random.
<<<
## What diffusion is
!def Diffusion | The movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, caused by their random movement.

- **Perfume.** Spray it in one corner and soon the whole room smells of it. Perfume particles mix with the moving air particles and spread out.
- **Ink in water.** A drop of ink slowly colours a whole beaker of water, without stirring.

Diffusion carries on until the particles are spread **evenly**.

## Explaining it with particles
The particles of a liquid or a gas move all the time, in random directions. They bump into each other and change direction. Bit by bit, particles of the two substances mix.

## How fast?
| Change | Effect on diffusion | Why |
| gas instead of liquid | much faster | gas particles move faster and there is more space between them |
| higher temperature | faster | particles have more energy and move faster |
| lighter particles | faster | at the same temperature, lighter particles move faster |
| solid | almost none | the particles cannot move from their fixed positions |
>>>

W: Particles do not "want" to spread out. They move at random, and that makes them spread.

M: Why diffusion happens :: the particles are in **constant random motion**
M: Why diffusion is faster in gases than in liquids :: gas particles **move faster** and are **further apart**
M: Effect of temperature on diffusion :: **faster** at a higher temperature because particles have **more energy**

== k3-3-4
S: The substances in a mixture are not chemically joined, so they can be separated by physical methods. The method to choose depends on what is mixed: filtering for an insoluble solid, evaporating for a dissolved solid, and distilling to get the liquid back.
<<<
## Choosing a method
| Mixture | Method | Example |
| an **insoluble solid** and a liquid | **filtration** | sand from water |
| a **soluble solid** dissolved in a liquid, when you want the solid | **evaporation** (crystallisation) | salt from salt water |
| a solution, when you want the **liquid** | **simple distillation** | pure water from sea water |
| liquids with **different boiling points** | **fractional distillation** | ethanol from water |
| several **dissolved coloured substances** | **chromatography** | the dyes in an ink |

## Filtration
Pour the mixture through **filter paper** in a funnel. The paper has tiny holes. The liquid particles pass through; the bits of insoluble solid are too big.

- The solid left on the paper is the **residue**.
- The liquid that passes through is the **filtrate**.

## Evaporation and crystallisation
Heat the solution in an evaporating basin. The solvent evaporates and the solute is left behind.

For good crystals, stop heating when crystals first begin to appear, then leave the solution to cool slowly. Slow cooling gives large crystals.

## Simple distillation
Distillation is **evaporation followed by condensation**.

- Heat the solution in a flask. The solvent boils and turns to vapour.
- The vapour passes into a **condenser**, a tube surrounded by a jacket of cold water.
- The vapour cools, condenses and drips into a beaker as pure liquid, the **distillate**.
- The solute stays behind in the flask.

## Worked example: pure salt from rock salt
Rock salt is a mixture of salt and sand.

- **Grind** the rock salt, so it dissolves faster.
- **Dissolve** it in warm water and stir. The salt dissolves; the sand does not.
- **Filter.** Sand is the residue. Salt solution is the filtrate.
- **Evaporate** the water from the filtrate to leave salt crystals.
>>>
W: Filtration cannot remove a dissolved solid. Dissolved particles are small enough to go through the paper.
W: Evaporation loses the solvent. Use distillation if you want to keep the liquid.
W: In a condenser, cold water goes in at the bottom and out at the top, so the jacket stays full.
M: Method to separate an insoluble solid from a liquid :: **filtration**
M: Residue and filtrate :: residue is the **solid left on the filter paper**; filtrate is the **liquid that passes through**
M: Method to get a dissolved solid from its solution :: **evaporation** (crystallisation)
M: What happens in distillation :: the liquid **evaporates** (boils), then the vapour **condenses**
M: Job of the condenser :: to **cool** the vapour so that it **condenses** back to a liquid

C: [1] You want pure water from salt water. Which method should you use? | filtration | evaporation | *distillation | chromatography | Distillation boils off the water and condenses it, so you keep the liquid.

== k3-3-5
S: Chromatography separates a mixture of soluble substances, such as the dyes in an ink. A solvent creeps up a strip of paper and carries each dye a different distance, so the mixture is pulled apart into spots.
<<<
## How to do it
- Draw a **start line in pencil** near the bottom of the chromatography paper. Pencil is insoluble, so it does not run.
- Put a small spot of the ink on the line.
- Stand the paper in a beaker with a little solvent, such as water. The solvent level must be **below the start line**, or the ink would just wash into the solvent.
- Wait while the solvent soaks up the paper.
- Take the paper out before the solvent reaches the top, and let it dry. The pattern of spots is a **chromatogram**.

## Why the dyes separate
Each dye is attracted to the paper and to the solvent by a different amount.

- A dye that is **very soluble** in the solvent is carried a **long way**.
- A dye that is **less soluble**, or sticks to the paper more strongly, travels only a **short way**.

## Reading a chromatogram
| What you see | What it means |
| **one spot** | a single dye: the substance may be pure |
| **two or more spots** | a mixture, with one spot for each dye |
| two spots at the **same height** | they are the same substance |
| a spot that **stays on the start line** | it is insoluble in that solvent |

## Uses
Chromatography is used to check food colourings, to compare inks in forensic work, and to see whether a substance is pure.
>>>
W: Draw the start line in pencil, never in ink. Ink would separate and spoil the result.
W: The solvent must start below the spots.
W: One spot does not prove a substance is pure: two dyes can travel the same distance. Try a different solvent to check.
M: Why the start line is drawn in pencil :: pencil is **insoluble** and **does not run** in the solvent
M: Why the solvent must be below the start line :: so the spots do **not dissolve into the solvent** in the beaker
M: Why the dyes separate :: they have **different solubilities** in the solvent, so they travel **different distances**
M: A chromatogram shows three spots from one ink :: the ink is a **mixture of three dyes**
C: [1] An ink gives four spots on a chromatogram. What does this show? | The ink is pure. | The solvent was too hot. | *The ink contains four dyes. | The paper was too long. | Each spot is a different substance.
C: [1] Why is the start line drawn in pencil? | Pencil is easier to see. | *Pencil does not dissolve in the solvent. | Pencil makes the dyes move faster. | Ink is too expensive. | An ink line would run up the paper with the sample.
C: [1] Which dye travels furthest up the paper? | the darkest one | the one with the largest spot | *the one most soluble in the solvent | the one put on first | The more soluble a dye is in the solvent, the further it is carried.

== k3-4-1
S: In a chemical reaction the atoms of the starting substances are rearranged to make new substances. That is the difference from a physical change such as melting, where the substance stays the same. Colour changes, bubbles and temperature changes are the clues that a reaction has happened.
<<<
## Physical changes and chemical changes
| | Physical change | Chemical change (reaction) |
| New substance? | no | **yes** |
| Easy to reverse? | usually | usually not |
| Examples | melting, boiling, dissolving, crushing | burning, rusting, cooking an egg, acid on a metal |

## Reactants and products
- The substances you start with are the **reactants**.
- The new substances that form are the **products**.

In a reaction, the atoms in the reactants **separate and join up in a new way**. The atoms themselves do not change, and none are lost. This is why the products have different properties from the reactants, and why mass is conserved.

Example: when hydrogen burns, hydrogen molecules and oxygen molecules break apart, and the atoms join again as water molecules.

## Signs of a chemical reaction
| Sign | Example |
| a **colour change** | blue copper sulfate solution fades when iron is added |
| a **gas** is given off (bubbles, fizzing) | magnesium in acid |
| a **temperature change** | a burning fuel heats its surroundings |
| a **solid appears** when two solutions are mixed (a precipitate) | mixing silver nitrate and salt solutions |
| light or sound | a firework |

One sign alone is not proof. Water bubbles when it boils, and that is only a physical change. The real test is whether a **new substance** has formed.
>>>
W: Dissolving and changes of state are physical changes. No new substance is made.
W: Atoms are not destroyed in a reaction, even in burning. They end up in the products, often as gases you cannot see.
M: Difference between a chemical and a physical change :: in a chemical change a **new substance** is formed
M: What happens to the atoms in a reaction :: they are **rearranged** (joined in a different way)
M: Reactants and products :: reactants are the **starting substances**; products are the **new substances formed**
M: Signs of a reaction :: **colour change**, **gas given off**, **temperature change**, **precipitate**

== k3-4-2
S: A chemical equation is a short way of writing a reaction: reactants on the left, an arrow, products on the right. A word equation uses names. A symbol equation uses formulae, and it must be balanced so that every atom is accounted for.
<<<
## Word equations
- Write the reactants on the left, joined by +.
- The arrow means "react to make". It is not an equals sign.
- Write the products on the right.

magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

iron + sulfur → iron sulfide

methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

## Symbol equations
Replace each name with its formula. Then check that there are the same number of each type of atom on both sides. Atoms cannot appear or vanish, so the equation must **balance**.

### Balancing, step by step
Hydrogen burns in oxygen to make water.

$$\mathrm{H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow H_2O}$$

Count the atoms. Left: 2 H and 2 O. Right: 2 H and 1 O. Oxygen does not balance.

Put a **big number in front** of a formula to multiply the whole formula. Two water molecules give 2 O:

$$\mathrm{H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O}$$

Now the right has 4 H, so use two hydrogen molecules:

$$\mathrm{2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O}$$

Left: 4 H and 2 O. Right: 4 H and 2 O. Balanced.

### More balanced equations
$$\mathrm{2Mg + O_2 \rightarrow 2MgO}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O}$$

!key Only ever change the **big numbers in front**. Never change the small numbers in a formula: that would turn it into a different substance.

## State symbols
| Symbol | Meaning |
| (s) | solid |
| (l) | liquid |
| (g) | gas |
| (aq) | aqueous: dissolved in water |

$$\mathrm{2Mg(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2MgO(s)}$$
>>>

W: To balance, change only the numbers in front of formulae. H₂O cannot become H₂O₂.
W: Oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen and chlorine gases are written O₂, H₂, N₂ and Cl₂ in symbol equations.
M: Word equation for burning magnesium :: magnesium + oxygen **→** magnesium oxide
M: Why a symbol equation must balance :: atoms are **not created or destroyed** in a reaction
M: Meaning of (aq) :: **aqueous**: dissolved in **water**
M: How to balance an equation :: put **numbers in front** of the formulae; never change the formulae themselves

## Neutralisation
!def Neutralisation | The reaction between an acid and a base (or alkali) to make a salt and water.

**acid + alkali → salt + water**

hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water

$$\mathrm{HCl + NaOH \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O}$$

As alkali is added to an acid, the pH rises towards 7. At exactly 7 the solution is neutral.

So an **acid** can be described as a substance that is neutralised by an alkali, and an **alkali** as a soluble substance that neutralises an acid.

## Naming the salt
The first part of the name comes from the **metal** in the alkali. The second part comes from the **acid**.

| Acid | Salt ending | Example |
| hydrochloric acid | chloride | sodium chloride |
| sulfuric acid | sulfate | copper sulfate |

## Acids and metals
**acid + metal → salt + hydrogen**

magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen

$$\mathrm{Mg + 2HCl \rightarrow MgCl_2 + H_2}$$

The mixture fizzes as hydrogen bubbles off, and the metal gets smaller. Unreactive metals such as copper do not react with dilute acids.

**Test for hydrogen:** hold a lighted splint at the mouth of the tube. Hydrogen burns with a **squeaky pop**.

## Acids and carbonates
acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide

This is why limestone buildings are slowly worn away by acid rain.

## Making salt crystals
- Add alkali to the acid a little at a time until the solution is neutral. Check with indicator paper.
- Evaporate some of the water by heating.
- Leave the solution to cool. Crystals of the salt form.

## Neutralisation in everyday life
- **Indigestion tablets** contain a base that neutralises excess stomach acid.
- Farmers spread **lime** on soil that is too acidic.
- **Toothpaste** is slightly alkaline. It neutralises the acids that bacteria make in your mouth.

## What a catalyst does
!def Catalyst | A substance that speeds up a chemical reaction and is not used up in the reaction.

- A catalyst makes a reaction go **faster**.
- It is **chemically unchanged** at the end, so it has the same mass and can be used again.
- Each catalyst works for particular reactions only.
- It works by giving the reaction an easier route that needs **less energy to get started**.

## Seeing a catalyst work
Hydrogen peroxide solution breaks down very slowly into water and oxygen. Add a little black manganese dioxide powder and it fizzes at once.

hydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen

Filter the mixture at the end and all the manganese dioxide is still there.

## Catalysts that matter
| Catalyst | Where | What it does |
| platinum and rhodium | catalytic converter in a car exhaust | turns poisonous carbon monoxide and nitrogen oxides into carbon dioxide and nitrogen |
| iron | making ammonia for fertilisers | speeds up the reaction of nitrogen with hydrogen |
| **enzymes** | living things | biological catalysts that speed up reactions such as digestion |

## Why industry uses catalysts
A reaction that is fast enough with a catalyst can be run at a **lower temperature**. That uses less fuel, costs less and puts less carbon dioxide into the air.
