S: Condensation polymers form when monomers with two functional groups join end to end, releasing a small molecule each time. Diols with dicarboxylic acids give polyesters; diamines with dicarboxylic acids give polyamides. A single monomer carrying both groups, such as an amino acid, can do the same.
<<<
## Condensation polymerisation
- Each monomer has **two functional groups**.
- When two monomers join, a **small molecule is eliminated**: **H₂O** (from a carboxylic acid) or **HCl** (from an acyl chloride).
- The monomers are linked by **ester** or **amide** bonds.

## Polyesters
| Monomers | Small molecule lost |
| a **diol** + a **dicarboxylic acid** | H₂O |
| a **diol** + a **dioyl chloride** | HCl |
| a **hydroxycarboxylic acid** (one monomer with –OH and –COOH) | H₂O |

The link formed is the **ester linkage, –COO–**.

### Terylene (PET)
Monomers: **ethane-1,2-diol**, HOCH₂CH₂OH, and **benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid**, HOOCC₆H₄COOH.

!mol Repeat unit of a polyester: Terylene (PET)
   /   O           O                \
   |   ||          ||               |
  -+-  C -[ring]-  C - O-CH2CH2-O  -+-
   \                                / n
!end

### From a hydroxycarboxylic acid
2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), CH₃CH(OH)COOH, polymerises with itself: the –OH of one molecule reacts with the –COOH of the next.

$$n\,\mathrm{HOCH(CH_3)COOH} \rightarrow \mathrm{-(OCH(CH_3)CO)_n-} + n\,\mathrm{H_2O}$$

The product, poly(lactic acid), is a biodegradable plastic.

## Polyamides
| Monomers | Small molecule lost |
| a **diamine** + a **dicarboxylic acid** | H₂O |
| a **diamine** + a **dioyl chloride** | HCl |
| an **aminocarboxylic acid** (one monomer with –NH₂ and –COOH) | H₂O |
| **amino acids** reacting together | H₂O |

The link formed is the **amide (peptide) linkage, –CO–NH–**.

### Nylon-6,6
Monomers: **hexane-1,6-diamine**, H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂, and **hexanedioic acid**, HOOC(CH₂)₄COOH (or hexanedioyl dichloride, ClOC(CH₂)₄COCl).

!mol Repeat unit of a polyamide: nylon-6,6
   /   O            O   H            H  \
   |   ||           ||  |            |  |
  -+-  C -(CH2)4-   C - N -(CH2)6-   N -+-
   \                                    / n
!end

### Other polyamides
- **Nylon-6** from one monomer, 6-aminohexanoic acid, H₂N(CH₂)₅COOH: repeat unit –HN(CH₂)₅CO–.
- **Kevlar** from benzene-1,4-diamine and benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid: very strong, because the rigid chains are held together by hydrogen bonds between the N–H and C=O groups.
- **Polypeptides and proteins** from **amino acids**: repeat unit –NH–CHR–CO–.

## Deducing the repeat unit from the monomers
- Draw the two monomers side by side, with the reacting groups facing each other.
- **Remove H₂O** (–OH from the acid, –H from the alcohol or amine), or **HCl** (–Cl from the acyl chloride, –H from the alcohol or amine).
- **Join** them through the new ester or amide link.
- The repeat unit contains **one of each monomer**. Draw a bond at each end through brackets, and write n.

For a single monomer with both groups, the repeat unit is that monomer minus H₂O.

## Identifying the monomers from a section of polymer
- Find the **ester (–COO–)** or **amide (–CONH–)** links.
- **Break** each link between the C=O carbon and the O or N.
- Add **–OH** to each C=O (making –COOH) and **–H** to each O or N (making –OH or –NH₂).

A chain –CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–NH–(CH₂)₆–NH– gives HOOC(CH₂)₄COOH and H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂.

If every unit is the same and has one –CO– end and one –O– (or –NH–) end, there is a single monomer: a hydroxycarboxylic acid or an aminocarboxylic acid.

<<<
## Why poly(alkene)s do not biodegrade
- Poly(alkene)s such as poly(ethene) and poly(propene) are long alkane chains.
- Their **C–C and C–H bonds are strong and non-polar**, so the polymers are **chemically inert**: they are not attacked by water, acids, alkalis or nucleophiles.
- Microorganisms and their enzymes cannot break them down, so they are **very difficult to biodegrade**.

They persist in landfill and in the oceans for hundreds of years. Burning them can release harmful gases.

## Polymers that degrade in light
Some polymers can be **degraded by the action of light**: they are **photodegradable**.

- Ultraviolet light is absorbed by particular groups in the chain, such as **C=O groups**, and breaks bonds near them.
- The chains break into shorter pieces, and the material becomes brittle and falls apart.
- Carbonyl groups can be built into a poly(alkene) chain deliberately to make it photodegradable.

Limitation: a photodegradable plastic buried in landfill receives no light, so it does not break down there.

## Polyesters and polyamides: biodegradable
**Polyesters and polyamides are biodegradable by acidic and alkaline hydrolysis.**

- Their chains contain **ester (–COO–)** or **amide (–CONH–)** links.
- These links are **polar**: the carbon of the C=O is δ+, so it can be attacked by nucleophiles such as water and OH⁻.
- **Hydrolysis** breaks the links and splits the chain back into small molecules, which microorganisms can use. Enzymes in living things catalyse the same reaction.

| | Polyester | Polyamide |
| **acid hydrolysis** | **diol + dicarboxylic acid** | **dicarboxylic acid + salt of the diamine** (H₃N⁺–R–NH₃⁺) |
| **alkaline hydrolysis** | **diol + salt of the dicarboxylic acid** (⁻OOC–R–COO⁻) | **diamine + salt of the dicarboxylic acid** |

In acid, any amine formed is protonated. In alkali, any carboxylic acid formed is deprotonated.

This is why nylon and polyester clothing is damaged by spills of acid or alkali, while poly(alkene) containers are used to store them.

## Examples of degradable polymers
| Polymer | Why it degrades |
| **poly(lactic acid), PLA** | a polyester, made from 2-hydroxypropanoic acid obtained from maize starch: its ester links are hydrolysed. Used for packaging, disposable cups and medical stitches that dissolve in the body |
| proteins | natural polyamides: their peptide links are hydrolysed by enzymes |
| starch-based plastics | natural condensation polymers that microorganisms can digest |

## Why it matters
- Less waste accumulates in landfill and in the environment.
- Polymers made from plant materials come from a renewable source, and reduce the use of crude oil.
- Hydrolysis can also be used deliberately to recycle a condensation polymer back to its monomers.

## Planning a route
- **Count carbons.** A longer chain needs –CN (KCN with a halogenoalkane, or HCN with a carbonyl compound) or a Friedel–Crafts reaction on a ring.
- **Work backwards** from the target one step at a time.
- **Think about directing effects** when two groups go onto a benzene ring: the order of the steps decides the isomer.
- **Protect reactive groups** by choosing the order: make an acyl chloride just before it is needed, and keep it dry.
- **Fewer steps** means a higher overall yield.

### Example 1: benzene to an azo dye
- benzene → nitrobenzene: concentrated HNO₃ and H₂SO₄, 25–60 °C
- nitrobenzene → phenylamine: Sn and concentrated HCl, heat; then NaOH(aq)
- phenylamine → benzenediazonium chloride: NaNO₂ and dilute HCl, below 10 °C
- couple with phenol in NaOH(aq)

### Example 2: methylbenzene to 3-nitrobenzoic acid
- **First** oxidise: hot alkaline KMnO₄, then dilute acid, to give benzoic acid.
- **Then** nitrate: –COOH is 3-directing, so the nitro group goes to position 3.

Nitrating first would give 2- and 4-nitromethylbenzene, and then the wrong acids.

### Example 3: ethanol to N-ethylethanamide, CH₃CONHCH₂CH₃
- Acid part: ethanol → ethanoic acid (acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, reflux) → ethanoyl chloride (SOCl₂).
- Amine part: ethanol → bromoethane (HBr) → ethylamine (NH₃ in ethanol, heat under pressure).
- Combine: ethanoyl chloride + ethylamine, room temperature.

### Example 4: bromoethane to propylamine
- bromoethane → propanenitrile: KCN in ethanol, heat
- propanenitrile → propylamine: LiAlH₄ (or H₂ / Ni)

Q: [4] Devise a synthesis of the azo dye formed from phenylamine and phenol, starting from benzene. Give reagents and conditions.
+ nitrate benzene: concentrated HNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄ (25–60 °C) :: nitr
+ reduce nitrobenzene: Sn and concentrated HCl, then NaOH(aq) :: tin|Sn
+ diazotise phenylamine: NaNO₂ and dilute HCl below 10 °C :: 10|NaNO2|nitrite|nitrous
+ couple with phenol in NaOH(aq) :: phenol
A: Nitrate benzene with concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids at 25–60 °C. Reduce the nitrobenzene with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid, then add NaOH(aq), to give phenylamine. Treat the phenylamine with sodium nitrite and dilute hydrochloric acid below 10 °C to form the diazonium salt. Add this to phenol dissolved in NaOH(aq).
Q: [3] Explain why the order of steps matters in the synthesis of 3-nitrobenzoic acid from methylbenzene.
+ the –CH₃ group directs to the 2- and 4-positions :: 2- and 4|2 and 4|2,4
+ the –COOH group directs to the 3-position :: 3-position|position 3|3 position|the 3
+ so the methyl group must be oxidised to –COOH before nitration :: oxidi & before|first
A: The methyl group directs an incoming group to the 2- and 4-positions, but the –COOH group directs to the 3-position. The methyl group must therefore be oxidised to –COOH first, before the nitration step.
