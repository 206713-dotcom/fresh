## Classifying alcohols
| Class | The carbon bonded to –OH is attached to | Example |
| **primary** | one other carbon | ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH; propan-1-ol |
| **secondary** | two other carbons | propan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ |
| **tertiary** | three other carbons | 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH₃)₃COH |

### With sodium
$$\mathrm{2C_2H_5OH + 2Na \rightarrow 2C_2H_5O^-Na^+ + H_2}$$

The sodium fizzes steadily and dissolves, giving **hydrogen** and **sodium ethoxide**. The reaction is slower than that of sodium with water.

### Oxidation
**Reagent:** acidified potassium dichromate(VI), K₂Cr₂O₇, or acidified potassium manganate(VII), KMnO₄.

| Alcohol | Conditions | Product |
| **primary** | **distil** off the product as it forms | **aldehyde** |
| **primary** | heat under **reflux**, with excess oxidising agent | **carboxylic acid** |
| **secondary** | heat (distil or reflux) | **ketone** |
| **tertiary** | | **no oxidation** |

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH + [O] \rightarrow CH_3CHO + H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH + 2[O] \rightarrow CH_3COOH + H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH)CH_3 + [O] \rightarrow CH_3COCH_3 + H_2O}$$

- **Distillation** removes the aldehyde from the oxidising agent as soon as it forms (it has a lower boiling point than the alcohol, having no hydrogen bonds), so it is not oxidised further.
- **Reflux** condenses the vapours back into the flask, so the aldehyde stays in contact with the oxidising agent and is fully oxidised.
- A tertiary alcohol has **no hydrogen atom on the carbon bonded to –OH**, so it cannot be oxidised without breaking a C–C bond.

**Colour changes:** acidified dichromate(VI) goes from **orange to green**; acidified manganate(VII) from **purple to colourless**.

!key **Distinguishing test:** warm with acidified K₂Cr₂O₇. **Primary and secondary** alcohols turn it from **orange to green**. **Tertiary** alcohols give **no change**.

### Dehydration to an alkene
Pass the vapour over a **heated catalyst** such as Al₂O₃, or heat with a **concentrated acid** (concentrated H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄). This is an elimination reaction.

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH \rightarrow CH_2{=}CH_2 + H_2O}$$

An unsymmetrical alcohol can give more than one alkene: butan-2-ol gives but-1-ene and cis- and trans-but-2-ene.

### Forming esters
Heat with a **carboxylic acid** and a **concentrated H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄ catalyst**.

$$\mathrm{CH_3COOH + C_2H_5OH \rightleftharpoons CH_3COOC_2H_5 + H_2O}$$

ethanoic acid + ethanol ⇌ ethyl ethanoate + water

## The tri-iodomethane (iodoform) test
Warm with **alkaline aqueous iodine**, I₂(aq) with NaOH(aq).

A **yellow precipitate** of **tri-iodomethane, CHI₃**, shows that the alcohol contains the group **CH₃CH(OH)–**. The other product is the ion **RCO₂⁻**, with one carbon fewer.

| Gives a yellow precipitate | Does not |
| ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH (the only primary alcohol) | methanol |
| propan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ | propan-1-ol |
| butan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ | 2-methylpropan-2-ol |

The iodine first oxidises the alcohol to a carbonyl compound with a CH₃CO– group, which then reacts.

## Acidity of alcohols compared with water
Alcohols are **weaker acids than water**.

$$\mathrm{C_2H_5OH \rightleftharpoons C_2H_5O^- + H^+}$$

**Explanation:** the **alkyl group is electron-donating** (a positive inductive effect). It pushes electron density towards the oxygen atom. This makes the O–H bond less polar and less easily broken, and it increases the charge density on the oxygen of the alkoxide ion, C₂H₅O⁻, making the ion **less stable** and more likely to accept a proton back. So the alcohol ionises less than water.

Evidence: sodium reacts less vigorously with ethanol than with water, and alcohols do not react with sodium hydroxide.

| Carbonyl compound | Product |
| aldehyde | **primary** alcohol |
| ketone | **secondary** alcohol |

$$\mathrm{CH_3CHO + 2[H] \rightarrow CH_3CH_2OH}$$

## Addition of hydrogen cyanide
**Reagents and conditions: HCN, with KCN as catalyst, and heat.** The product is a **hydroxynitrile**.

$$\mathrm{CH_3CHO + HCN \rightarrow CH_3CH(OH)CN}$$

ethanal → 2-hydroxypropanenitrile

$$\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3 + HCN \rightarrow (CH_3)_2C(OH)CN}$$

propanone → 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile

The carbon chain is **one carbon longer**: a useful step in synthesis.

### Mechanism: nucleophilic addition
- **Step 1.** The **lone pair on the carbon of the cyanide ion** attacks the **δ+ carbon** of the C=O group. The **π bond breaks**, and its pair of electrons moves onto the oxygen, giving an intermediate with a **negative charge on oxygen**.
- **Step 2.** The **lone pair on the O⁻** takes a **proton from HCN** (or from water). This forms the –OH group and **regenerates the CN⁻** ion, which is why it is a catalyst.

!mol Nucleophilic addition of HCN to ethanal
     CH3             CH3               CH3
      |               |                 |
NC:-  C = O  ->  NC - C - O:-  ->  NC - C - OH
      |               |                 |
      H               H                 H
 arrows: lone pair of CN- to C(d+); C=O pi
 bond to O; then O- lone pair to H of H-CN
!end

HCN alone reacts very slowly, because it is a weak acid and gives few CN⁻ ions. KCN supplies the nucleophile.

The carbonyl carbon is planar, so the cyanide ion can attack from either side. With an aldehyde or an unsymmetrical ketone the product has a chiral centre, and both optical isomers are formed in equal amounts.

Carboxylic acids are **weak acids**: partially dissociated in water.

$$\mathrm{CH_3COOH \rightleftharpoons CH_3COO^- + H^+}$$

They have high boiling points for their size and dissolve in water, because they form hydrogen bonds.

## Making carboxylic acids
| From | Reagents and conditions |
| a **primary alcohol** or an **aldehyde** | **acidified K₂Cr₂O₇** or **acidified KMnO₄**, heat under **reflux** |

| an **ester** | hydrolysis: heat with **dilute acid**; or with **dilute alkali, followed by acidification** |

### From alcohols and aldehydes
$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH + 2[O] \rightarrow CH_3COOH + H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_3CHO + [O] \rightarrow CH_3COOH}$$

### From nitriles
$$\mathrm{CH_3CN + 2H_2O + HCl \rightarrow CH_3COOH + NH_4Cl}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_3CN + NaOH + H_2O \rightarrow CH_3COONa + NH_3}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_3COONa + HCl \rightarrow CH_3COOH + NaCl}$$

### From esters
$$\mathrm{CH_3COOC_2H_5 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons CH_3COOH + C_2H_5OH}$$

In the structural formula the acid part is usually written first; in the name the alcohol part comes first.

## Making esters
**A condensation reaction** between an **alcohol** and a **carboxylic acid**, with **concentrated H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄ as catalyst**, heated under reflux.

$$\mathrm{CH_3COOH + CH_3CH_2OH \rightleftharpoons CH_3COOCH_2CH_3 + H_2O}$$

- The reaction is **reversible** and slow, and reaches equilibrium.
- The concentrated acid is a catalyst. It also absorbs water, which moves the equilibrium to the right.
- The ester usually has the lowest boiling point in the mixture (it cannot hydrogen-bond to itself), so it can be distilled off.

Esters have sweet, fruity smells. They are used as solvents, and in perfumes and food flavourings.

## Hydrolysis of esters
Hydrolysis breaks the ester linkage with water. The C–O bond between the C=O carbon and the oxygen breaks.

### With dilute acid, and heat
$$\mathrm{CH_3COOCH_2CH_3 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons CH_3COOH + CH_3CH_2OH}$$

- Products: the **carboxylic acid** and the **alcohol**.
- **Reversible**: an equilibrium mixture forms, so the hydrolysis is incomplete.
- The acid (dilute H₂SO₄ or HCl) is a catalyst.

### With dilute alkali, and heat
$$\mathrm{CH_3COOCH_2CH_3 + NaOH \rightarrow CH_3COO^-Na^+ + CH_3CH_2OH}$$

- Products: the **salt of the carboxylic acid** (a carboxylate) and the **alcohol**.
- **Goes to completion**: it is not reversible, because the carboxylate ion does not react with the alcohol.
- To obtain the carboxylic acid itself, **acidify** the mixture afterwards with a dilute strong acid.

| | Acid hydrolysis | Alkaline hydrolysis |
| Reagent | dilute acid, heat | dilute alkali, heat |
| Reversible? | yes | **no** |
| Products | carboxylic acid + alcohol | carboxylate salt + alcohol |
| Role of the reagent | catalyst | reactant |

Alkaline hydrolysis of the esters in fats and oils is how soap is made.

## Working out the products
Split the ester between the C=O carbon and the –O–. Add –OH to the C=O side to make the acid, and –H to the oxygen side to make the alcohol.

Methyl butanoate, CH₃CH₂CH₂COOCH₃, gives butanoic acid and methanol.
