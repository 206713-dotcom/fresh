## Classifying amines
| Class | Alkyl groups on N | Example |

- The substitution reactions are **nucleophilic substitution**: the nitrogen lone pair attacks the δ+ carbon of the halogenoalkane.
- Reducing a nitrile gives an amine with **one more carbon** than the halogenoalkane the nitrile was made from: bromoethane → propanenitrile → propylamine.
- Reducing an amide converts **C=O to CH₂**.

## Condensation with acyl chlorides
Ammonia and amines react with acyl chlorides at **room temperature** to give **amides**, with loss of HCl.

$$\mathrm{CH_3COCl + NH_3 \rightarrow CH_3CONH_2 + HCl}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_3COCl + CH_3CH_2NH_2 \rightarrow CH_3CONHCH_2CH_3 + HCl}$$

The second product is N-ethylethanamide. The nitrogen lone pair attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon: addition–elimination.

A **tertiary** amine cannot form an amide: it has no hydrogen on the nitrogen to lose.

## Basicity
Amines are **bases**: the **lone pair on the nitrogen atom accepts a proton**.

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2NH_2 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons CH_3CH_2NH_3^+ + OH^-}$$

Aqueous solutions of amines are **alkaline**: the OH⁻ ions give a pH of about 11 to 12.

With acids they form **salts**:

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2NH_2 + HCl \rightarrow CH_3CH_2NH_3^+Cl^-}$$

The product is ethylammonium chloride, an ionic solid that is soluble in water and has no smell. Adding a strong alkali releases the amine again.

### Why amines are stronger bases than ammonia
The **alkyl group is electron-donating** (a positive inductive effect). It **increases the electron density on the nitrogen atom**, so the **lone pair is more available** to accept a proton. It also helps to stabilise the positive charge on the alkylammonium ion.

Small amines are soluble in water because they form hydrogen bonds with water molecules, and they smell of fish or ammonia.

## Reactions of phenylamine
### With aqueous bromine
At **room temperature**, with no catalyst:

$$\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2 + 3Br_2 \rightarrow C_6H_2Br_3NH_2 + 3HBr}$$

The bromine is **decolourised** and a **white precipitate** of **2,4,6-tribromophenylamine** forms.

### Diazotisation
**Reagents: HNO₂, or NaNO₂ and dilute acid. Temperature: below 10 °C.**

$$\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2 + HNO_2 + HCl \rightarrow C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^- + 2H_2O}$$

- Nitrous acid is unstable, so it is made in the flask from sodium nitrite and dilute hydrochloric acid.
- The mixture is kept **below 10 °C** (in ice) because the diazonium ion **decomposes** at higher temperatures.

**Further warming of the diazonium salt with water gives phenol:**

$$\mathrm{C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^- + H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_5OH + N_2 + HCl}$$

## Azo compounds

!end

- The **alkaline** conditions convert phenol to the phenoxide ion, which is more reactive.
- The mixture must be kept **cold**, so that the diazonium ion does not decompose.

### The azo group
The **azo group is –N=N–**, joining two aromatic rings.

### Azo dyes
- Azo compounds are **often used as dyes**.
- The delocalised π systems of the two rings are linked through the –N=N– group into one **extended delocalised system**. This absorbs light in the visible region, so the compounds are strongly coloured, and it makes them very stable, so the colours do not fade.
- **Other azo dyes are made by the same route**: diazotise a different aromatic amine, and couple it with a different phenol or aromatic amine. Changing the components changes the colour. Coupling with naphthol gives a red dye.

**LiAlH₄** reduces the **C=O group of an amide to CH₂**, giving an **amine**.

$$\mathrm{CH_3CONH_2 + 4[H] \rightarrow CH_3CH_2NH_2 + H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_3CONHCH_3 + 4[H] \rightarrow CH_3CH_2NHCH_3 + H_2O}$$

A primary amide gives a primary amine; an N-substituted amide gives a secondary amine.

## Why amides are much weaker bases than amines
- In an amine, the lone pair on nitrogen is available to accept a proton.
- In an amide, the nitrogen is next to a **C=O group**. The **lone pair on the nitrogen is delocalised** with the π electrons of the C=O bond (the electronegative oxygen withdraws electron density).
- The lone pair is therefore **not available to accept a proton**.

Amides are essentially **neutral**: their aqueous solutions have a pH of about 7, and they do not form salts with dilute acids.

S: An amino acid carries both a basic –NH₂ group and an acidic –COOH group, so it exists as a zwitterion and changes its charge with pH. Amino acids link through peptide bonds, and a mixture of them can be separated by electrophoresis because each moves according to its charge.
<<<
## Amino acids
The amino acids in proteins are **2-amino acids**: the –NH₂ and –COOH groups are on the **same carbon atom**.

$$\mathrm{H_2N{-}CHR{-}COOH}$$

| Amino acid | R group | Systematic name |
| glycine | –H | aminoethanoic acid |
| alanine | –CH₃ | 2-aminopropanoic acid |

Except for glycine, the central carbon has four different groups: it is a **chiral centre**, so the amino acid has two optical isomers.

## Acid and base properties
- The **–COOH group is acidic**: it can donate a proton.
- The **–NH₂ group is basic**: its lone pair can accept a proton.

So an amino acid is **amphoteric**: it reacts with both acids and bases.

### Zwitterions
!def Zwitterion | An ion that carries both a positive and a negative charge, with no overall charge.

The –COOH group donates its proton to the –NH₂ group of the **same molecule**.

!mol Glycine and its zwitterion
     H   H   O                H   H   O
     |   |   ||               |   |   ||
 H - N - C - C - O-H      H - N+- C - C - O-
         |                    |   |
         H                    H   H
   uncharged form             zwitterion
!end

Amino acids exist as zwitterions in the solid state and in neutral solution. This explains why they are **crystalline solids with high melting points** and are **soluble in water**: there are strong ionic attractions between the zwitterions.

### The isoelectric point
!def Isoelectric point | The pH at which an amino acid has no overall charge (it exists as the zwitterion).

Each amino acid has its own isoelectric point: about 6 for glycine and alanine.

### The effect of pH
| pH | What happens | Species | Overall charge |
| **below** the isoelectric point (acidic) | –COO⁻ **accepts a proton** | H₃N⁺–CHR–COOH | **positive** |
| **at** the isoelectric point | | H₃N⁺–CHR–COO⁻ | **zero** |
| **above** the isoelectric point (alkaline) | –NH₃⁺ **loses a proton** | H₂N–CHR–COO⁻ | **negative** |

$$\mathrm{H_3N^+CH_2COO^- + H^+ \rightarrow H_3N^+CH_2COOH}$$

$$\mathrm{H_3N^+CH_2COO^- + OH^- \rightarrow H_2NCH_2COO^- + H_2O}$$

Because they remove both added H⁺ and added OH⁻, amino acid solutions act as buffers.

An amino acid whose R group contains a second –COOH (such as aspartic acid) has a low isoelectric point; one with a second –NH₂ (such as lysine) has a high one.

## Peptide bonds
The –COOH group of one amino acid reacts with the –NH₂ group of another. **Water is lost**, and an **amide (peptide) bond, –CO–NH–**, forms. This is a condensation reaction.

!mol Two amino acids form a dipeptide
       H   O             H   O
       |   ||            |   ||
 H2N - C - C - OH + H2N - C - C - OH
       |                 |
       R1                R2

       H   O   H   H   O
       |   ||  |   |   ||
 H2N - C - C - N - C - C - OH  +  H2O
       |           |
       R1          R2
         peptide bond: -CO-NH-
!end

| Peptide | Amino acids | Peptide bonds | Water molecules lost |
| **dipeptide** | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| **tripeptide** | 3 | 2 | 2 |
| polypeptide / protein | many | many | |

Two different amino acids, glycine and alanine, can give **two different dipeptides**: Gly–Ala (glycine's –COOH used) and Ala–Gly (alanine's –COOH used). They are structural isomers.

**Hydrolysis** (heating with aqueous acid or alkali) breaks the peptide bonds and gives back the amino acids.

## Electrophoresis
Electrophoresis separates amino acids and peptides by their **charge**.

### Method
- A spot of the mixture is placed in the middle of a strip of paper or gel soaked in a **buffer solution** of known pH.
- A **potential difference** is applied across the strip.
- After a time the strip is dried and sprayed with a locating agent (ninhydrin) to show where the amino acids are.

### What moves where
| The species is | Because the buffer pH is | It moves towards |
| a **cation** (positive) | **below** its isoelectric point | the **negative electrode** (cathode) |
| a **zwitterion** (no overall charge) | **equal to** its isoelectric point | it **does not move** |
| an **anion** (negative) | **above** its isoelectric point | the **positive electrode** (anode) |

### How far
- A **greater charge** moves the ion **faster**.
- A **smaller** ion (lower $M_r$) moves **faster**; a larger one moves more slowly.

**Example.** At pH 6, glycine (isoelectric point 6.0) stays at the start. Lysine (isoelectric point 9.7) is positively charged and moves towards the negative electrode. Aspartic acid (isoelectric point 2.8) is negatively charged and moves towards the positive electrode.

A dipeptide behaves in the same way: its free –NH₂ and –COOH ends, and any ionisable R groups, decide its charge at a given pH. Being larger than a single amino acid of the same charge, it moves more slowly.
