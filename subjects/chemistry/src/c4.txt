== ci-8-1
S: The Periodic Table lists the elements in order of proton number, in rows called periods and columns called groups. Elements in the same group have the same number of outer-shell electrons, so they react in similar ways. Knowing where an element sits lets you predict what it is like.
<<<
## How the table is arranged
- The elements are in order of **increasing proton number** (atomic number).
- A horizontal row is a **period**. A vertical column is a **group**.
- The **period number** is the number of occupied electron shells.
- The **group number** (I to VII) is the number of outer-shell electrons. Group VIII, the noble gases, have a full outer shell.

## Metals and non-metals
- **Metals** are on the left and in the centre. **Non-metals** are on the right.
- **Across a period**, from left to right, the character of the elements changes from **metallic to non-metallic**.

In Period 3: sodium, magnesium and aluminium are metals; silicon is in between; phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine and argon are non-metals. Their oxides change in the same way, from basic through amphoteric to acidic.

## Group number and the charge on the ion
| Group | I | II | III | V | VI | VII |
| Outer electrons | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| Ion formed | 1+ | 2+ | 3+ | 3− | 2− | 1− |
| Example | Na⁺ | Mg²⁺ | Al³⁺ | N³⁻ | O²⁻ | Cl⁻ |

- Metals in Groups I, II and III lose their outer electrons: the charge equals the group number.
- Non-metals in Groups V, VI and VII gain electrons to fill the outer shell: the charge is the group number minus 8.

## Why elements in a group are alike
!key Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of outer-shell electrons.

Chemical reactions involve the outer-shell electrons. Lithium (2,1), sodium (2,8,1) and potassium (2,8,8,1) each have one, so they all react in the same way, for example with water.

Down a group the properties change gradually. These gradual changes are called **trends**.

## Using position to predict properties
From an element's position you can predict:

- whether it is a metal or a non-metal
- its number of outer-shell electrons and the charge on its ion
- the formulae of its compounds
- its properties, by following the trend from the elements above and below it.

**Example.** Rubidium is below potassium in Group I. So it is a soft metal, more reactive than potassium, with a lower melting point. It forms Rb⁺ ions and its chloride is RbCl.

## Identifying a trend from data
You may be given a table of data for a group you have not studied.

- Look at how the property changes **down the group**: increasing or decreasing.
- Check whether the change is steady.
- Predict a missing value by choosing a number that fits between its neighbours, or continues the pattern.

**Example.** The melting points of three elements going down a group are 181 °C, 98 °C and 63 °C. The trend is a decrease, so the next element should melt below 63 °C: about 39 °C.

S: Lithium, sodium and potassium are the alkali metals. They are soft, light, and react vigorously with water to give hydrogen and an alkali. Going down the group they melt more easily, get denser and become more reactive.

## The alkali metals
Group I contains lithium, sodium and potassium (and rubidium, caesium and francium below them).

- They are **relatively soft metals**: they can be cut with a knife. A freshly cut surface is shiny but tarnishes quickly in air.
- They have **low densities**: lithium, sodium and potassium float on water.
- They have **low melting points** for metals.
- They are very reactive, so they are stored under **oil** to keep out air and water.
- Each atom has **one outer-shell electron**, which it loses to form a **1+ ion**.

## Trends down the group
| Property | Trend down the group |
| **melting point** | **decreases** |
| **density** | **increases** |
| **reactivity** | **increases** |

| Element | Melting point / °C | Density / g/cm³ |
| lithium | 181 | 0.53 |
| sodium | 98 | 0.97 |
| potassium | 63 | 0.86 |

The general trend in density is an increase, although potassium is slightly less dense than sodium.

## Reaction with water
All the alkali metals react with cold water to form a **metal hydroxide** and **hydrogen**. The hydroxide dissolves to give an **alkaline** solution.

$$\mathrm{2Na(s) + 2H_2O(l) \rightarrow 2NaOH(aq) + H_2(g)}$$

The same equation works for each metal: just change the symbol.

| Metal | Observations |
| **lithium** | floats; fizzes steadily; moves slowly; gets smaller and disappears |
| **sodium** | floats; **melts into a ball**; fizzes rapidly and moves quickly across the surface; disappears |
| **potassium** | floats; melts; fizzes very rapidly; **catches fire and burns with a lilac flame**; may spit or explode at the end |

Universal indicator added to the water turns purple, showing that the solution is alkaline.

### Why reactivity increases down the group
Going down the group, the atoms have more electron shells. The outer electron is further from the nucleus and is attracted less strongly, so it is **lost more easily**.

== ci-8-3
S: Chlorine, bromine and iodine are the halogens: coloured, diatomic non-metals. Down the group they get darker, denser and less reactive. A more reactive halogen pushes a less reactive one out of its compounds.
<<<
## The halogens
Group VII contains fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine.

- They are **non-metals**.
- They exist as **diatomic molecules**: Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.
- Each atom has **seven outer-shell electrons**. It gains one to form a **1− ion**, called a halide ion: Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻.
- They are poisonous.

## Appearance at room temperature and pressure
| Halogen | Appearance at r.t.p. |
| **chlorine** | **pale yellow-green gas** |
| **bromine** | **red-brown liquid** |
| **iodine** | **grey-black solid** |

## Trends down the group
| Property | Trend down the group |
| **density** | **increases** |
| **reactivity** | **decreases** |
| colour | gets darker |
| melting and boiling points | increase (gas → liquid → solid) |

!warn Reactivity **decreases** down Group VII. This is the opposite of Group I.

Why: going down the group, the atoms are larger. The outer shell is further from the nucleus, so an extra electron is attracted less strongly and is **gained less easily**.

## Displacement reactions
!key A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from an aqueous solution of its halide.

Order of reactivity: **chlorine > bromine > iodine**.

| Halogen added | to potassium chloride | to potassium bromide | to potassium iodide |
| **chlorine** | (no reaction) | **orange** solution: bromine formed | **brown** solution: iodine formed |
| **bromine** | no reaction | (no reaction) | **brown** solution: iodine formed |
| **iodine** | no reaction | no reaction | (no reaction) |

$$\mathrm{Cl_2(aq) + 2KBr(aq) \rightarrow 2KCl(aq) + Br_2(aq)}$$

$$\mathrm{Cl_2(aq) + 2KI(aq) \rightarrow 2KCl(aq) + I_2(aq)}$$

$$\mathrm{Br_2(aq) + 2KI(aq) \rightarrow 2KBr(aq) + I_2(aq)}$$

The potassium ions are spectators. The ionic equation shows what really happens:

$$\mathrm{Cl_2 + 2Br^- \rightarrow 2Cl^- + Br_2}$$

This is a redox reaction. Chlorine gains electrons: it is reduced, and it is the oxidising agent. Bromide ions lose electrons: they are oxidised.

### Explaining a result
Chlorine displaces bromine from potassium bromide **because chlorine is more reactive than bromine**. Iodine does not react with potassium bromide because iodine is less reactive than bromine.

## Predicting the properties of other Group VII elements
- **Fluorine**, above chlorine: a pale yellow gas, the **most reactive** halogen, with the lowest density and boiling point. It displaces all the other halogens.
- **Astatine**, below iodine: a **black solid**, the **least reactive**, with the highest density and melting point. It would be displaced by all the others.

## The noble gases
Group VIII contains helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon.

- They are **unreactive** (inert).
- They are **monatomic**: they exist as single atoms, not molecules. Their formulae are He, Ne, Ar.
- They are colourless gases at room temperature, with very low melting and boiling points.

## Why they are unreactive
!key The noble gases have a **full outer shell** of electrons. This electronic configuration is stable, so they do not gain, lose or share electrons.

| Element | Electronic configuration |
| helium | 2 |
| neon | 2,8 |
| argon | 2,8,8 |

Other atoms react in order to reach a noble gas configuration. The noble gases already have one.

This also explains why they are monatomic: with a full outer shell, an atom has no need to share electrons with another atom, so no molecules form.

- Density increases.
- Boiling point increases.

## Chemical properties of metals
### With dilute acids
$$\text{metal} + \text{acid} \rightarrow \text{salt} + \text{hydrogen}$$

$$\mathrm{Zn + 2HCl \rightarrow ZnCl_2 + H_2}$$

Only metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series react. You see effervescence, and the metal gets smaller.

### With cold water
The most reactive metals react with cold water.

$$\text{metal} + \text{water} \rightarrow \text{metal hydroxide} + \text{hydrogen}$$

$$\mathrm{Ca + 2H_2O \rightarrow Ca(OH)_2 + H_2}$$

### With steam
Less reactive metals such as magnesium, zinc and iron react with steam, giving the oxide.

$$\text{metal} + \text{steam} \rightarrow \text{metal oxide} + \text{hydrogen}$$

$$\mathrm{Mg + H_2O(g) \rightarrow MgO + H_2}$$

### With oxygen
$$\text{metal} + \text{oxygen} \rightarrow \text{metal oxide}$$

$$\mathrm{2Mg + O_2 \rightarrow 2MgO}$$

Magnesium burns with a bright white flame to give a white powder. Copper does not burn, but its surface turns black as copper(II) oxide forms. Metal oxides are basic.

## Reactivity and extraction
An **ore** is a rock from which a metal can be extracted. Most metals are found as compounds, often oxides.

| Position in the reactivity series | Method of extraction | Examples |
| **above carbon** | **electrolysis** of the molten compound | potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, **aluminium** |

| very unreactive | found **uncombined** (native) | silver, gold |

!key The more reactive the metal, the harder it is to extract, because its compounds are more stable.

Carbon can only reduce the oxides of metals that are less reactive than carbon. Electrolysis works for any metal but uses a great deal of electricity, so it is used only when reduction with carbon is impossible.

## Extracting iron in the blast furnace
The raw materials:

| Raw material | What it is | Why it is added |
| **hematite** | iron ore: mainly iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃ | the source of iron |
| **coke** | carbon | burns to give heat; makes the reducing agent |
| **limestone** | calcium carbonate, CaCO₃ | removes acidic impurities |
| **hot air** | | supplies oxygen for burning the coke |

### What happens
- **The coke burns** in the hot air. This provides the heat and produces carbon dioxide.

$$\mathrm{C + O_2 \rightarrow CO_2}$$

- **The carbon dioxide is reduced to carbon monoxide** by more hot coke.

$$\mathrm{C + CO_2 \rightarrow 2CO}$$

- **The carbon monoxide reduces the iron(III) oxide** to iron.

$$\mathrm{Fe_2O_3 + 3CO \rightarrow 2Fe + 3CO_2}$$

- **The limestone decomposes** in the heat to calcium oxide.

$$\mathrm{CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2}$$

- **Slag forms.** The calcium oxide, a basic oxide, reacts with silicon(IV) oxide (sand), an acidic impurity in the ore, to form calcium silicate: slag.

$$\mathrm{CaO + SiO_2 \rightarrow CaSiO_3}$$

Molten iron runs to the bottom of the furnace. Molten slag is less dense, so it floats on the iron. The two are tapped off separately.

In the main reaction, iron(III) oxide loses oxygen: it is **reduced**. Carbon monoxide gains oxygen: it is **oxidised**, and it is the **reducing agent**.

## Extracting aluminium
The main ore of aluminium is **bauxite**. It is purified to give **aluminium oxide**, Al₂O₃ (alumina). Aluminium is more reactive than carbon, so it is extracted by **electrolysis**.

### The electrolysis cell
| Part | Detail |
| **electrolyte** | **aluminium oxide dissolved in molten cryolite** |
| **cathode** (−) | the carbon (graphite) lining of the steel cell |
| **anode** (+) | blocks of carbon (graphite) dipping into the electrolyte |

### The role of cryolite
Aluminium oxide melts at over 2000 °C, which would be far too expensive to maintain.

- Aluminium oxide **dissolves in molten cryolite**, giving a mixture that is molten at about 950 °C.
- This **lowers the operating temperature**, so **less energy** is needed and the cost is lower.
- It also improves the **electrical conductivity** of the electrolyte.

### At the electrodes
**Cathode:** aluminium ions gain electrons. This is reduction.

$$\mathrm{Al^{3+} + 3e^- \rightarrow Al}$$

Molten aluminium collects at the bottom of the cell and is run off.

**Anode:** oxide ions lose electrons. This is oxidation.

$$\mathrm{2O^{2-} \rightarrow O_2 + 4e^-}$$

Oxygen gas is formed at the anode.

### Why the anodes must be replaced
!key The oxygen formed at the hot carbon anodes **reacts with the carbon** to form carbon dioxide. The anodes **burn away**, so they must be replaced regularly.

$$\mathrm{C + O_2 \rightarrow CO_2}$$

S: Clean air is about 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen. Burning fuels and farming add pollutants: carbon dioxide and methane warm the planet, carbon monoxide is toxic, particulates damage the lungs, and sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen cause acid rain. Each has its own source and its own remedy.

## The composition of clean, dry air
| Gas | Percentage by volume |
| **nitrogen**, N₂ | approximately **78%** |
| **oxygen**, O₂ | **21%** |
| the remainder | a mixture of **noble gases** (mainly argon) and **carbon dioxide** |

## Air pollutants: sources and effects
| Pollutant | Source | Adverse effect |
| **carbon dioxide** | **complete combustion** of carbon-containing fuels | higher levels lead to increased **global warming**, which leads to **climate change** |
| **carbon monoxide** | **incomplete combustion** of carbon-containing fuels | a **toxic gas** |
| **particulates** (soot) | **incomplete combustion** of carbon-containing fuels | increased risk of **respiratory problems** and **cancer** |
| **methane** | **decomposition of vegetation**; **waste gases from digestion in animals** | higher levels lead to increased **global warming**, which leads to **climate change** |
| **oxides of nitrogen** | **car engines** | **acid rain**, **photochemical smog** and **respiratory problems** |
| **sulfur dioxide** | combustion of **fossil fuels which contain sulfur compounds** | **acid rain** |

### Complete and incomplete combustion
- **Complete combustion** happens when there is plenty of oxygen. The carbon in the fuel becomes **carbon dioxide**.
- **Incomplete combustion** happens when the oxygen supply is limited. **Carbon monoxide** and **carbon** (particulates, soot) are formed.

Carbon monoxide is toxic because it combines with haemoglobin in the blood, so the blood carries less oxygen. It is colourless and has no smell.

### Acid rain
Sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen dissolve in rain water and react to form acids. Acid rain damages buildings made of limestone, corrodes metals, kills trees and makes lakes too acidic for fish.

## How greenhouse gases cause global warming
**Carbon dioxide** and **methane** are greenhouse gases.

- Thermal energy from the Sun passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface.
- The warm surface emits thermal energy back towards space.
- The greenhouse gases **absorb** some of this thermal energy, and **reflect and emit** it in all directions, including back towards the Earth.
- This **reduces the loss of thermal energy to space**, so the average temperature of the Earth rises.

More carbon dioxide and methane in the atmosphere means more thermal energy is kept in. This is global warming, and it leads to climate change: melting ice, rising sea levels, and more extreme weather.

## Oxides of nitrogen and catalytic converters
- In a **car engine**, the temperature is very high. **Nitrogen and oxygen from the air react** together to form oxides of nitrogen.
- A **catalytic converter** in the exhaust removes them. Its catalyst (platinum and similar metals) makes the oxides of nitrogen react with carbon monoxide, another pollutant, to form harmless gases.

$$\mathrm{2CO + 2NO \rightarrow 2CO_2 + N_2}$$

Carbon monoxide is oxidised to carbon dioxide, and nitrogen monoxide is reduced to nitrogen.

## Reducing the problems
### Climate change
- **planting trees**: they remove carbon dioxide by photosynthesis
- **reduction in livestock farming**: less methane
- **decreasing the use of fossil fuels**
- **increasing the use of hydrogen and renewable energy**, such as **wind** and **solar**.

### Acid rain
- **catalytic converters** in vehicles: remove oxides of nitrogen
- **low-sulfur fuels**: less sulfur dioxide is formed
- **flue gas desulfurisation with calcium oxide**: in power stations, the waste gases are passed through calcium oxide, a base, which reacts with the acidic sulfur dioxide and removes it.

$$\mathrm{CaO + SO_2 \rightarrow CaSO_3}$$

## Photosynthesis
Photosynthesis is the reaction between **carbon dioxide and water** to produce **glucose and oxygen**, in the presence of **chlorophyll** and using **energy from light**.

carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen

$$\mathrm{6CO_2 + 6H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2}$$

It removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and releases oxygen, which is why planting trees helps to slow climate change.
