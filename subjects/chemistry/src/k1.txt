== k3-1-1
S: Everything is made of particles that are far too small to see. How closely the particles are packed, and how freely they can move, decides whether a substance is a solid, a liquid or a gas.
<<<
## The three states of matter
| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
| Shape | fixed | takes the shape of the bottom of its container | fills the whole container |
| Volume | fixed | fixed | changes to fill the space it is given |
| Can it be squashed? | no | no | yes |
| Does it flow? | no | yes | yes |

## The particle model
The particle model says that all matter is made of tiny particles, and that there are forces of attraction between the particles.

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
| **How close?** | touching | touching | far apart |
| **Arrangement** | regular rows (a pattern) | random | random |
| **Movement** | vibrate on the spot | slide past each other | move fast in all directions |
| **Forces between particles** | strong | weaker | almost none |

## Explaining properties with particles
- A solid keeps its shape because strong forces hold its particles in fixed positions.
- A liquid flows and can be poured because its particles can slide past each other.
- Solids and liquids cannot be squashed because their particles are already touching: there is no space to move into.
- A gas can be squashed (**compressed**) because there is a lot of empty space between its particles.
- A gas spreads out to fill its container because its particles move quickly in every direction.

## Density
Density tells you how much mass is packed into a certain volume. Solids and liquids are dense because their particles are packed closely together. Gases have a very low density because their particles are far apart.

!key When you draw particles, make them all the same size. Solid: neat rows, touching. Liquid: touching but jumbled. Gas: only a few, far apart.
>>>
W: Liquid particles still touch each other. Do not draw gaps between them.
W: The particles of a solid are not still. They vibrate about a fixed position.
W: The particles themselves are not solid, liquid or gas. A state describes how lots of particles behave together.
M: Arrangement of particles in a solid :: **regular** pattern, particles **touching**
M: Movement of particles in a solid :: **vibrate** about **fixed positions**
M: Particles in a liquid :: **touching**, **random** arrangement, **slide past** each other
M: Particles in a gas :: **far apart**, **random**, move **quickly in all directions**
M: Why a gas can be compressed :: there is **empty space between** the particles
M: Why a liquid can be poured :: the particles can **move (slide) past each other**

== k3-1-2
S: The particles of a gas are always moving. Each time a particle hits the wall of its container it gives the wall a tiny push. Billions of these pushes every second add up to the pressure of the gas.
<<<
## What causes gas pressure
Gas particles move quickly in straight lines until they hit something. When a particle **collides with the wall** of the container, it pushes on the wall. Pressure is the total push of all the collisions on each square centimetre of wall.

!def Gas pressure | The force on the walls of a container caused by gas particles colliding with them.

## Changing the pressure
| Change | What the particles do | Pressure |
| pump **more gas** in | more particles, so more collisions with the walls each second | goes **up** |
| make the container **smaller** | the same particles hit the walls more often | goes **up** |
| **heat** the gas | particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and harder | goes **up** |
| **cool** the gas | particles move more slowly, so they hit the walls less often and less hard | goes **down** |

## Seeing it happen
- **Pumping up a tyre.** Each stroke forces more air particles in, so there are more collisions with the inside of the tyre and the pressure rises.
- **A balloon in the sun.** The air inside warms up, its particles move faster and push the rubber outwards, so the balloon gets bigger.
- **The collapsing can.** Boil a little water in a can, seal it and cool it. The steam inside condenses, leaving very few gas particles to hit the inside walls. The air outside is still hitting the outside, so the can is crushed.

## Atmospheric pressure
The air around us is a gas, so its particles are hitting everything all the time. This is atmospheric pressure. We do not notice it because the pressure inside our bodies balances it.
>>>
W: Particles do not get bigger when a gas is heated. They move faster and spread further apart.
W: Pressure comes from particles hitting the walls, not from particles hitting each other.
M: Cause of gas pressure :: particles **collide with the walls** of the container
M: Effect of heating a gas in a sealed container :: particles move **faster**, collide with the walls **more often** and **harder**, so pressure **increases**
M: Effect of squashing a gas into a smaller volume :: particles hit the walls **more often**, so pressure **increases**
M: Why a balloon gets bigger when warmed :: particles move **faster** and **push the walls outwards**
C: [1] What causes the pressure of a gas? | particles hitting each other | particles getting bigger | *particles hitting the walls of the container | particles sticking to the walls | Every collision with the wall gives it a small push.
C: [1] A sealed metal can of air is heated. What happens to the pressure inside? | It stays the same. | *It increases. | It decreases. | It drops to zero. | Faster particles hit the walls more often and with more force.
C: [1] More air is pumped into a football. Why does the pressure rise? | The particles move faster. | The particles get heavier. | The ball gets colder. | *There are more particles hitting the inside of the ball. | More particles in the same space means more collisions with the walls every second.

== k3-1-3
S: Heating gives particles more energy and cooling takes energy away. When the particles have enough energy to break free of the forces holding them, or lose enough to be pulled back together, the substance changes state. Nothing is lost: the particles are the same before and after.
<<<
## Naming the changes
| Change | Name | Energy |
| solid to liquid | **melting** | taken in |
| liquid to gas | **boiling** or **evaporating** | taken in |
| gas to liquid | **condensing** | given out |
| liquid to solid | **freezing** | given out |
| solid straight to gas | **subliming** | taken in |

## What the particles do
- **Melting.** As a solid is heated its particles vibrate faster. At the melting point they have enough energy to break away from their fixed positions and slide past each other.
- **Boiling.** At the boiling point the particles have enough energy to break free of each other completely. Bubbles of gas form all through the liquid.
- **Evaporating.** The fastest particles at the surface escape, even when the liquid is well below its boiling point. This is why a puddle dries up.
- **Condensing.** As a gas cools its particles slow down. The forces of attraction pull them together to form a liquid.
- **Freezing.** As a liquid cools its particles slow down until they can only vibrate in fixed positions.

## Melting point and boiling point
Every pure substance has its own melting point and boiling point. Use them to work out the state at any temperature.

| Temperature of the substance | State |
| below the melting point | solid |
| between the melting point and the boiling point | liquid |
| above the boiling point | gas |

Example: oxygen melts at −219 °C and boils at −183 °C. Room temperature, 20 °C, is above the boiling point, so oxygen is a gas.

## Boiling and evaporating are different
| | Boiling | Evaporating |
| Temperature | only at the boiling point | at any temperature |
| Where | all through the liquid | only at the surface |
| Speed | fast | slow |

!key A change of state is a **physical change**. No new substance is made, the mass stays the same, and the change can be reversed.
>>>
W: Ice, water and steam are the same substance. Only the arrangement and movement of the particles change.
W: The bubbles in boiling water are water as a gas (steam), not air.
W: The temperature does not rise while a substance is melting or boiling, even though it is still being heated.
M: Solid to liquid :: **melting**
M: Gas to liquid :: **condensing**
M: Liquid to solid :: **freezing**
M: Explaining melting :: particles **gain energy**, **vibrate faster** and **break away from fixed positions**
M: Difference between boiling and evaporation :: boiling happens **throughout** the liquid at the **boiling point**; evaporation happens at the **surface** at **any temperature**
M: Working out a state :: compare the temperature with the **melting point** and the **boiling point**
C: [1] What is the change from gas to liquid called? | melting | freezing | *condensing | evaporating | Condensing is gas to liquid. It gives out energy.
C: [1] A substance melts at 50 °C and boils at 120 °C. What is its state at 80 °C? | solid | *liquid | gas | it cannot be worked out | 80 °C is above the melting point and below the boiling point.
C: [1] Where does evaporation happen? | all through the liquid | at the bottom of the liquid | *at the surface of the liquid | only at the boiling point | Only particles at the surface can escape below the boiling point.

== k3-2-1
S: An atom is the smallest particle of an element that can exist. Around 1803 John Dalton pictured atoms as tiny solid spheres, like snooker balls, that cannot be broken. His simple model still explains elements, compounds and why mass is conserved in reactions.
<<<
## What an atom is
!def Atom | The smallest particle of an element that can exist.

Atoms are extremely small. About five million of them, side by side, would stretch across one millimetre.

## Dalton's atomic model
John Dalton was an English schoolteacher. His model says:

- All matter is made of **atoms**, which are tiny solid spheres.
- Atoms **cannot be created, destroyed or divided**.
- All the atoms of one **element** are identical.
- Atoms of **different elements** are different: they have different masses.
- Atoms of different elements join in **fixed, whole-number ratios** to make **compounds**.
- In a chemical reaction the atoms are **rearranged**. No atoms appear or disappear.

## What the model explains
| Observation | Dalton's explanation |
| there are different elements | there is a different kind of atom for each one |
| a compound always has the same composition | its atoms are always joined in the same ratio |
| mass is conserved in a reaction | the same atoms are there before and after |

## How good is the model?
A model is a simple picture that helps us explain and predict. Dalton's model works well for everything in KS3. Later scientists found that atoms are not solid spheres: they contain even smaller particles called protons, neutrons and electrons. You will meet that model at IGCSE. Scientists change a model when new evidence does not fit it.
>>>
W: Atoms are not the same as cells. Cells are made of many billions of atoms.
W: A model is not wrong because it is simple. It is useful as long as it explains the evidence you have.
M: Define atom :: the **smallest particle** of an **element** that can exist
M: Dalton's picture of an atom :: a tiny **solid sphere** that **cannot be divided**
M: Atoms of one element in Dalton's model :: all **identical**
M: What happens to atoms in a chemical reaction :: they are **rearranged**; none are **made or destroyed**
C: [1] In Dalton's model, what is an atom like? | a ball of positive charge with electrons in it | *a tiny solid sphere | a nucleus with electrons around it | a tiny cube | Dalton pictured atoms as solid spheres that could not be divided.
C: [1] According to Dalton, what happens to atoms during a chemical reaction? | They are destroyed. | They change into other atoms. | *They are rearranged. | They get lighter. | The atoms are the same before and after: only the way they are joined changes.
C: [1] Why do scientists change a scientific model? | Models go out of fashion. | *New evidence does not fit the old model. | A famous scientist asks them to. | Models must change every hundred years. | A model has to explain the evidence. When it cannot, it is improved or replaced.

S: An element contains only one type of atom. A compound contains two or more different elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio, and it behaves quite differently from the elements in it. A mixture is just substances jumbled together without being joined.
<<<
## The key words
!def Element | A substance made of only one type of atom. It cannot be broken down into anything simpler.

!def Molecule | Two or more atoms chemically joined together.

!def Compound | A substance made of two or more different elements chemically joined in fixed proportions.

!def Mixture | Two or more substances that are together but not chemically joined.

There are about 118 elements. They are all listed in the Periodic Table.

## Molecules of elements and molecules of compounds
| Substance | Formula | Element or compound? | Why |
| oxygen | O₂ | element | both atoms are the same type |
| nitrogen | N₂ | element | both atoms are the same type |
| water | H₂O | compound | hydrogen and oxygen atoms joined |
| carbon dioxide | CO₂ | compound | carbon and oxygen atoms joined |

Not all elements are made of molecules. Helium exists as single atoms. Metals such as iron are huge numbers of atoms joined in a giant structure.

## A compound is not like its elements
- **Sodium** is a soft metal that reacts violently with water. **Chlorine** is a poisonous green gas. Joined together they make **sodium chloride**: the salt you put on food.
- **Iron** is magnetic and **sulfur** is a yellow powder. Heat them together and they react to form **iron sulfide**, a grey solid that is not magnetic.

| Separating | needs a chemical reaction | physical methods, such as filtering |

## Chemical formulae
A formula shows the elements in a substance and the number of atoms of each.

- A **small number after a symbol** tells you how many of that atom there are. No number means one.

== k3-2-4
S: In any change of state or chemical reaction, the total mass at the end is the same as the total mass at the start. No atoms are made or destroyed. If the mass seems to change, a gas has escaped or been taken in from the air.
<<<
## The law
!def Conservation of mass | In a chemical reaction or a change of state, the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.

It works because the atoms are only **rearranged**. The same atoms, with the same masses, are there before and after.

## Using it
If you know all the masses but one, you can work out the missing one.

- 24 g of magnesium reacts with 16 g of oxygen. Mass of magnesium oxide made = 24 + 16 = **40 g**.
- 100 g of calcium carbonate breaks down to give 56 g of calcium oxide. Mass of carbon dioxide = 100 − 56 = **44 g**.

## Changes of state and dissolving
- 10 g of ice melts to give 10 g of water. The particles are the same; only their arrangement has changed.
- 5 g of salt dissolved in 100 g of water gives 105 g of solution. The salt particles are still there, spread among the water particles.

## When the mass seems to change
| What you see | What really happened |
| the mass **goes down**, for example when chalk fizzes in acid in an open flask | a **gas escaped** into the air and took its mass with it |
| the mass **goes up**, for example when magnesium burns | the metal joined with **oxygen from the air**, which had mass that was not weighed at the start |

!key In a **sealed container** nothing can get in or out, so the reading on the balance does not change.
>>>
W: Gases have mass. A reaction that gives off a gas has not destroyed any matter.
W: When a solid dissolves it has not disappeared. Its mass is still in the solution.
M: State the law of conservation of mass :: total mass of **products** = total mass of **reactants**
M: Why mass is conserved :: atoms are only **rearranged**; **none are made or destroyed**
M: Why the mass falls when a gas is given off in an open flask :: the **gas escapes** into the air
M: Why magnesium gains mass when it burns :: it **combines with oxygen** from the air
