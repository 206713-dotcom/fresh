== ci-5-1
S: An exothermic reaction gives out thermal energy and the surroundings get hotter. An endothermic reaction takes in thermal energy and the surroundings get colder. The difference comes from bonds: breaking bonds takes energy in, and making bonds gives energy out.
<<<
## Two kinds of reaction
| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
| Thermal energy | **transferred to** the surroundings | **taken in from** the surroundings |
| Temperature of the surroundings | **increases** | **decreases** |
| Enthalpy change, ΔH | **negative** | **positive** |
| Examples | combustion, neutralisation, respiration, most oxidation reactions | thermal decomposition, photosynthesis, dissolving ammonium nitrate |

The "surroundings" include the solution, the container, the thermometer and the air. A thermometer in the mixture shows a rise for an exothermic reaction and a fall for an endothermic one.

## Enthalpy change and activation energy
!def Enthalpy change, ΔH | The transfer of thermal energy during a reaction. ΔH is negative for exothermic reactions and positive for endothermic reactions.

!def Activation energy, Ea | The minimum energy that colliding particles must have to react.

Every reaction, even an exothermic one, needs some energy to get started, because bonds in the reactants must first be broken. A match supplies the activation energy to light a fuel.

## Reaction pathway diagrams
A reaction pathway diagram (energy level diagram) shows energy on the vertical axis and the progress of the reaction along the horizontal axis.

| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
| Level of the products | **lower** than the reactants | **higher** than the reactants |
| ΔH arrow | points **down**, from reactants to products | points **up**, from reactants to products |
| Energy has been | given out | taken in |

### Drawing and labelling one
- Draw a horizontal line for the **reactants** and label it with their formulae.
- Draw a horizontal line for the **products**: lower for exothermic, higher for endothermic.
- Join them with a curve that rises to a peak and falls: the "hump".
- **Activation energy, $E_a$:** a vertical arrow from the **reactants level up to the top of the hump**.
- **Enthalpy change, ΔH:** a vertical arrow from the **reactants level to the products level**.
- Label the axes: energy, and progress of reaction.

A catalyst gives a lower hump. The levels of the reactants and products do not change, so ΔH is the same.

## Bonds and energy
!key **Bond breaking is endothermic. Bond making is exothermic.**

In a reaction, bonds in the reactants are broken first, and then new bonds are made to form the products.

- If **more energy is released making bonds** than is taken in breaking bonds, the reaction is **exothermic**.
- If **more energy is taken in breaking bonds** than is released making bonds, the reaction is **endothermic**.

## Calculating ΔH from bond energies
The bond energy is the energy needed to break one mole of a particular bond, in kJ/mol. The same amount is released when the bond is made.

$$\Delta H = \text{energy taken in breaking bonds} - \text{energy released making bonds}$$

### Method
- Draw the displayed formula of every molecule, so that you can see every bond.
- Add up the energy for all the bonds **broken** (reactants). Remember the numbers in front of the formulae.
- Add up the energy for all the bonds **made** (products).
- Subtract: broken − made. Keep the sign.

### Worked example 1
$$\mathrm{H_2 + Cl_2 \rightarrow 2HCl}$$

Bond energies in kJ/mol: H–H 436, Cl–Cl 242, H–Cl 431.

Bonds broken: one H–H and one Cl–Cl.

$$436 + 242 = 678\ \mathrm{kJ}$$

Bonds made: two H–Cl.

$$2 \times 431 = 862\ \mathrm{kJ}$$

$$\Delta H = 678 - 862 = -184\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$

The sign is negative, so the reaction is exothermic.

### Worked example 2
$$\mathrm{CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O}$$

Bond energies in kJ/mol: C–H 412, O=O 496, C=O 805, O–H 463.

Bonds broken: four C–H and two O=O.

$$(4 \times 412) + (2 \times 496) = 1648 + 992 = 2640\ \mathrm{kJ}$$

Bonds made: two C=O (in one CO₂) and four O–H (in two H₂O).

$$(2 \times 805) + (4 \times 463) = 1610 + 1852 = 3462\ \mathrm{kJ}$$

$$\Delta H = 2640 - 3462 = -822\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$

== ci-6-1
S: In a physical change no new substance is made: only the form or state changes, and it is usually easy to reverse. In a chemical change one or more new substances are made, there is usually an energy change, and it is usually hard to reverse.
<<<
## Physical changes
A physical change alters the form of a substance but not what it is.

- **Changes of state:** melting, boiling, evaporating, condensing, freezing.
- **Dissolving:** sugar or salt dissolving in water.
- **Changing shape:** crushing a solid into a powder; bending a metal.
- **Separating a mixture:** filtration, distillation.

After a physical change, the substance has the same formula and the same chemical properties.

## Chemical changes
A chemical change (a chemical reaction) produces one or more **new substances**.

- burning magnesium to make magnesium oxide
- rusting of iron
- an acid reacting with a carbonate
- electrolysis
- thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate
- cooking an egg

## The differences
| | Physical change | Chemical change |
| New substance formed? | **no** | **yes** |
| Easily reversed? | usually **yes** | usually **no** |
| Energy change | usually small | often large: heat or light given out or taken in |
| Properties afterwards | the same chemical properties | different properties |
| Total mass | unchanged | unchanged |

## Signs that a chemical change has happened
- a **colour change**
- a **gas** is given off: bubbles (effervescence)
- a **precipitate** forms: a solid appears when two solutions are mixed
- a **temperature change**: the mixture gets hot or cold
- light or sound is produced.

One sign alone is not proof. Water boiling gives off a gas, but it is a physical change, because the gas is still water.

### Hard cases
| Change | Type | Why |
| ice melting | physical | still water, H₂O; easily reversed by cooling |
| salt dissolving in water | physical | no new substance; evaporate the water to get the salt back |
| a candle burning | chemical | the wax reacts with oxygen to make carbon dioxide and water |
| candle wax melting | physical | still wax |
| iron rusting | chemical | a new substance, hydrated iron(III) oxide, is formed |
>>>

== ci-6-2
S: A reaction happens when particles collide with enough energy. Anything that makes those successful collisions more frequent makes the reaction faster: a higher concentration or pressure, a bigger surface area, a higher temperature or a catalyst. You measure the rate by timing how fast a gas is made or mass is lost.
<<<
## What changes the rate of a reaction
| Change | Effect on the rate |
| increasing the **concentration** of a solution | faster |
| increasing the **pressure** of a gas | faster |
| increasing the **surface area** of a solid (using a powder, not lumps) | faster |
| increasing the **temperature** | faster |
| adding a **catalyst** (or an enzyme) | faster |

!def Catalyst | A substance that increases the rate of a reaction and is unchanged at the end of the reaction.

Enzymes are biological catalysts.

## Collision theory
For particles to react they must **collide**, and they must collide with at least the **activation energy, $E_a$**: the minimum energy that colliding particles must have to react. A collision that leads to a reaction is a successful collision.

!key The rate of a reaction depends on the **frequency of successful collisions**.

Two things control this:

- the **number of particles per unit volume** and how fast they move, which set the **frequency of collisions**
- the **kinetic energy** of the particles, compared with the **activation energy**, which sets how many of the collisions are successful.

## Explaining each factor
| Factor | Explanation in terms of collision theory |
| **higher concentration** of a solution | **more particles per unit volume**, so collisions are **more frequent**, so there are more successful collisions per second |
| **higher pressure** of a gas | the particles are pushed closer together: **more particles per unit volume**, so collisions are **more frequent** |
| **larger surface area** of a solid | **more particles of the solid are exposed** at the surface, so collisions with the other reactant are **more frequent** |
| **higher temperature** | particles have **more kinetic energy** and move faster, so they **collide more frequently**; and **a greater proportion of particles have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy**, so more collisions are successful |
| **catalyst** | provides a different pathway with a **lower activation energy**, so a greater proportion of collisions are successful |

!warn For temperature, give both reasons: more frequent collisions, and more of the collisions have enough energy. The second reason matters more.

!exam Write "more frequent collisions" or "more collisions per second". "More collisions" alone does not earn the mark.

## Measuring the rate
$$\text{rate} = \frac{\text{change in amount of reactant or product}}{\text{time taken}}$$

### Method 1: the volume of gas formed
Example: calcium carbonate (marble chips) and dilute hydrochloric acid.

$$\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow CaCl_2(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g)}$$

- Put the acid in a conical flask. Add the marble chips, fit the bung at once and start the stop-watch.
- A delivery tube leads to a **gas syringe**, or to an upturned measuring cylinder full of water.
- Record the volume of gas at regular intervals, for example every 30 seconds, until it stops changing.

### Method 2: the change in mass
- Stand the flask of acid on a **balance**. Add the marble chips and put a loose plug of **cotton wool** in the neck.
- The carbon dioxide escapes, so the mass falls. Record the mass at regular intervals.
- The cotton wool lets the gas out but stops acid spray from escaping.

### Comparing the two methods
| | Gas syringe | Change in mass |
| Advantages | works for any gas, including hydrogen; measures the product directly | no gas-tight seal needed; easy to read |
| Disadvantages | some gas escapes before the bung is fitted; the syringe can stick; it has a limited volume | not suitable when the gas has a very low density, such as **hydrogen**: the change in mass is too small to measure well |

Collecting the gas over water is not suitable for a gas that is soluble in water.

## Interpreting rate graphs
A graph of volume of gas (or loss of mass) against time has a typical shape.

| Part of the curve | Meaning |
| steepest at the start | the rate is fastest: the concentration of the reactants is highest |
| gets less steep | the rate decreases: the reactants are being used up, so collisions are less frequent |
| horizontal (flat) | the reaction has **finished**: one reactant has been used up |

- **The steeper the line, the faster the rate.**
- A faster reaction (warmer, more concentrated, powdered solid, or with a catalyst) gives a **steeper curve that levels off sooner**.
- If the amounts of reactants are the same, the curve levels off at the **same final volume**.
- If the amount of the limiting reactant is halved, the final volume is halved.

The rate at any moment is the gradient of the curve at that point.

The average rate up to a given time:

$$\text{average rate} = \frac{\text{volume of gas formed}}{\text{time}}$$

For example, 48 cm³ of gas in 120 s is 48 ÷ 120 = 0.40 cm³/s.

== ci-6-3
S: Some reactions go both ways. In a closed system a reversible reaction reaches equilibrium, where the forward and reverse reactions happen at the same rate and the amounts stop changing. Changing the temperature, pressure or concentration shifts the equilibrium, and industry chooses conditions that balance yield, rate and cost.
<<<
## Reversible reactions
Some reactions can be reversed by changing the conditions. A reversible reaction is shown by the symbol **⇌**.

### Hydrated and anhydrous salts
| | Hydrated | Anhydrous |
| **copper(II) sulfate** | **blue** crystals, CuSO₄·5H₂O | **white** powder, CuSO₄ |
| **cobalt(II) chloride** | **pink** crystals, CoCl₂·6H₂O | **blue** solid, CoCl₂ |

$$\mathrm{CuSO_4{\cdot}5H_2O(s) \rightleftharpoons CuSO_4(s) + 5H_2O(g)}$$

$$\mathrm{CoCl_2{\cdot}6H_2O(s) \rightleftharpoons CoCl_2(s) + 6H_2O(g)}$$

- **Heating** the hydrated compound drives off the water: blue copper(II) sulfate turns white; pink cobalt(II) chloride turns blue. This direction is endothermic.
- **Adding water** to the anhydrous compound reverses it: white turns blue; blue turns pink. This direction is exothermic: the solid gets hot.

These two colour changes are the chemical tests for water.

## Equilibrium
In a **closed system**, where nothing can enter or leave, a reversible reaction does not go to completion. It reaches a state of **equilibrium**.

!key At equilibrium, the **rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction**, and the **concentrations of reactants and products are no longer changing**.

Both reactions are still going on, which is why it is called a dynamic equilibrium. The concentrations are constant, but they are not necessarily equal.

## Changing the conditions
If a condition is changed, the position of equilibrium moves in the direction that **opposes the change**.

| Change | The equilibrium shifts… |
| **increase the temperature** | in the **endothermic** direction |
| **decrease the temperature** | in the **exothermic** direction |
| **increase the pressure** (gases) | to the side with **fewer gas molecules** |
| **decrease the pressure** | to the side with **more gas molecules** |
| **increase the concentration of a reactant** | to the **right**: more products |
| **increase the concentration of a product** | to the **left**: more reactants |
| **remove a product** | to the **right** |
| **add a catalyst** | **no shift**. Both reactions speed up equally, so equilibrium is reached faster |

To use the pressure rule, count the moles of **gas** on each side of the equation. If they are equal, pressure has no effect on the position of equilibrium.

## The Haber process: making ammonia
$$\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g)}$$

The forward reaction is exothermic.

| | |
| **Source of nitrogen** | the **air** |
| **Source of hydrogen** | **methane** (natural gas), reacted with steam |
| **Temperature** | **450 °C** |
| **Pressure** | **20 000 kPa (200 atm)** |
| **Catalyst** | **iron** |

The mixture leaving the reactor is cooled. The ammonia condenses to a liquid and is removed, and the unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled.

### Why these conditions
- **Temperature, 450 °C.** The forward reaction is exothermic, so a **low temperature gives a higher yield** of ammonia. But at a low temperature the **rate is too slow**. 450 °C is a **compromise**: a reasonable yield at an acceptable rate.
- **Pressure, 200 atm.** There are 4 moles of gas on the left and 2 on the right, so a **high pressure shifts the equilibrium to the right** and gives a higher yield. It also increases the rate. But very high pressures need thick, strong pipes and powerful pumps, which are **expensive**, and they are **dangerous**. 200 atm is a compromise between yield, **cost and safety**.
- **Iron catalyst.** It increases the **rate**, so equilibrium is reached sooner, at a lower temperature than would otherwise be needed. It does **not change the yield**.

## The Contact process: making sulfur trioxide for sulfuric acid
$$\mathrm{2SO_2(g) + O_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2SO_3(g)}$$

The forward reaction is exothermic.

| | |
| **Source of sulfur dioxide** | **burning sulfur**, or **roasting sulfide ores** |
| **Source of oxygen** | the **air** |
| **Temperature** | **450 °C** |
| **Pressure** | **200 kPa (2 atm)** |
| **Catalyst** | **vanadium(V) oxide**, V₂O₅ |

### Why these conditions
- **Temperature, 450 °C.** The forward reaction is exothermic, so a lower temperature would give a higher yield but a rate that is too slow. 450 °C is a compromise.
- **Pressure, 2 atm.** There are 3 moles of gas on the left and 2 on the right, so a high pressure would increase the yield. But the yield is **already very high at low pressure**, so the extra cost and danger of high pressure are not worth it. A pressure just above atmospheric is enough to push the gases through the plant.
- **Vanadium(V) oxide catalyst.** It increases the rate without changing the yield.

## The solubility rules
| Salts | Soluble? |
| **sodium, potassium and ammonium** salts | all **soluble** |
| **nitrates** | all **soluble** |
| **chlorides** | soluble, **except lead and silver** |
| **sulfates** | soluble, **except barium, calcium and lead** |
| **carbonates** | **insoluble**, except sodium, potassium and ammonium |
| **hydroxides** | **insoluble**, except sodium, potassium, ammonium and calcium (partially) |

## Choosing a method
| The salt is… | Reactants | Method |
| soluble, and a **sodium, potassium or ammonium** salt | acid + **alkali** | **titration** |
| soluble, any other metal | acid + **excess** insoluble metal, base or carbonate | **excess solid**, then filter |
| **insoluble** | two soluble salts in solution | **precipitation** |

Which acid: hydrochloric acid gives chlorides, sulfuric acid gives sulfates, nitric acid gives nitrates.

## Method 1: acid and excess insoluble solid
Use this for soluble salts such as copper(II) sulfate, zinc chloride or magnesium sulfate. The solid can be an **excess of the metal**, an **insoluble base** (the metal oxide) or an **insoluble carbonate**.

Example: copper(II) sulfate from copper(II) oxide and dilute sulfuric acid.

$$\mathrm{CuO(s) + H_2SO_4(aq) \rightarrow CuSO_4(aq) + H_2O(l)}$$

- **Warm** the dilute acid in a beaker.
- Add the solid, a little at a time, **with stirring, until it is in excess**: no more dissolves and some solid is left over. This makes sure that **all the acid has reacted**.
- **Filter** to remove the excess solid. The filtrate is the salt solution.
- **Heat** the filtrate in an evaporating basin to evaporate some of the water, until the solution is **saturated** (the crystallisation point: crystals form on a cold glass rod dipped in).
- Leave it to **cool**, so that **crystals** form.
- **Filter** off the crystals, **wash** them with a little cold distilled water and **dry** them between filter papers or in a warm oven.

!warn Do not evaporate to dryness if the salt is hydrated. Strong heating would drive off the water of crystallisation.

A metal can only be used if it is reactive enough but not dangerously so: magnesium, zinc and iron are suitable. Copper does not react with dilute acids, and sodium and potassium are too reactive.

## Method 2: titration
Use this when both reactants are soluble, as for sodium, potassium and ammonium salts. There is no solid to tell you when the acid has all reacted, so an indicator is needed.

Example: sodium chloride from sodium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid.

- Use a **volumetric pipette** to put 25.0 cm³ of the alkali into a conical flask.
- Add a few drops of **indicator**, such as thymolphthalein or methyl orange.
- Add the acid from a **burette**, swirling, until the indicator **just changes colour**. This is the end-point. Record the volume of acid added.
- **Repeat without the indicator**, adding exactly the same volume of acid to a fresh 25.0 cm³ of alkali. The solution is now pure salt and water.
- Evaporate, crystallise, filter and dry as in method 1.

## Method 3: precipitation
Use this for **insoluble** salts. Mix two solutions: one containing the positive ion you want, the other containing the negative ion.

Example: lead(II) iodide from aqueous lead(II) nitrate and aqueous potassium iodide.

$$\mathrm{Pb(NO_3)_2(aq) + 2KI(aq) \rightarrow PbI_2(s) + 2KNO_3(aq)}$$

$$\mathrm{Pb^{2+}(aq) + 2I^-(aq) \rightarrow PbI_2(s)}$$

- **Mix** the two solutions. A **precipitate** of the insoluble salt forms.
- **Filter**. The salt is the **residue** on the filter paper.
- **Wash** the residue with distilled water, to remove the soluble impurities.
- **Dry** it in a warm oven or between filter papers.

Other examples: barium sulfate from barium nitrate and sodium sulfate; silver chloride from silver nitrate and sodium chloride.

+ add copper(II) oxide to warm acid until it is in excess (no more dissolves) :: excess|no more
+ filter to remove the unreacted copper(II) oxide :: filter
+ heat the filtrate to evaporate some of the water (until saturated, crystallisation point) :: evaporat|heat|saturat
+ leave to cool so that crystals form :: cool|crystallis

A: Add copper(II) oxide to warm dilute sulfuric acid, with stirring, until it is in excess. Filter to remove the unreacted copper(II) oxide. Heat the filtrate to evaporate some of the water until the solution is saturated. Leave it to cool so that crystals form. Filter off the crystals and dry them between filter papers.

A: Mix aqueous barium nitrate with aqueous sodium sulfate. A white precipitate of barium sulfate forms. Filter to collect the precipitate, wash the residue with distilled water and dry it in a warm oven.
