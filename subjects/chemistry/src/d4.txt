## Atomic and ionic radius
### Atomic radius decreases from Na to Ar
- The **nuclear charge increases**.
- Electrons are added to the **same shell**, so **shielding is similar**.
- The outer electrons are attracted more strongly.

### Ionic radius
| Ions | Electrons | Trend |
| Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, Si⁴⁺ | 2,8 | **decreases**: the same number of electrons is attracted by an increasing nuclear charge |
| P³⁻, S²⁻, Cl⁻ | 2,8,8 | much **larger** than the cations (one more shell); then **decreases** from P³⁻ to Cl⁻ as nuclear charge increases |

## Melting point
| Element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl | Ar |
| Formula | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P₄ | S₈ | Cl₂ | Ar |
| Melting point / °C | 98 | 650 | 660 | 1414 | 44 | 115 | −101 | −189 |
| Structure | giant metallic | giant metallic | giant metallic | giant molecular | simple molecular | simple molecular | simple molecular | simple atomic |

### Na to Al: increases
The metallic bonding becomes stronger: the ions have a **greater charge** (1+, 2+, 3+) and a **smaller radius**, and there are **more delocalised electrons** per atom. The attraction between the ions and the delocalised electrons increases.

### Si: the highest
Silicon has a **giant molecular** structure. **Many strong covalent bonds** must be broken, which needs a great deal of energy.

### P to Ar: low
These are **simple molecular**. Only **weak instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces** between the molecules have to be overcome.

The order is **S₈ > P₄ > Cl₂ > Ar**, because the id–id forces are stronger when the molecule has **more electrons**: S₈ has 128, P₄ has 60, Cl₂ has 34 and Ar has 18.

## Electrical conductivity
| Elements | Conductivity | Explanation |
| **Na, Mg, Al** | **high**, increasing from Na to Al | **delocalised electrons** are free to move; the number of delocalised electrons per atom increases (1, 2, 3) |
| **Si** | low: a **semiconductor** | giant molecular: no delocalised electrons in the pure solid at room temperature |
| **P, S, Cl, Ar** | **none** | simple molecular (or atoms): the electrons are held in covalent bonds or in atoms; no mobile charged particles |

## Using periodicity to predict
Elements in the same group have the **same number of outer-shell electrons**, so they have **similar chemical properties**, with gradual **trends** down the group.

| Property | Across a period | Down a group |
| atomic radius | decreases | increases |
| first ionisation energy | increases (in general) | decreases |
| electronegativity | increases | decreases |
| metallic character | decreases | increases |
| nature of oxide | basic → amphoteric → acidic | more basic |
| bonding in chloride | ionic → covalent | more ionic |

## Predicting from the group
| Group | Outer electrons | Typical ion | Oxide | Chloride |
| 1 | 1 | 1+ | basic; ionic; M₂O | ionic; MCl; neutral in water |

| 14 | 4 | | acidic or amphoteric; MO₂ | covalent; MCl₄; hydrolysed |
| 15 | 5 | 3− | acidic | covalent; hydrolysed |

| 17 | 7 | 1− | acidic | |

**Example.** Predict the properties of rubidium, Rb, in Group 1, Period 5.

- It is a soft metal with a low melting point: lower than that of potassium.
- It forms Rb⁺ ions.
- It reacts **more violently** with water than potassium: $\mathrm{2Rb + 2H_2O \rightarrow 2RbOH + H_2}$.
- Its oxide, Rb₂O, is basic and its hydroxide is a strong alkali.
- Its chloride, RbCl, is an ionic solid that dissolves in water to give a neutral solution.

**Example.** Germanium, Ge, is below silicon in Group 14.

- Its oxide is GeO₂ and its chloride is GeCl₄.
- GeCl₄ is a covalent liquid that is hydrolysed by water to give HCl fumes.

## Identifying an unknown element
Work from the evidence to the group, then to the period.

| Evidence | Deduction |
| conducts electricity when solid | a metal: left or centre of the table |

| oxide is amphoteric | near the metal / non-metal boundary, such as Al |
| oxide is acidic; chloride fumes in moist air | a non-metal on the right |
| very high melting point; does not conduct | giant molecular, such as Si |
| formula of the chloride is XCl₂ | Group 2 (2 outer electrons) |
| large jump after the n-th ionisation energy | n outer electrons |

## Reactions of the elements
Reactivity **increases down the group**: the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded, so they are lost more easily (the ionisation energies decrease).

### With oxygen
$$\mathrm{2M + O_2 \rightarrow 2MO}$$

| Metal | Flame |
| Mg | bright white |
| Ca | brick-red (orange-red) |
| Sr | red |
| Ba | green |

All give a white solid oxide.

### With water
| Metal | Reaction |
| Mg | very slow with cold water. Burns in **steam**: $\mathrm{Mg + H_2O(g) \rightarrow MgO + H_2}$ |
| Ca | steady with cold water; the solution goes cloudy |
| Sr | fast |
| Ba | vigorous |

$$\mathrm{M + 2H_2O \rightarrow M(OH)_2 + H_2}$$

### With dilute acids
$$\mathrm{M + 2HCl \rightarrow MCl_2 + H_2}$$

$$\mathrm{M + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow MSO_4 + H_2}$$

All react vigorously with hydrochloric acid. With sulfuric acid, magnesium reacts quickly, but calcium, strontium and barium form an **insoluble sulfate** that coats the metal and slows or stops the reaction.

## Reactions of the oxides, hydroxides and carbonates
### With water
| Compound | Reaction with water |
| oxides | $\mathrm{MO + H_2O \rightarrow M(OH)_2}$. MgO reacts only slightly (pH 9–10). CaO reacts vigorously and exothermically (pH 12). The solutions are more alkaline down the group, because the hydroxides are more soluble. |
| hydroxides | dissolve to different extents, releasing OH⁻ ions: $\mathrm{M(OH)_2(s) \rightarrow M^{2+}(aq) + 2OH^-(aq)}$ |
| carbonates | insoluble: no reaction |

### With dilute acids
The oxides and hydroxides are **bases**: they neutralise acids. The carbonates also give carbon dioxide.

$$\mathrm{MO + 2HCl \rightarrow MCl_2 + H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{M(OH)_2 + 2HCl \rightarrow MCl_2 + 2H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{MCO_3 + 2HCl \rightarrow MCl_2 + H_2O + CO_2}$$

$$\mathrm{MO + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow MSO_4 + H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{MCO_3 + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow MSO_4 + H_2O + CO_2}$$

With sulfuric acid, the calcium, strontium and barium compounds become coated with insoluble sulfate, so the reaction is slow and incomplete.

## Thermal decomposition
### Carbonates
$$\mathrm{MCO_3 \rightarrow MO + CO_2}$$

### Nitrates
$$\mathrm{2M(NO_3)_2 \rightarrow 2MO + 4NO_2 + O_2}$$

Brown fumes of nitrogen dioxide are seen, and the oxygen relights a glowing splint.

### Trend
**Thermal stability increases down the group**: a higher temperature is needed to decompose the carbonates and nitrates of the lower elements.

| Carbonate | MgCO₃ | CaCO₃ | SrCO₃ | BaCO₃ |
| Decomposes at about / °C | 540 | 900 | 1280 | 1360 |

## Solubility
| Compounds | Trend down the group | Extremes |
| **hydroxides** | solubility **increases** | Mg(OH)₂ is sparingly soluble; Ba(OH)₂ is soluble, giving a strongly alkaline solution |
| **sulfates** | solubility **decreases** | MgSO₄ is soluble; BaSO₄ is insoluble |

## Predicting from the trends
Radium is below barium. It should react **more vigorously** with water than barium; its hydroxide should be **more soluble**; its sulfate should be **less soluble**; and its carbonate should need a **higher temperature** to decompose.

Beryllium, above magnesium, does not react with water, and its oxide is amphoteric.

## Uses
Calcium hydroxide (slaked lime) and calcium carbonate (powdered limestone) are bases, used in agriculture to **neutralise acidic soil**. Magnesium hydroxide is used in indigestion remedies to neutralise excess stomach acid.
