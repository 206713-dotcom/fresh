## Measuring time, temperature and mass
| Quantity | Apparatus | Typical precision | Unit |
| **time** | **stop-watch** (or stop-clock) | to the nearest second, or 0.01 s | s, min |
| **temperature** | **thermometer** | to 1 °C, or 0.5 °C or better | °C |
| **mass** | **balance** | to 0.01 g, or 0.001 g | g |

## Measuring volume
| Apparatus | Used for | Accuracy |
| **measuring cylinder** | an approximate volume of liquid | low: to about 1 cm³ |
| **volumetric pipette** | one **fixed** volume of liquid, such as 25.0 cm³, very accurately | high |
| **burette** | **variable** volumes of liquid, added a little at a time; read to 0.05 cm³ | high |
| **gas syringe** | the volume of a gas | to about 1 cm³ |

Reading a volume: put your eye level with the liquid surface and read the **bottom of the meniscus**. A burette is numbered downwards from 0 at the top, so the volume delivered is the final reading minus the initial reading.

## Choosing apparatus: advantages and disadvantages
| Choice | Advantage | Disadvantage |
| measuring cylinder | quick; measures any volume | not accurate |
| volumetric pipette | very accurate | only one fixed volume; slower |
| burette | accurate; delivers any volume; can add drop by drop | slower to set up; must be read carefully |
| gas syringe | measures the gas directly; works for gases that dissolve in water | limited volume; the plunger can stick; gas escapes before the bung is in |
| collecting gas over water in a measuring cylinder | simple, cheap | no use for gases that are soluble in water; less precise |

To improve an experiment: use more precise apparatus, repeat and take a mean, insulate and add a lid where heat is being measured, and stir so that the temperature or concentration is even.

## Six words to use exactly
!def Solvent | A substance that dissolves a solute.

!def Solute | A substance that is dissolved in a solvent.

!def Solution | A mixture of one or more solutes dissolved in a solvent.

!def Saturated solution | A solution containing the maximum concentration of a solute dissolved in the solvent at a specified temperature.

!def Residue | A substance that remains after evaporation, distillation, filtration or any similar process.

!def Filtrate | A liquid or solution that has passed through a filter.

When salt dissolves in water: the salt is the solute, the water is the solvent, and the salt water is the solution.

When sandy salt water is filtered: the sand left on the filter paper is the residue, and the salt solution that runs through is the filtrate.

Solubility usually increases with temperature, which is why the definition of a saturated solution has to state the temperature. A hot saturated solution forms crystals as it cools.

== ci-12-2
S: A titration finds exactly how much of one solution reacts with a fixed volume of another. One solution goes in a conical flask from a pipette, the other is added from a burette, and an indicator shows the end-point: the moment the reaction is just complete.
<<<
## The apparatus
| Apparatus | Job |
| **volumetric pipette** (with a pipette filler) | measures an accurate, fixed volume of one solution, usually 25.0 cm³, into the flask |
| **burette** | adds the other solution, and measures how much has been added |
| **conical flask** | holds the mixture; its sloping sides let you swirl without spilling |
| **indicator** | changes colour at the end-point |
| **white tile** | under the flask, so the colour change is easy to see |

## Carrying out an acid–base titration
- Rinse the burette with the solution it will hold, then fill it. Make sure the tip is full and free of air bubbles. **Record the initial reading.**
- Use the **volumetric pipette** to transfer 25.0 cm³ of the other solution into a clean **conical flask**.
- Add a **few drops of indicator**.
- Stand the flask on a **white tile** under the burette.
- Add the solution from the burette, **swirling** the flask all the time.
- Near the end-point, add the solution **drop by drop**.
- Stop when the indicator **just changes colour** and the colour stays. **Record the final reading.**
- **Volume added (the titre) = final reading − initial reading.**
- **Repeat** until you have results that agree closely (concordant: within 0.10 cm³), and calculate the mean of those.

The first titration is usually a rough one, to find roughly where the end-point is. It is not included in the mean.

## The end-point
The **end-point** is the point at which the indicator **just changes colour**: the acid and alkali have exactly neutralised each other.

| Indicator | In acid | In alkali | End-point when acid is added to alkali |
| **thymolphthalein** | colourless | blue | blue → **colourless** |
| **methyl orange** | red | yellow | yellow → **orange** (then red) |

Universal indicator is not suitable: it changes gradually through many colours, so it has no sharp end-point.

## Reading the burette
- Read from the **bottom of the meniscus**, with your eye level with it.
- Record every reading to **two decimal places**, ending in 0 or 5: 23.45 cm³.

| | Rough | 1 | 2 |
| final reading / cm³ | 24.10 | 23.65 | 47.20 |
| initial reading / cm³ | 0.00 | 0.10 | 23.65 |
| titre / cm³ | 24.10 | 23.55 | 23.55 |

Mean titre = (23.55 + 23.55) ÷ 2 = 23.55 cm³.

## What titrations are used for
- to find the **concentration** of an acid or an alkali
- to find the exact volumes needed to prepare a **soluble salt** from an acid and an alkali.

== ci-12-3
S: Chromatography separates a mixture of dissolved substances by letting a solvent carry them up a strip of paper. Each substance travels a different distance, so a pure substance gives one spot and a mixture gives several. The Rf value identifies each spot.
<<<
## Paper chromatography
Paper chromatography separates **mixtures of soluble coloured substances**, such as the dyes in an ink or the pigments in a food colouring, using a **suitable solvent**.

### Method
- Draw a **baseline in pencil** about 2 cm from the bottom of a strip of chromatography paper.
- Put a **small, concentrated spot** of the mixture on the baseline. Spots of known substances can be put alongside for comparison.
- Stand the paper in a beaker containing a little solvent. The **solvent level must be below the baseline**.
- Put a lid on the beaker.
- Let the solvent travel up the paper. It carries the substances with it at different speeds.
- Remove the paper **before the solvent reaches the top**, and mark the **solvent front** in pencil at once. Leave it to dry.

The result is called a **chromatogram**.

### Why each step matters
| Step | Reason |
| the baseline is drawn in **pencil** | pencil is **insoluble** in the solvent. Ink would dissolve and run, and mix with the spots |
| the solvent starts **below the baseline** | otherwise the spots would **dissolve into the solvent** in the beaker and not travel up the paper |
| a lid is used | to stop the solvent evaporating |
| the spot is small | so that the separated spots do not smear into each other |

### Why the substances separate
Each substance differs in how **soluble** it is in the solvent and how strongly it is attracted to the paper. A substance that is more soluble in the solvent travels further up the paper.

## Interpreting a chromatogram
| What you see | What it means |
| **one spot** | a **pure** substance |
| **two or more spots** | a **mixture** (impure). The number of spots is the number of substances |
| a spot at the **same height** as a known substance, in the same solvent | the unknown **contains that substance** |
| a spot that stays on the baseline | that substance is **insoluble** in the solvent |

## Colourless substances: locating agents
Chromatography also works for colourless substances, such as amino acids and sugars. Their spots cannot be seen, so after the chromatogram has dried it is sprayed with a **locating agent**.

!def Locating agent | A substance that reacts with colourless spots on a chromatogram to make them visible as coloured spots.

Method: run the chromatogram with a suitable solvent, dry the paper, spray it with the locating agent (warming if needed), then mark the coloured spots.

## $R_f$ values
$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance travelled by the substance}}{\text{distance travelled by the solvent}}$$

- Measure **both distances from the baseline**.
- Measure to the **centre** of the spot, and to the **solvent front**.
- $R_f$ has **no units** and is always **between 0 and 1**.

**Example.** A spot travels 4.2 cm and the solvent front travels 6.0 cm.

$$R_f = \frac{4.2}{6.0} = 0.70$$

A substance always has the same $R_f$ value in the same solvent, under the same conditions. So a substance can be identified by comparing its $R_f$ value with known values.

== ci-12-4
S: A mixture is separated by using a difference between its parts: solubility, particle size or boiling point. Filtration, crystallisation and the two kinds of distillation each exploit one of these. A pure substance melts and boils at one sharp temperature; an impure one does not.
<<<
## Choosing a method
| Mixture | Method | Difference used |
| an **insoluble solid** and a liquid: sand and water | **filtration** | particle size: the solid cannot pass through the filter paper |
| a **dissolved solid** from its solution, to get the **solid**: copper(II) sulfate from its solution | **crystallisation** | solubility falls as the solution cools |
| a **solvent** from a solution, to get the **liquid**: pure water from sea water | **simple distillation** | the solvent has a much lower boiling point than the solute |
| two or more **miscible liquids**: ethanol and water; the fractions of petroleum | **fractional distillation** | the liquids have different boiling points |
| two solids, **one soluble and one insoluble**: salt and sand | **use a suitable solvent**, then filtration, then crystallisation | solubility |
| soluble coloured substances | chromatography | solubility in the solvent |

## Filtration
- Fold a filter paper and put it in a filter funnel over a beaker or flask.
- Pour the mixture in.
- The **liquid passes through**: the **filtrate**.
- The **insoluble solid stays** on the paper: the **residue**.

## Crystallisation
- Pour the solution into an evaporating basin and **heat gently** to evaporate some of the solvent.
- Stop heating when the solution is **saturated**: the crystallisation point. To test, dip in a cold glass rod: crystals form on it.
- Leave the solution to **cool slowly**. Crystals form, because the solid is less soluble in the cold solution.
- **Filter** to collect the crystals, **wash** them with a little cold distilled water, and **dry** them between filter papers or in a warm place.

Slow cooling gives larger crystals.

## Using a suitable solvent
To separate two solids, choose a solvent that dissolves only one of them.

**Salt from sand:**

- add water and stir: the salt dissolves, the sand does not
- filter: sand is the residue, salt solution is the filtrate
- evaporate and crystallise the filtrate to get the salt
- wash and dry the sand.

## Simple distillation
Used to get a pure solvent from a solution.

- Heat the solution in a flask.
- The solvent **boils** and its vapour passes into the **condenser**.
- The condenser is cooled by cold water in its outer jacket. The vapour **condenses** back to a liquid.
- The pure liquid, the **distillate**, drips into a collecting vessel.
- The dissolved solid stays in the flask.

Water enters the condenser at the bottom and leaves at the top, so the jacket stays full. The thermometer bulb sits at the side-arm, where it measures the temperature of the vapour: the boiling point of the liquid being collected.

## Fractional distillation
Used to separate liquids with different boiling points that mix completely.

- A **fractionating column**, packed with glass beads, is fitted between the flask and the condenser.
- Heat the mixture. The liquid with the **lower boiling point** boils first.
- Its vapour rises up the column, passes into the condenser, condenses and is collected.
- Vapour of the liquid with the higher boiling point condenses on the cooler beads in the column and runs back into the flask.
- When the temperature on the thermometer starts to rise, the first liquid has all distilled. Change the collecting vessel to collect the next fraction.

For ethanol (boiling point 78 °C) and water (100 °C), ethanol distils first and the thermometer reads 78 °C.

## Purity
Purity matters in food, drugs and medicines, where an impurity could be harmful.

!key A **pure** substance has a **sharp** melting point and boiling point, at exact, fixed temperatures. An **impure** substance melts and boils **over a range** of temperatures.

An impurity **lowers the melting point** and **raises the boiling point**.

- To **identify** a substance: measure its melting point or boiling point and compare it with data-book values.
- To **assess purity**: see whether it melts sharply at the correct temperature.

Pure water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C. Sea water freezes below 0 °C and boils above 100 °C.

## Tests for gases
| Gas | Test | Result |
| **ammonia, NH₃** | damp **red litmus paper** | turns **blue** |
| **carbon dioxide, CO₂** | bubble through **limewater** | limewater turns **milky** (a white precipitate) |
| **chlorine, Cl₂** | damp **litmus paper** | the paper is **bleached** (turns white) |

| **oxygen, O₂** | **glowing splint** | the splint **relights** |
| **sulfur dioxide, SO₂** | **acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII)** | turns from **purple to colourless** |

Ammonia is the only common alkaline gas.

## Flame tests for metal ions
Dip a clean wire (cleaned in acid) into the solid or solution, then hold it in the edge of a blue, non-luminous Bunsen flame.

| Cation | Flame colour |
| **lithium, Li⁺** | **red** |
| **sodium, Na⁺** | **yellow** |
| **potassium, K⁺** | **lilac** |
| **calcium, Ca²⁺** | **orange-red** |
| **barium, Ba²⁺** | **light green** |
| **copper(II), Cu²⁺** | **blue-green** |
