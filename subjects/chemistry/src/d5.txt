S: The halogens are oxidising agents, and their oxidising power falls down the group: chlorine displaces bromine and iodine from their salts. They react with hydrogen less and less vigorously, and the hydrogen halides they form decompose more and more easily on heating.

## Oxidising agents
A halogen atom gains one electron to form a halide ion: it is **reduced**, so it acts as an **oxidising agent**.

$$\mathrm{X_2 + 2e^- \rightarrow 2X^-}$$

**Oxidising power decreases down the group:** Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂.

**Explanation:** down the group the atoms are **larger**, and the outer shell is **further from the nucleus** and **more shielded**. An incoming electron is attracted less strongly, so the atom gains an electron less easily.

### Displacement reactions
A halogen higher in the group oxidises (displaces) the halide ion of a halogen below it.

| | KCl(aq) | KBr(aq) | KI(aq) |
| **Cl₂(aq)** | no reaction | **orange** solution: Br₂ formed | **brown** solution: I₂ formed |
| **Br₂(aq)** | no reaction | no reaction | **brown** solution: I₂ formed |
| **I₂(aq)** | no reaction | no reaction | no reaction |

$$\mathrm{Cl_2 + 2Br^- \rightarrow 2Cl^- + Br_2}$$

$$\mathrm{Cl_2 + 2I^- \rightarrow 2Cl^- + I_2}$$

$$\mathrm{Br_2 + 2I^- \rightarrow 2Br^- + I_2}$$

Shaking with an organic solvent makes the result clearer: bromine gives an orange upper layer and iodine a purple one.

## Reactions with hydrogen
$$\mathrm{H_2 + X_2 \rightarrow 2HX}$$

| Halogen | Conditions | Reaction |
| chlorine | sunlight, or a spark | **explosive** |
| bromine | about 300 °C, with a platinum catalyst | steady |
| iodine | heat | slow, **reversible** and incomplete: $\mathrm{H_2 + I_2 \rightleftharpoons 2HI}$ |

**Reactivity with hydrogen decreases down the group.**

**Explanation:** the halogens become weaker oxidising agents, and the **H–X bond formed is weaker** down the group, so less energy is released when it forms.

## The hydrogen halides
HCl, HBr and HI are colourless gases that fume in moist air and dissolve in water to give strong acids.

$$\mathrm{2HI \rightarrow H_2 + I_2}$$

**Explanation:** down the group the **halogen atom is larger**, so the **H–X bond is longer and weaker** (the bonding pair is further from the halogen nucleus). **Less energy is needed to break it.**

## Halide ions as reducing agents
A halide ion can lose an electron: it is oxidised, so it acts as a **reducing agent**.

$$\mathrm{2X^- \rightarrow X_2 + 2e^-}$$

**Reducing power increases down the group:** I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻.

**Explanation:** down the group the ions are **larger**, so the outer electrons are **further from the nucleus** and **more shielded**. They are attracted less strongly and are **lost more easily**.

## Test: aqueous silver ions, then aqueous ammonia
Acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous silver nitrate.

$$\mathrm{Ag^+(aq) + X^-(aq) \rightarrow AgX(s)}$$

| Halide | Precipitate | Add dilute NH₃(aq) | Add concentrated NH₃(aq) |
| **chloride** | **white**, AgCl | **dissolves** | dissolves |
| **bromide** | **cream**, AgBr | does not dissolve | **dissolves** |
| **iodide** | **yellow**, AgI | does not dissolve | **does not dissolve** |

The colours are close, so the ammonia step confirms which halide is present. The nitric acid removes ions such as carbonate, which would also give a precipitate with silver ions.

The solubility of the silver halides decreases from AgCl to AgI.

## Chlorine with sodium hydroxide
Both reactions are **disproportionation**: chlorine is **both oxidised and reduced**.

Sodium chlorate(I) is the active ingredient in bleach.

### Hot, concentrated aqueous sodium hydroxide
$$\mathrm{3Cl_2 + 6NaOH \rightarrow 5NaCl + NaClO_3 + 3H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{3Cl_2 + 6OH^- \rightarrow 5Cl^- + ClO_3^- + 3H_2O}$$

| NaClO₃, sodium chlorate(V) | **+5** (oxidised) |

Check: five chlorine atoms each fall by 1, and one rises by 5.

## Chlorine in water purification
Chlorine is added to drinking water and swimming pools to **kill bacteria**.

$$\mathrm{Cl_2 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons HCl + HOCl}$$

The chloric(I) acid partly dissociates:

$$\mathrm{HOCl \rightleftharpoons H^+ + ClO^-}$$

The **active species are HOCl and ClO⁻**. They are oxidising agents that **kill bacteria**.

### Benefits and risks
- Chlorination prevents water-borne diseases such as cholera and typhoid.
- Chlorine is toxic, so the amount added is carefully controlled, and it can react with organic matter in water to form small amounts of chlorinated compounds. The benefit to public health far outweighs these risks.

== ca-12-1
S: Nitrogen gas is unreactive because its triple bond is so strong, yet ammonia is a base and the oxides of nitrogen are serious pollutants. Formed in hot engines, they cause photochemical smog and acid rain, and they catalyse the oxidation of sulfur dioxide to sulfuric acid.
<<<
## Why nitrogen is unreactive
- The N≡N **triple bond is very strong** (945 kJ mol⁻¹), so a great deal of energy is needed to break it: reactions of nitrogen have a very high activation energy.
- The molecule is **non-polar**, so it does not attract polar reagents, electrophiles or nucleophiles.

Nitrogen reacts only under extreme conditions: at very high temperatures, or with a catalyst as in the Haber process.

## Ammonia as a base
Ammonia is a **Brønsted–Lowry base**: the **lone pair** on the nitrogen atom **accepts a proton**.

$$\mathrm{NH_3 + H^+ \rightarrow NH_4^+}$$

In water it is a weak base, only partially ionised:

$$\mathrm{NH_3(aq) + H_2O(l) \rightleftharpoons NH_4^+(aq) + OH^-(aq)}$$

## The ammonium ion
The ammonium ion forms in an **acid–base reaction**: ammonia accepts a proton from an acid.

$$\mathrm{NH_3(g) + HCl(g) \rightarrow NH_4Cl(s)}$$

White smoke of ammonium chloride is seen.

- The nitrogen lone pair forms a **coordinate (dative covalent) bond** to the H⁺ ion.
- The ion has **four N–H bonds**, all the same length and strength.
- With four bonding pairs and no lone pairs, it is **tetrahedral**, with bond angles of **109.5°**.

## Displacing ammonia from ammonium salts
Warming an ammonium salt with a base gives ammonia. This is also an **acid–base reaction**: the ammonium ion **donates a proton** to the hydroxide ion.

$$\mathrm{NH_4Cl + NaOH \rightarrow NaCl + NH_3 + H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{NH_4^+ + OH^- \rightarrow NH_3 + H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{2NH_4Cl + Ca(OH)_2 \rightarrow CaCl_2 + 2NH_3 + 2H_2O}$$

This is the test for the ammonium ion: the gas given off turns damp red litmus paper blue.

## Oxides of nitrogen
### Where they come from
| Source | How |
| **natural: lightning** | the high temperature makes nitrogen and oxygen in the air react |
| **man-made: internal combustion engines** (and furnaces, power stations) | the high temperature in the engine makes nitrogen and oxygen **from the air** react |

$$\mathrm{N_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2NO}$$

$$\mathrm{2NO + O_2 \rightarrow 2NO_2}$$

The nitrogen comes from the air, not from the fuel.

### Catalytic removal
In a **catalytic converter**, the exhaust gases pass over a catalyst of platinum, palladium and rhodium. Nitrogen monoxide is reduced to nitrogen by carbon monoxide.

$$\mathrm{2NO + 2CO \rightarrow N_2 + 2CO_2}$$

## Photochemical smog
Atmospheric **NO and NO₂** react with **unburned hydrocarbons**, in sunlight, to form **peroxyacetyl nitrate, PAN**. PAN is a component of **photochemical smog**. It irritates the eyes and lungs and damages plants.

## Acid rain
### Directly
Nitrogen dioxide reacts with water and oxygen in the atmosphere to form nitric acid.

$$\mathrm{4NO_2 + 2H_2O + O_2 \rightarrow 4HNO_3}$$

### As a catalyst
Nitrogen dioxide catalyses the oxidation of atmospheric sulfur dioxide to sulfur trioxide.

$$\mathrm{SO_2 + NO_2 \rightarrow SO_3 + NO}$$

$$\mathrm{NO + \tfrac{1}{2}O_2 \rightarrow NO_2}$$

The NO₂ is **regenerated**, so it is a catalyst. The sulfur trioxide then forms sulfuric acid.

$$\mathrm{SO_3 + H_2O \rightarrow H_2SO_4}$$

Sulfur dioxide comes from burning fossil fuels that contain sulfur.

### Effects of acid rain
- lowers the pH of lakes and rivers, killing fish and other aquatic life
- damages trees and leaches nutrients from the soil
- corrodes limestone buildings and metal structures.
