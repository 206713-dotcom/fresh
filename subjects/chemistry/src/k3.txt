# KS3 Chemistry, topics 5 and 6: energetics; the Periodic Table.

== k3-5-1
S: Changing state always involves energy. A substance must take energy in to melt or boil, because its particles have to pull away from each other. It gives the same energy back out when it condenses or freezes.
<<<
## Energy in, energy out
| Change of state | Energy | Why |
| melting, boiling, evaporating, subliming | **taken in** from the surroundings | particles need energy to overcome the forces of attraction between them |
| condensing, freezing | **given out** to the surroundings | forces of attraction pull the particles together, and energy is released |

## A heating curve
Heat a solid steadily and plot its temperature against time.

| Part of the graph | What is happening |
| line slopes up | the solid, liquid or gas is getting hotter: its particles move faster |
| line is **flat** | the substance is **changing state**: melting or boiling |

!key While a substance melts or boils its temperature does **not** rise. The energy going in is being used to separate the particles, not to make them move faster.

The first flat section is at the **melting point**. The second is at the **boiling point**.

## Meeting it in real life
- **Sweating cools you.** As sweat evaporates it takes the energy it needs from your skin.
- **Steam scalds badly.** When steam condenses on your skin it gives out energy, as well as being hot.
- **Ice keeps a drink cold.** Melting ice takes in energy from the drink.
- **Frost protection.** Farmers sometimes spray fruit trees with water on a freezing night: as the water freezes it gives out energy.
>>>
W: Heating does not always raise the temperature. During melting and boiling the temperature stays constant.
W: Freezing gives out energy. That sounds odd, but the surroundings have to take energy away for a liquid to freeze.
M: Energy change when a solid melts :: energy is **taken in**
M: Energy change when a gas condenses :: energy is **given out**
M: Why the temperature is constant during melting :: energy is used to **overcome the forces between particles**
M: Flat section on a heating curve :: a **change of state**
M: Why sweating cools the body :: evaporation **takes in energy** from the skin
C: [1] What happens to the temperature of pure ice while it is melting? | It rises. | It falls. | *It stays the same. | It rises, then falls. | The energy supplied separates the particles, so the temperature stays at the melting point.
C: [1] Which change of state gives out energy to the surroundings? | melting | boiling | evaporating | *condensing | When a gas condenses, forces pull the particles together and energy is released.
C: [1] Why does evaporating sweat cool your skin? | Sweat is cold. | Sweat reflects heat. | *Evaporation takes in energy from the skin. | Sweat blocks sunlight. | The particles that escape take energy with them.

== k3-5-2
S: Every chemical reaction involves an energy change. An exothermic reaction transfers energy to the surroundings, so they get hotter. An endothermic reaction takes energy in from the surroundings, so they get colder.
<<<
## Two kinds of reaction
!def Exothermic reaction | A reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings rises.

!def Endothermic reaction | A reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings falls.

| | Exothermic | Endothermic |
| Energy | given **out** | taken **in** |
| Temperature of the surroundings | goes **up** | goes **down** |
| Examples | combustion, neutralisation, rusting, respiration | thermal decomposition, photosynthesis, citric acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate |
| Everyday use | hand warmers, self-heating cans | sports-injury cold packs, sherbet fizzing on your tongue |

A way to remember: **ex**othermic, energy **ex**its. **En**dothermic, energy **en**ters.

## Measuring the change
- Put one reactant in an **insulated cup**, such as a polystyrene cup with a lid.
- Measure the temperature.
- Add the other reactant and stir.
- Record the highest or lowest temperature reached.
- Temperature rose: exothermic. Temperature fell: endothermic.

The insulation matters: it stops energy escaping to the room, or coming in from it, before you take the reading.

## Energy is conserved
Energy is never made or destroyed in a reaction. It is only **transferred** between the chemicals and their surroundings.
>>>
W: The "surroundings" include the solution, the beaker, the thermometer and the air. The thermometer reads the surroundings.
W: Endothermic does not mean no reaction happened. A fall in temperature is a sign of a reaction.
M: Define exothermic :: transfers energy **to the surroundings**; temperature **rises**
M: Define endothermic :: takes in energy **from the surroundings**; temperature **falls**
M: Two exothermic reactions :: **combustion** and **neutralisation**
M: An endothermic reaction :: **thermal decomposition**
M: Why an insulated cup is used :: to **reduce energy transfer** (heat loss) to or from the room
C: [1] Two solutions are mixed and the temperature falls from 20 °C to 14 °C. What kind of reaction is this? | exothermic | *endothermic | combustion | neutralisation | The reaction took in energy from the surroundings, so they became colder.
C: [1] Which of these is exothermic? | photosynthesis | thermal decomposition of copper carbonate | melting ice | *burning methane | Combustion always transfers energy to the surroundings.
C: [1] Why is a polystyrene cup used to measure a temperature change? | It does not react with water. | It is cheap. | *It is a good insulator. | It is see-through. | Insulation keeps the energy in the mixture long enough to measure it.

== k3-6-1
S: In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the known elements in order of the mass of their atoms and lined up elements with similar properties. His masterstroke was to leave gaps for elements nobody had found yet, and to predict what they would be like.
<<<
## Before Mendeleev
By the 1860s about 60 elements were known. Chemists could see that some were alike, such as lithium, sodium and potassium, but nobody had a system that fitted them all.

## What Mendeleev did
- He wrote each element on a card with its properties.
- He put the elements in order of **atomic mass**, lightest first.
- He started a new row each time the properties began to repeat, so that **similar elements lined up** in the same column.
- Where no known element fitted, he **left a gap**.
- Where the order of mass put an element in the wrong family, he **swapped** elements so that the properties matched. He trusted the pattern.

## The predictions
Mendeleev said the gaps were elements still to be discovered, and he predicted their properties from their neighbours.

| | Mendeleev's prediction for "eka-silicon" (1871) | Germanium, discovered 1886 |
| Appearance | grey metal | grey-white metal |
| Density | 5.5 g/cm³ | 5.35 g/cm³ |
| Formula of its oxide | XO₂ | GeO₂ |

Gallium (1875) and scandium (1879) also matched his predictions.

## Why scientists accepted his table
A scientific idea is convincing when it makes **predictions that turn out to be right**. The newly found elements fitted the gaps almost exactly, so other chemists accepted the table.

## The modern Periodic Table
Today the elements are in order of **atomic number**, the number of protons in the atom. This explains the swaps Mendeleev had to make, and it leaves no gaps.
>>>
W: Mendeleev used atomic mass. The modern table uses atomic number.
W: The gaps were not mistakes. They were predictions, and they are the reason the table was believed.
M: How Mendeleev ordered the elements :: in order of **atomic mass**
M: Why he left gaps :: for elements that had **not yet been discovered**
M: How he grouped elements :: elements with **similar properties** in the same **column (group)**
M: Why his table was accepted :: his **predictions** about undiscovered elements were **correct**
M: Order of the modern Periodic Table :: **atomic (proton) number**
C: [1] In what order did Mendeleev arrange the elements? | alphabetical | date of discovery | *atomic mass | atomic number | He ordered them by the mass of their atoms, lightest first.
C: [1] Why did Mendeleev leave gaps in his table? | He ran out of elements he liked. | To make the table symmetrical. | *For elements not yet discovered. | For elements that are gases. | He predicted that elements would be found to fill them.
C: [1] What convinced other scientists that Mendeleev was right? | He was famous. | His table was colourful. | He discovered germanium himself. | *New elements matched his predictions. | A correct prediction is strong evidence for a scientific idea.

== k3-6-2
S: The Periodic Table is a map of the elements. Rows are called periods and columns are called groups. Elements in the same group behave alike, and a zig-zag line separates the metals on the left from the non-metals on the right.
<<<
## Rows and columns
| | What it is | What it tells you |
| **Period** | a horizontal row | properties change gradually from metal to non-metal as you go across |
| **Group** | a vertical column | the elements have **similar chemical properties** |

## Metals and non-metals
- **Metals** are on the **left** and in the **centre**. About three-quarters of all the elements are metals.
- **Non-metals** are on the **right**, together with hydrogen.
- A **zig-zag (staircase) line** separates them. Elements next to the line, such as silicon, have some properties of both.

## Groups with names
| Group | Name | Members include | What they are like |
| 1 | alkali metals | lithium, sodium, potassium | soft, very reactive metals |
| 2 | alkaline earth metals | magnesium, calcium | reactive metals |
| 7 | halogens | fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine | reactive, coloured non-metals |
| 0 | noble gases | helium, neon, argon | unreactive gases |

The block between Group 2 and Group 3 contains the **transition metals**, such as iron, copper and gold. They are the typical hard, strong, everyday metals.

## Using the table
If you know how one element in a group behaves, you can predict how the others will behave. Potassium is just below sodium in Group 1, so, like sodium, it is a soft metal that reacts with water.
>>>
W: Periods go across; groups go down. A period is like a full stop at the end of a line of writing: a row.
W: Hydrogen sits on the left of many tables, but it is a non-metal.
M: What a group is :: a **vertical column** of elements with **similar properties**
M: What a period is :: a **horizontal row**
M: Where metals are found :: on the **left** and in the **centre**
M: Name of Group 1 :: the **alkali metals**
M: Name of Group 7 :: the **halogens**
M: Name of Group 0 :: the **noble gases**
C: [1] What is a column in the Periodic Table called? | a period | *a group | a row | a block | Groups are vertical columns of similar elements.
C: [1] Where are the non-metals found in the Periodic Table? | on the left | in the centre | *on the right | at the bottom | Non-metals are to the right of the zig-zag line.
C: [1] Which group contains unreactive gases? | Group 1 | Group 2 | Group 7 | *Group 0 | Group 0 contains the noble gases.

== k3-6-3
S: Most metals are shiny, strong and good conductors of heat and electricity. Most non-metals are the opposite: dull, brittle and poor conductors. Knowing the typical properties, and the few exceptions, lets you decide which kind of element you have.
<<<
## Physical properties compared
| Property | Metals | Non-metals |
| Appearance | **shiny** when freshly cut or polished | **dull** |
| Conducting electricity | **good** conductors | **poor** conductors (insulators) |
| Conducting heat | **good** conductors | **poor** conductors |
| When hit | **malleable**: can be hammered into shape | **brittle**: solid non-metals shatter |
| When pulled | **ductile**: can be drawn into wires | break |
| Melting and boiling points | usually **high** | usually **low** |
| Density | usually **high** | usually **low** |
| Sound when struck | **sonorous**: they ring | dull thud |
| State at room temperature | solids (except mercury) | many are gases; bromine is a liquid |

Only three common metals are **magnetic**: iron, cobalt and nickel.

## Exceptions worth knowing
- **Mercury** is a metal that is liquid at room temperature.
- **Sodium** and **potassium** are soft enough to cut with a knife, and they float on water.
- **Graphite**, a form of the non-metal carbon, conducts electricity.
- **Diamond**, another form of carbon, is extremely hard and has a very high melting point.

## Matching property to use
| Use | Metal | Property that matters |
| electrical wires | copper | conducts electricity; ductile |
| saucepans | aluminium, steel | conducts heat; high melting point |
| aircraft bodies | aluminium | low density for a metal; strong |
| bells | bronze | sonorous |
>>>

W: Malleable means it can be hammered into shape. Ductile means it can be pulled into a wire.
W: Judge an element on several properties, not one. Graphite conducts but is still a non-metal.

M: Meaning of ductile :: can be **drawn into wires**
M: Electrical conductivity of metals :: **good conductors**

== k3-6-4
S: The elements in a group are alike, but they change in a regular way from top to bottom. Once you know the trend, you can predict the properties of an element you have never seen.
<<<
## Group 1: the alkali metals
They are soft metals with low densities, stored under oil because they react with air and water.

alkali metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen

| Metal | What you see in water |
| lithium | floats and fizzes steadily |
| sodium | melts into a ball and whizzes across the surface |
| potassium | catches fire at once and burns with a lilac flame |

**Trend:** reactivity **increases down** the group. Melting points decrease down the group.

The solution left behind is alkaline, which is how the alkali metals got their name.

## Group 7: the halogens
They are coloured, poisonous non-metals.

| Halogen | At room temperature |
| fluorine | pale yellow gas |
| chlorine | pale green gas |
| bromine | red-brown liquid |
| iodine | grey-black solid |

**Trends:** melting and boiling points **increase down** the group. Reactivity **decreases down** the group, the opposite way to Group 1.

A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its compound:

chlorine + potassium bromide → potassium chloride + bromine

## Group 0: the noble gases
They are colourless gases that are **unreactive**: they hardly ever form compounds. Their boiling points increase down the group.

| Gas | Use | Why |
| helium | balloons and airships | much less dense than air, and does not burn |
| neon | advertising signs | glows red when electricity passes through it |
| argon | filling light bulbs | will not react with the hot filament |

## Making a prediction
| Question | Reasoning | Prediction |
| How does rubidium (below potassium) react with water? | reactivity increases down Group 1 | more violently than potassium: it explodes |
| What state is astatine (below iodine)? | melting points increase down Group 7 | a dark solid |
| Does krypton react with water? | Group 0 elements are unreactive | no |
>>>
W: Group 1 gets more reactive going down. Group 7 gets less reactive going down.
W: The alkali metal does not just "dissolve" in water. It reacts, making a hydroxide and hydrogen gas.
M: Trend in reactivity down Group 1 :: reactivity **increases**
M: Products of an alkali metal with water :: **metal hydroxide** and **hydrogen**
M: Trend in reactivity down Group 7 :: reactivity **decreases**
M: Trend in melting point down Group 7 :: melting point **increases**
M: Reactivity of the noble gases :: **unreactive** (inert)
M: Why argon is used in light bulbs :: it is **unreactive**, so the filament does not burn
C: [1] Which alkali metal reacts most violently with water? | lithium | sodium | *potassium | they all react the same | Reactivity increases down Group 1.
C: [1] Which halogen is a liquid at room temperature? | fluorine | chlorine | *bromine | iodine | Bromine is a red-brown liquid.
C: [1] Why is helium used in airships instead of hydrogen? | It is cheaper. | It is denser than air. | It is coloured. | *It is unreactive, so it cannot catch fire. | Helium is a noble gas and does not burn.

== k3-6-5
S: Metals and non-metals both react with oxygen to make oxides, but the oxides behave in opposite ways. Metal oxides are bases, and non-metal oxides are acidic.
<<<
## Making oxides
metal + oxygen → metal oxide

non-metal + oxygen → non-metal oxide

| Element burned | Oxide formed | Oxide is |
| magnesium | magnesium oxide, a white solid | a metal oxide |
| calcium | calcium oxide, a white solid | a metal oxide |
| carbon | carbon dioxide, a gas | a non-metal oxide |
| sulfur | sulfur dioxide, a choking gas | a non-metal oxide |

## Metal oxides are basic
- A metal oxide is a **base**: it **neutralises an acid** to make a salt and water.
- copper oxide + sulfuric acid → copper sulfate + water
- Some metal oxides dissolve in water to make **alkaline** solutions. Sodium oxide makes sodium hydroxide, and calcium oxide makes calcium hydroxide (limewater).
- These solutions turn universal indicator **blue or purple**.

## Non-metal oxides are acidic
- Many non-metal oxides dissolve in water to make **acidic** solutions.
- Carbon dioxide makes carbonic acid, which is why fizzy drinks are acidic.
- Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fuels dissolve in rainwater to make **acid rain**.
- These solutions turn universal indicator **orange or red**.

## Summary
| | Metal oxides | Non-metal oxides |
| Usual state | solids | often gases |
| Nature | **basic** | **acidic** |
| pH if it dissolves in water | above 7 | below 7 |
| Reacts with | acids | alkalis |

Testing an oxide with indicator is one way to decide whether an element is a metal or a non-metal.
>>>
W: An insoluble metal oxide such as copper oxide is still a base. It does not change the indicator, but it does neutralise acids.
W: It is the oxide that is acidic or basic, not the element itself.
M: Nature of metal oxides :: **basic**
M: Nature of non-metal oxides :: **acidic**
M: What a basic oxide does to an acid :: **neutralises** it to make a **salt and water**
M: Cause of acid rain :: **sulfur dioxide** and **nitrogen oxides** dissolving in rainwater
M: pH of a solution of a non-metal oxide :: **below 7**
