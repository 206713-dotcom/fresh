S: Lattice energy measures how strongly the ions in a solid attract each other. It cannot be measured directly, so it is found from a Born–Haber cycle: a Hess's law cycle that takes the elements to gaseous ions step by step. Small, highly charged ions give the most exothermic lattice energies.
<<<
## Definitions
!def Enthalpy change of atomisation, ΔHat | The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms is formed from the element in its standard state, under standard conditions.

$$\mathrm{Na(s) \rightarrow Na(g)} \qquad \mathrm{\tfrac{1}{2}Cl_2(g) \rightarrow Cl(g)}$$

Always **endothermic**. It is per mole of **atoms formed**, so for chlorine it is half the Cl–Cl bond energy.

!def Lattice energy, ΔHlatt | The enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions, under standard conditions.

$$\mathrm{Na^+(g) + Cl^-(g) \rightarrow NaCl(s)}$$

Always **exothermic**: gaseous ions come together to form a solid.

!def First electron affinity, EA | The enthalpy change when one mole of electrons is added to one mole of gaseous atoms, to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions.

$$\mathrm{Cl(g) + e^- \rightarrow Cl^-(g)}$$

- The **first** electron affinity is usually **exothermic**: the incoming electron is attracted by the nucleus.
- The **second** electron affinity is **endothermic**: an electron is being added to an ion that is already negative, so energy is needed to overcome the **repulsion**.

$$\mathrm{O^-(g) + e^- \rightarrow O^{2-}(g)} \qquad EA_2 = +798\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$$

## What affects electron affinity
| Factor | Effect |
| **nuclear charge** | greater nuclear charge: stronger attraction for the incoming electron: more exothermic |
| **atomic radius** | larger atom: the electron is added further from the nucleus: less exothermic |
| **shielding** | more inner shells: weaker attraction: less exothermic |

### Trends in Groups 16 and 17
**Down the group, electron affinities generally become less exothermic.** The atoms are larger and there is more shielding, so the attraction between the nucleus and the added electron is weaker.

| | F | Cl | Br | I |
| EA / kJ mol⁻¹ | −328 | −349 | −325 | −295 |

Fluorine (and oxygen) do not fit: the atom is so small that the added electron is **repelled** by the electrons already crowded into the small outer shell, so its electron affinity is less exothermic than chlorine's.

## Born–Haber cycles
A Born–Haber cycle applies Hess's law to the formation of an ionic solid.

**Route 1:** elements → compound directly: ΔHf.

**Route 2:** elements → gaseous atoms → gaseous ions → solid lattice.

$$\Delta H_f = \Delta H_{at}(\text{metal}) + IE + \Delta H_{at}(\text{non-metal}) + EA + \Delta H_{latt}$$

### Sodium chloride
| Step | Equation | ΔH / kJ mol⁻¹ |
| atomise sodium | Na(s) → Na(g) | +107 |
| ionise sodium | Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻ | +496 |
| atomise chlorine | ½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g) | +122 |
| electron affinity | Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g) | −349 |
| lattice energy | Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) → NaCl(s) | ? |
| formation | Na(s) + ½Cl₂(g) → NaCl(s) | −411 |

$$\Delta H_{latt} = -411 - (107 + 496 + 122 - 349) = -411 - 376 = -787\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$$

!mol Shape of the Born-Haber cycle for NaCl
 Na+(g) + e- + Cl(g)
   ^                 |  EA
   | at(Cl)          v
 Na+(g) + e- + 1/2Cl2    Na+(g) + Cl-(g)
   ^ IE                        |
 Na(g) + 1/2Cl2(g)             |  lattice
   ^ at(Na)                    |  energy
 Na(s) + 1/2Cl2(g)             |
   |  formation                v
   +----------------------->  NaCl(s)
!end

Endothermic steps are drawn as arrows going up; exothermic steps as arrows going down.

### Ions with 2+ and 2− charges
| Compound | Changes needed |
| **MgCl₂** | first **and second** ionisation energies of Mg; **2 ×** ΔHat of chlorine; **2 ×** the electron affinity of chlorine |
| **MgO** | first and second ionisation energies of Mg; ΔHat of oxygen; **first and second** electron affinities of oxygen |
| **Na₂O** | **2 ×** ΔHat of Na; **2 ×** IE of Na; ΔHat of O; first and second electron affinities of O |

**Magnesium oxide.** ΔHf = −602; ΔHat(Mg) = +148; IE₁ = +738; IE₂ = +1451; ΔHat(O) = +249; EA₁ = −141; EA₂ = +798.

$$\Delta H_{latt} = -602 - (148 + 738 + 1451 + 249 - 141 + 798) = -602 - 3243 = -3845\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$$

| **ionic radius** | smaller ions: **more exothermic** | the ions are closer together, so they attract more strongly |

S: When an ionic solid dissolves, the lattice is pulled apart and the freed ions are surrounded by water molecules. Whether the solid dissolves with a rise or fall in temperature depends on the balance between lattice energy and the enthalpy changes of hydration.
<<<
## Definitions
!def Enthalpy change of hydration, ΔHhyd | The enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous ions dissolves in water to form a solution of infinite dilution.

$$\mathrm{Na^+(g) \rightarrow Na^+(aq)}$$

Always **exothermic**: bonds form between the ions and the polar water molecules (ion–dipole attractions). The δ− oxygen of water is attracted to cations, and the δ+ hydrogens to anions.

!def Enthalpy change of solution, ΔHsol | The enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid dissolves in water to form a solution of infinite dilution.

$$\mathrm{NaCl(s) \rightarrow Na^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq)}$$

It can be **exothermic or endothermic**.

"Infinite dilution" means so dilute that adding more water causes no further enthalpy change.

## The energy cycle
Dissolving can be imagined in two steps.

- **Break up the lattice** into gaseous ions: the reverse of lattice energy, so +(−ΔHlatt): endothermic.
- **Hydrate the gaseous ions**: the sum of the hydration enthalpies: exothermic.

$$\Delta H_{sol} = -\Delta H_{latt} + \sum \Delta H_{hyd}$$

or, going round the cycle the other way:

$$\Delta H_{latt} + \Delta H_{sol} = \sum \Delta H_{hyd}$$

!mol Energy cycle for dissolving an ionic solid
          gaseous ions
          M+(g) + X-(g)
          /            \
  lattice/              \ hydration
  energy/                \ (both ions)
       v                  v
   MX(s)  -------->  M+(aq) + X-(aq)
            solution
!end

**Example.** Sodium chloride: ΔHlatt = −787; ΔHhyd(Na⁺) = −406; ΔHhyd(Cl⁻) = −377 kJ mol⁻¹.

$$\Delta H_{sol} = -(-787) + (-406) + (-377) = +787 - 783 = +4\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$$

**For a compound such as MgCl₂**, use **two** hydration enthalpies of Cl⁻.

$$\Delta H_{sol} = -\Delta H_{latt} + \Delta H_{hyd}(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}) + 2\Delta H_{hyd}(\mathrm{Cl^-})$$

The cycle can be used to find any one of the three quantities if the other two are known.

## What affects the enthalpy change of hydration
| Factor | Effect | Reason |
| **ionic charge** | greater charge: **more exothermic** | stronger attraction between the ion and the water molecules |
| **ionic radius** | smaller ion: **more exothermic** | higher charge density; the water molecules get closer and are attracted more strongly |

| Ion | Li⁺ | Na⁺ | K⁺ | Mg²⁺ | Al³⁺ |
| ΔHhyd / kJ mol⁻¹ | −519 | −406 | −322 | −1920 | −4690 |

Down a group the ions get larger and ΔHhyd becomes less exothermic. Across Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ the charge rises and the radius falls, so it becomes much more exothermic.

## Predicting solubility
- If ΔHsol is **exothermic or only slightly endothermic**, the solid is likely to be soluble.
- If ΔHsol is **strongly endothermic**, the solid is likely to be insoluble: the hydration enthalpies do not make up for the energy needed to break the lattice.

Both lattice energy and hydration enthalpy depend on charge and radius in the same way, so solubility depends on a fine balance between them. (Entropy matters too.)

S: Entropy counts the number of ways particles and their energy can be arranged: the more ways, the higher the entropy. Gases have far more entropy than solids, so a reaction that makes more moles of gas has a positive entropy change.
<<<
## Entropy
!def Entropy, S | The number of possible arrangements of the particles and their energy in a given system.

- Units: **J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹** (joules, not kilojoules).
- The more ways the particles and their energy can be arranged (the more disordered the system), the **higher the entropy**.
- All substances have a positive entropy above 0 K. A perfect crystal at 0 K has zero entropy.

## Predicting the sign of an entropy change
### Changes of state and dissolving
$$S(\text{solid}) < S(\text{liquid}) \ll S(\text{gas})$$

| Change | ΔS | Reason |
| melting | **positive** | particles become free to move past each other: more possible arrangements |
| boiling, evaporating, subliming | **large and positive** | particles in a gas move freely in all directions and are far apart |
| condensing, freezing | **negative** | fewer possible arrangements |
| a solid dissolving | usually **positive** | ions or molecules are spread through the solution and free to move |

### Temperature
Raising the temperature **increases** entropy: the particles have more energy, and there are more ways of sharing that energy among them.

### A change in the number of moles of gas
| Reaction | ΔS |
| **more** moles of gas in the products | **positive** |
| **fewer** moles of gas in the products | **negative** |
| the same number of moles of gas | small |

| Reaction | Moles of gas | Sign of ΔS |
| CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g) | 0 → 1 | positive |
| N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g) | 4 → 2 | negative |
| 2H₂O₂(l) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g) | 0 → 1 | positive |
| NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s) | 2 → 0 | negative |
| H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g) | 2 → 2 | close to zero |

## Calculating an entropy change
$$\Delta S^\ominus = \sum S^\ominus(\text{products}) - \sum S^\ominus(\text{reactants})$$

Multiply each standard entropy by its balancing number. Elements do **not** have zero entropy.

**Example.** $\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightarrow 2NH_3(g)}$

S⦵ / J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: N₂ 191.6; H₂ 130.6; NH₃ 192.3.

$$\Delta S^\ominus = (2 \times 192.3) - [191.6 + (3 \times 130.6)] = 384.6 - 583.4 = -198.8\ \text{J K}^{-1}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$$

Negative, as predicted: four moles of gas become two.

**Example.** $\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) \rightarrow CaO(s) + CO_2(g)}$

S⦵: CaCO₃ 92.9; CaO 39.7; CO₂ 213.6.

$$\Delta S^\ominus = (39.7 + 213.6) - 92.9 = +160.4\ \text{J K}^{-1}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$$

## Why entropy matters
Processes tend to go in the direction that increases the total entropy. An endothermic process can still happen if the entropy increases enough, which is why ice melts and ammonium nitrate dissolves. Whether a reaction is feasible depends on both ΔH and ΔS, combined in the Gibbs free energy.

== ca-23-4
S: The Gibbs free energy change, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, combines enthalpy and entropy into one test. A reaction is feasible when ΔG is negative. Because T multiplies ΔS, changing the temperature can switch a reaction between feasible and not feasible.
<<<
## The Gibbs equation
$$\Delta G^\ominus = \Delta H^\ominus - T\Delta S^\ominus$$

| Symbol | Quantity | Unit |
| ΔG⦵ | standard Gibbs free energy change | kJ mol⁻¹ |
| ΔH⦵ | standard enthalpy change | kJ mol⁻¹ |
| T | temperature | K |
| ΔS⦵ | standard entropy change | **J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: divide by 1000** to get kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ |

## Feasibility
| ΔG | Reaction |
| **negative** | **feasible** (can happen spontaneously) |
| **positive** | **not feasible** |
| zero | at equilibrium: just feasible |

!key Feasible does not mean fast. A feasible reaction may have such a high activation energy that it is too slow to observe. ΔG says nothing about rate.

## Calculations
**Example 1.** $\mathrm{N_2 + 3H_2 \rightarrow 2NH_3}$ at 298 K. ΔH⦵ = −92 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔS⦵ = −198.8 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

- ΔS⦵ = −0.1988 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
- ΔG⦵ = −92 − (298 × −0.1988) = −92 + 59.2 = **−32.8 kJ mol⁻¹**
- Negative: feasible at 298 K.

**Example 2.** $\mathrm{CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2}$ at 298 K. ΔH⦵ = +178 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔS⦵ = +160.4 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

- ΔG⦵ = +178 − (298 × 0.1604) = +178 − 47.8 = **+130.2 kJ mol⁻¹**
- Positive: not feasible at 298 K.

## The effect of temperature
The −TΔS term grows as T rises. Assume ΔH and ΔS do not change with temperature.

| ΔH | ΔS | −TΔS | Feasible? |
| **negative** | **positive** | negative | **always** (ΔG always negative) |
| **positive** | **negative** | positive | **never** (ΔG always positive) |
| **negative** | **negative** | positive | only at **low** temperatures |
| **positive** | **positive** | negative | only at **high** temperatures |

### The temperature at which a reaction becomes feasible
Set ΔG = 0:

$$T = \frac{\Delta H}{\Delta S}$$

Use the same energy units for both.

**Example.** For the decomposition of calcium carbonate:

$$T = \frac{178\,000}{160.4} = 1110\ \text{K}$$

Above 1110 K, ΔG is negative and the decomposition is feasible.

For the Haber process, ΔH and ΔS are both negative: the reaction is feasible only below T = 92 000 ÷ 198.8 = 463 K. Above that, the equilibrium increasingly favours the reactants.

## Explaining in words
- An **exothermic reaction with a decrease in entropy** becomes less feasible as temperature rises: −TΔS is positive and gets bigger, eventually outweighing ΔH.
- An **endothermic reaction with an increase in entropy** becomes feasible at high temperature: −TΔS is negative and eventually outweighs the positive ΔH.

This is why endothermic reactions such as thermal decomposition need heating: at high temperature the entropy term wins.

Q: [2] State the Gibbs equation and the condition for a reaction to be feasible.
+ ΔG = ΔH − TΔS :: ΔH − TΔS|delta H|H minus T
+ the reaction is feasible if ΔG is negative (less than or equal to zero) :: negative|less than|zero
A: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, which in words is delta H minus T delta S. The reaction is feasible if ΔG is negative.
Q: [3] The Haber process, N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, has a negative ΔH and a negative ΔS. Explain how the feasibility of the reaction changes as the temperature increases.
+ −TΔS is positive (because ΔS is negative) :: positive
+ as temperature increases, this term becomes larger, so ΔG becomes less negative (more positive) :: less negative|more positive|larger|increas
+ so the reaction becomes less feasible (not feasible at high temperature) :: less feasible|not feasible|no longer feasible
A: Because ΔS is negative, the term −TΔS is positive. As the temperature increases this term becomes larger, so ΔG becomes less negative and eventually positive. The reaction therefore becomes less feasible, and is not feasible at high temperatures.

A: The reaction may have a high activation energy, so although it is feasible, its rate is too slow to be observed.
