== ci-3-1
S: A formula says which atoms are in a substance and how many. A balanced equation says what reacts, what forms and in what ratio, with the same number of each atom on both sides. State symbols say whether each substance is solid, liquid, gas or dissolved in water.

## Formulae you must know
### Elements
- Most elements are written as a single atom: Fe, Mg, C, S, He.
- Seven elements exist as **diatomic molecules**: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.

### Common compounds
| Name | Formula | | Name | Formula |
| water | H₂O | | hydrochloric acid | HCl |
| carbon dioxide | CO₂ | | sulfuric acid | H₂SO₄ |
| carbon monoxide | CO | | nitric acid | HNO₃ |
| ammonia | NH₃ | | sodium hydroxide | NaOH |
| methane | CH₄ | | calcium carbonate | CaCO₃ |
| sulfur dioxide | SO₂ | | copper(II) sulfate | CuSO₄ |

## Molecular and empirical formulae
!def Molecular formula | The number and type of different atoms in one molecule.

!def Empirical formula | The simplest whole number ratio of the different atoms or ions in a compound.

| Compound | Molecular formula | Empirical formula |
| ethane | C₂H₆ | CH₃ |
| glucose | C₆H₁₂O₆ | CH₂O |
| water | H₂O | H₂O |

To get from a model or diagram to a formula, count the atoms of each element. A molecule drawn with one carbon atom joined to two oxygen atoms is CO₂.

The formula of an ionic compound is always an empirical formula, because it is a giant lattice, not a molecule.

## Formulae of ionic compounds
An ionic compound has no overall charge, so the positive and negative charges must cancel.

### Ions to learn
| Positive ions | | Negative ions | |
| sodium | Na⁺ | chloride | Cl⁻ |
| potassium | K⁺ | bromide | Br⁻ |
| silver | Ag⁺ | hydroxide | OH⁻ |
| ammonium | NH₄⁺ | nitrate | NO₃⁻ |
| magnesium | Mg²⁺ | oxide | O²⁻ |
| calcium | Ca²⁺ | sulfate | SO₄²⁻ |
| zinc | Zn²⁺ | carbonate | CO₃²⁻ |
| copper(II) | Cu²⁺ | | |
| iron(II) | Fe²⁺ | | |
| iron(III) | Fe³⁺ | | |
| aluminium | Al³⁺ | | |

The Roman numeral in a name gives the charge on the metal ion: iron(III) is Fe³⁺.

### Method
- Write the two ions with their charges.
- Find how many of each make the total positive charge equal to the total negative charge.
- Write the formula without the charges. Put brackets round a group of atoms if you need more than one of it.

| Compound | Ions | Balance the charges | Formula |
| sodium chloride | Na⁺, Cl⁻ | 1+ and 1− | NaCl |
| magnesium chloride | Mg²⁺, Cl⁻ | 2+ needs two 1− | MgCl₂ |
| aluminium oxide | Al³⁺, O²⁻ | two 3+ (6+) and three 2− (6−) | Al₂O₃ |
| calcium hydroxide | Ca²⁺, OH⁻ | 2+ needs two 1− | Ca(OH)₂ |
| ammonium sulfate | NH₄⁺, SO₄²⁻ | two 1+ and 2− | (NH₄)₂SO₄ |

From a diagram of a lattice, count the ions: if there are twice as many chloride ions as calcium ions, the formula is CaCl₂.

## Equations
A **word equation** names the substances:

magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

A **symbol equation** uses formulae and must be **balanced**: the same number of atoms of each element on both sides. Atoms are not created or destroyed in a reaction.

### Balancing, step by step
- Write the correct formula for every substance.
- Count the atoms of each element on each side.
- Put numbers **in front** of formulae to make the counts equal. Never change the small numbers inside a formula.
- Check every element again.

**Example:** magnesium burning in oxygen.

$$\mathrm{Mg + O_2 \rightarrow MgO}$$

There are two O on the left and one on the right, so put a 2 in front of MgO.

$$\mathrm{Mg + O_2 \rightarrow 2MgO}$$

Now there are two Mg on the right and one on the left, so put a 2 in front of Mg.

$$\mathrm{2Mg + O_2 \rightarrow 2MgO}$$

### State symbols
| Symbol | Meaning |
| (s) | solid |
| (l) | liquid |
| (g) | gas |
| (aq) | aqueous: dissolved in water |

$$\mathrm{2Mg(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2MgO(s)}$$

$$\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow CaCl_2(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g)}$$

Water is (l). An acid, an alkali or a dissolved salt is (aq). A precipitate is (s).

## Ionic equations
An ionic equation shows only the particles that actually change. Ions that are the same before and after are **spectator ions**, and are left out.

### Method
- Write the balanced symbol equation with state symbols.
- Split every **(aq) ionic** substance into its ions. Leave solids, liquids and gases as they are.
- Cross out the ions that appear unchanged on both sides.
- Write what is left, and check that atoms and charges both balance.

**Example:** hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide.

$$\mathrm{HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) \rightarrow NaCl(aq) + H_2O(l)}$$

$$\mathrm{H^+ + Cl^- + Na^+ + OH^- \rightarrow Na^+ + Cl^- + H_2O}$$

Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are spectator ions.

$$\mathrm{H^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) \rightarrow H_2O(l)}$$

**Example:** silver nitrate solution and sodium chloride solution give a white precipitate of silver chloride.

$$\mathrm{Ag^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) \rightarrow AgCl(s)}$$

**Example:** zinc metal and copper(II) sulfate solution.

$$\mathrm{Zn(s) + Cu^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq) + Cu(s)}$$

== ci-3-2
S: Atoms are far too light to weigh one at a time, so their masses are compared with carbon-12. The relative atomic mass of an element is the average mass of its atoms on that scale, and the relative molecular mass of a compound is the sum of the relative atomic masses in its formula.
<<<
## Relative atomic mass
!def Relative atomic mass, Ar | The average mass of the isotopes of an element compared to 1/12th of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.

- It is an **average**, because most elements are a mixture of isotopes.
- It is **relative**: a comparison, so it has **no units**.
- One atom of carbon-12 is given a mass of exactly 12.

$A_r$ values are printed on the Periodic Table: H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16, Na = 23, Mg = 24, S = 32, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40, Fe = 56, Cu = 64.

## Relative molecular mass
!def Relative molecular mass, Mr | The sum of the relative atomic masses of the atoms in a molecule.

For an ionic compound, which has no molecules, the same sum is called the **relative formula mass**, and the same symbol $M_r$ is used.

### Calculating $M_r$
Add up the $A_r$ of every atom in the formula. A number outside a bracket multiplies everything inside.

| Substance | Working | $M_r$ |
| H₂O | (2 × 1) + 16 | 18 |
| CO₂ | 12 + (2 × 16) | 44 |
| CaCO₃ | 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) | 100 |
| H₂SO₄ | (2 × 1) + 32 + (4 × 16) | 98 |
| Ca(OH)₂ | 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) | 74 |
| (NH₄)₂SO₄ | 2 × (14 + 4) + 32 + (4 × 16) | 132 |
| CuSO₄·5H₂O | 64 + 32 + 64 + 5 × 18 | 250 |

## Reacting masses by simple proportion
The balanced equation, with the $M_r$ values, gives the masses that react. Other masses are in the same proportion.

### Worked example
What mass of magnesium oxide is made when 12 g of magnesium burns?

$$\mathrm{2Mg + O_2 \rightarrow 2MgO}$$

Masses from the equation: 2 × 24 = 48 g of Mg gives 2 × (24 + 16) = 80 g of MgO.

$$48\ \mathrm{g\ Mg} \rightarrow 80\ \mathrm{g\ MgO}$$

$$1\ \mathrm{g\ Mg} \rightarrow \frac{80}{48}\ \mathrm{g\ MgO}$$

$$12\ \mathrm{g\ Mg} \rightarrow \frac{80}{48} \times 12 = 20\ \mathrm{g\ MgO}$$

### Another
What mass of calcium oxide is made by heating 25 g of calcium carbonate?

$$\mathrm{CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2}$$

100 g of CaCO₃ gives 56 g of CaO.

$$25\ \mathrm{g} \rightarrow \frac{56}{100} \times 25 = 14\ \mathrm{g}$$

### Conservation of mass
The total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants. In the example above, 25 g of calcium carbonate gives 14 g of calcium oxide, so 25 − 14 = 11 g of carbon dioxide is given off.

== ci-3-3
S: A mole is a fixed number of particles, 6.02 × 10²³. The mass of one mole of a substance is its relative formula mass in grams, which lets you count particles by weighing. Every calculation in chemistry runs through moles: masses, gas volumes, solutions, formulae, yield and purity.
<<<
## The mole
!def The mole | The unit of amount of substance. One mole contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles, for example atoms, ions or molecules. This number is the Avogadro constant.

- The **molar mass** is the mass of one mole. It is the $A_r$ or $M_r$ in grams per mole.
- One mole of carbon, C, has a mass of 12 g. One mole of water, H₂O, has a mass of 18 g.

## Moles and mass
$$\text{amount of substance (mol)} = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{\text{molar mass (g/mol)}}$$

$$n = \frac{m}{M} \qquad m = n \times M \qquad M = \frac{m}{n}$$

**Example 1.** How many moles are in 22 g of carbon dioxide? ($M_r$ = 44)

$$n = \frac{22}{44} = 0.50\ \mathrm{mol}$$

**Example 2.** What is the mass of 0.25 mol of calcium carbonate? ($M_r$ = 100)

$$m = 0.25 \times 100 = 25\ \mathrm{g}$$

**Example 3.** 0.20 mol of a compound has a mass of 11.7 g. Find its $M_r$.

$$M = \frac{11.7}{0.20} = 58.5$$

### Number of particles
$$\text{number of particles} = \text{amount (mol)} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}$$

0.50 mol of water contains 0.50 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²³ molecules. Each molecule has 3 atoms, so that is 9.03 × 10²³ atoms.

## Moles and gases
!key One mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³ at room temperature and pressure (r.t.p.). This is the molar gas volume.

$$\text{amount (mol)} = \frac{\text{volume (dm}^3)}{24} \qquad \text{volume (dm}^3) = \text{amount (mol)} \times 24$$

24 dm³ = 24 000 cm³. If the volume is in cm³, divide by 24 000.

**Example.** What volume does 0.10 mol of oxygen occupy at r.t.p.?

$$V = 0.10 \times 24 = 2.4\ \mathrm{dm^3}$$

## Moles and solutions
Concentration is the amount of solute dissolved in 1 dm³ of solution. It is measured in **g/dm³** or **mol/dm³**.

$$\text{concentration (mol/dm}^3) = \frac{\text{amount (mol)}}{\text{volume (dm}^3)} \qquad n = c \times V$$

!warn Volumes must be in dm³. To convert cm³ to dm³, **divide by 1000**. 25.0 cm³ = 0.0250 dm³.

To change between the two units of concentration:

$$\text{concentration (g/dm}^3) = \text{concentration (mol/dm}^3) \times M_r$$

**Example.** 4.0 g of sodium hydroxide ($M_r$ = 40) is dissolved to make 250 cm³ of solution. Find the concentration.

$$n = \frac{4.0}{40} = 0.10\ \mathrm{mol}$$

$$V = \frac{250}{1000} = 0.250\ \mathrm{dm^3}$$

$$c = \frac{0.10}{0.250} = 0.40\ \mathrm{mol/dm^3}$$

In g/dm³ this is 0.40 × 40 = 16 g/dm³.

## Calculations from equations
The numbers in front of the formulae in a balanced equation give the **ratio of moles**.

### The three steps
- **Moles** of the substance you know.
- **Ratio** from the equation, to get moles of the substance you want.
- **Convert** those moles to a mass, a gas volume or a concentration.

**Example: mass to mass.** What mass of iron is made from 320 g of iron(III) oxide? ($M_r$ of Fe₂O₃ = 160; $A_r$ of Fe = 56)

$$\mathrm{Fe_2O_3 + 3CO \rightarrow 2Fe + 3CO_2}$$

$$n(\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}) = \frac{320}{160} = 2.0\ \mathrm{mol}$$

The ratio Fe₂O₃ : Fe is 1 : 2.

$$n(\mathrm{Fe}) = 2.0 \times 2 = 4.0\ \mathrm{mol}$$

$$m(\mathrm{Fe}) = 4.0 \times 56 = 224\ \mathrm{g}$$

**Example: mass to gas volume.** What volume of carbon dioxide, at r.t.p., is made from 5.0 g of calcium carbonate? ($M_r$ = 100)

$$\mathrm{CaCO_3 + 2HCl \rightarrow CaCl_2 + H_2O + CO_2}$$

$$n(\mathrm{CaCO_3}) = \frac{5.0}{100} = 0.050\ \mathrm{mol}$$

The ratio is 1 : 1, so n(CO₂) = 0.050 mol.

$$V = 0.050 \times 24 = 1.2\ \mathrm{dm^3}$$

## Limiting reactants
Usually one reactant runs out first. It is the **limiting reactant**, and it decides how much product forms. The other reactant is **in excess**.

To find it: calculate the moles of each reactant, then compare them using the ratio in the equation.

**Example.** 4.8 g of magnesium ($A_r$ = 24) is added to a solution containing 0.30 mol of hydrochloric acid.

$$\mathrm{Mg + 2HCl \rightarrow MgCl_2 + H_2}$$

$$n(\mathrm{Mg}) = \frac{4.8}{24} = 0.20\ \mathrm{mol}$$

0.20 mol of Mg needs 0.20 × 2 = 0.40 mol of HCl. Only 0.30 mol is present, so **the acid is the limiting reactant** and the magnesium is in excess.

Use the limiting reactant to find the product: 0.30 mol of HCl gives 0.30 ÷ 2 = 0.15 mol of H₂, which is 0.15 × 24 = 3.6 dm³ at r.t.p.

## Titration calculations
In a titration you know the volume and concentration of one solution and the volume of the other.

**Example.** 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution is neutralised by 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid. Find the concentration of the sodium hydroxide.

$$\mathrm{NaOH + HCl \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O}$$

Moles of the solution you know everything about:

$$n(\mathrm{HCl}) = 0.100 \times \frac{20.0}{1000} = 0.00200\ \mathrm{mol}$$

Ratio 1 : 1, so n(NaOH) = 0.00200 mol.

$$c(\mathrm{NaOH}) = \frac{0.00200}{25.0 \div 1000} = \frac{0.00200}{0.0250} = 0.0800\ \mathrm{mol/dm^3}$$

If the ratio is not 1 : 1, as with H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH, multiply or divide by the ratio at the second step.

## Empirical and molecular formulae
### Empirical formula from masses or percentages
- Write the mass (or percentage) of each element.
- Divide each by its $A_r$ to get moles.
- Divide every answer by the smallest one.
- If needed, multiply to get whole numbers.

**Example.** A compound contains 2.4 g of carbon and 0.8 g of hydrogen.

| | C | H |
| mass (g) | 2.4 | 0.8 |
| ÷ $A_r$ | 2.4 ÷ 12 = 0.20 | 0.8 ÷ 1 = 0.80 |
| ÷ smallest | 0.20 ÷ 0.20 = 1 | 0.80 ÷ 0.20 = 4 |

Empirical formula: CH₄.

### Molecular formula
$$\text{number of empirical units} = \frac{M_r}{\text{mass of the empirical formula}}$$

**Example.** The empirical formula is CH₂ and $M_r$ = 56. The mass of CH₂ is 14. 56 ÷ 14 = 4, so the molecular formula is C₄H₈.

## Percentage yield, composition and purity
$$\text{percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100$$

The theoretical yield is the mass calculated from the equation. The actual yield is what you really collect. It is usually lower because the reaction may be reversible, some product is lost in transfers and purification, and other reactions may happen.

$$\text{percentage composition by mass} = \frac{\text{number of atoms} \times A_r}{M_r} \times 100$$

**Example.** Percentage of nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃ ($M_r$ = 80): there are two N atoms.

$$\frac{2 \times 14}{80} \times 100 = 35\%$$

$$\text{percentage purity} = \frac{\text{mass of pure substance}}{\text{total mass of the sample}} \times 100$$

**Example.** A 5.0 g sample of limestone contains 4.5 g of calcium carbonate. Purity = 4.5 ÷ 5.0 × 100 = 90%.

S: Electrolysis uses an electric current to split an ionic compound into simpler substances. The compound must be molten or dissolved so that its ions can move. Positive ions go to the negative cathode and gain electrons; negative ions go to the positive anode and lose electrons.
<<<
## What electrolysis is
!def Electrolysis | The decomposition of an ionic compound, when molten or in aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current.

| Part | What it is |
| **electrolyte** | the molten or aqueous substance that undergoes electrolysis |
| **anode** | the **positive** electrode |
| **cathode** | the **negative** electrode |

Inert electrodes, which do not react, are made of **platinum** or **carbon (graphite)**.

A solid ionic compound cannot be electrolysed: its ions are in fixed positions and cannot move.

## How charge is carried
- **In the external circuit (the wires):** **electrons** move, from the anode, through the power supply, to the cathode.
- **In the electrolyte:** **ions** move. Positive ions (cations) move to the cathode. Negative ions (anions) move to the anode.
- **At the cathode:** positive ions **gain electrons**. This is **reduction**.
- **At the anode:** negative ions **lose electrons**. This is **oxidation**.

!key **OIL RIG**: Oxidation Is Loss of electrons, Reduction Is Gain. Reduction happens at the cathode; oxidation at the anode.

## The general rule
- **Metals or hydrogen** form at the **cathode**.
- **Non-metals** (other than hydrogen) form at the **anode**.

## Molten compounds
A molten binary compound contains only two kinds of ion, so the products are simply its two elements.

### Molten lead(II) bromide, PbBr₂
The ions present are Pb²⁺ and Br⁻.

| Electrode | Product | Observation | Half-equation |
| cathode (−) | **lead** | a silvery-grey liquid metal collects at the bottom | $\mathrm{Pb^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Pb}$ |
| anode (+) | **bromine** | a red-brown gas is given off | $\mathrm{2Br^- \rightarrow Br_2 + 2e^-}$ |

### Predicting any molten binary compound
The metal forms at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode.

- molten sodium chloride → sodium at the cathode, chlorine at the anode
- molten aluminium oxide → aluminium at the cathode, oxygen at the anode
- molten zinc iodide → zinc at the cathode, iodine at the anode

## Aqueous solutions
Water supplies two extra ions: **H⁺** and **OH⁻**. So at each electrode there are two ions competing.

### At the cathode
- If the metal is **more reactive than hydrogen** (potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron), **hydrogen** is produced.
- If the metal is **less reactive than hydrogen** (copper, silver, gold), **the metal** is produced.

### At the anode
- If the solution is a **concentrated** solution of a **halide** (chloride, bromide, iodide), the **halogen** is produced.
- Otherwise (a dilute halide solution, a sulfate or a nitrate), **oxygen** is produced, from the OH⁻ ions.

### Concentrated aqueous sodium chloride (brine)
Ions present: Na⁺, Cl⁻, H⁺, OH⁻.

| Electrode | Product | Observation | Half-equation |
| cathode (−) | **hydrogen** | bubbles of colourless gas | $\mathrm{2H^+ + 2e^- \rightarrow H_2}$ |
| anode (+) | **chlorine** | bubbles of pale yellow-green gas | $\mathrm{2Cl^- \rightarrow Cl_2 + 2e^-}$ |

The Na⁺ and OH⁻ ions are left in the solution, which becomes **sodium hydroxide** solution.

### Dilute sulfuric acid
Ions present: H⁺, SO₄²⁻, OH⁻.

| Electrode | Product | Observation | Half-equation |
| cathode (−) | **hydrogen** | bubbles of colourless gas | $\mathrm{2H^+ + 2e^- \rightarrow H_2}$ |
| anode (+) | **oxygen** | bubbles of colourless gas | $\mathrm{4OH^- \rightarrow O_2 + 2H_2O + 4e^-}$ |

The volume of hydrogen is twice the volume of oxygen.

### Aqueous copper(II) sulfate with inert (carbon) electrodes
Ions present: Cu²⁺, SO₄²⁻, H⁺, OH⁻.

| Electrode | Product | Observation | Half-equation |
| cathode (−) | **copper** | a pink-brown solid coats the cathode | $\mathrm{Cu^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Cu}$ |
| anode (+) | **oxygen** | bubbles of colourless gas | $\mathrm{4OH^- \rightarrow O_2 + 2H_2O + 4e^-}$ |

The **blue colour of the solution fades**, because Cu²⁺ ions are removed from it.

### Aqueous copper(II) sulfate with copper electrodes
The copper anode is not inert: it takes part.

| Electrode | What happens | Observation | Half-equation |
| cathode (−) | copper is deposited | the cathode **gains mass** | $\mathrm{Cu^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Cu}$ |
| anode (+) | the copper anode dissolves | the anode **loses mass** | $\mathrm{Cu \rightarrow Cu^{2+} + 2e^-}$ |

The **blue colour stays the same**: Cu²⁺ ions enter the solution at the anode as fast as they leave it at the cathode. This is how copper is purified.

### Halide solutions: dilute or concentrated
| Solution | Cathode | Anode |
| concentrated aqueous sodium chloride | hydrogen | **chlorine** |
| dilute aqueous sodium chloride | hydrogen | **oxygen** |
| concentrated aqueous potassium bromide | hydrogen | bromine |
| concentrated aqueous copper(II) chloride | copper | chlorine |

## Writing half-equations
- At the cathode, electrons are on the **left**: ions gain electrons (reduction).
- At the anode, electrons are on the **right**: ions lose electrons (oxidation).
- The number of electrons equals the charge on the ion, multiplied by the number of ions.
- Check that the charges balance on both sides.

## Electroplating
Electroplating coats an object with a thin layer of a metal. It is done to:

- **improve appearance**
- **improve resistance to corrosion**.

### How to electroplate an object
| Part of the cell | What to use |
| **cathode** (−) | the **object to be plated** |
| **anode** (+) | the **plating metal** |
| **electrolyte** | an aqueous solution of a **salt of the plating metal** |

To silver-plate a spoon: the spoon is the cathode, the anode is pure silver, and the electrolyte is aqueous silver nitrate.

- At the anode, silver atoms lose electrons and go into solution: $\mathrm{Ag \rightarrow Ag^+ + e^-}$.
- At the cathode, silver ions gain electrons and are deposited on the spoon: $\mathrm{Ag^+ + e^- \rightarrow Ag}$.

## What a fuel cell does
!key A hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell uses hydrogen and oxygen to produce electricity, with water as the only chemical product.

$$\mathrm{2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O}$$

In an engine, a fuel is burned and the heat is used to drive pistons. In a fuel cell, the hydrogen and oxygen never burn. They react at two electrodes, and the energy is released as electricity.

- Hydrogen is supplied to one electrode and oxygen (from the air) to the other.
- At the hydrogen electrode, hydrogen loses electrons (it is oxidised).
- The electrons travel through the external circuit, where they do useful work, for example driving an electric motor.
- At the oxygen electrode, oxygen gains electrons (it is reduced), and water is formed.

The cell keeps producing electricity for as long as hydrogen and oxygen are supplied.

## Fuel cells and petrol engines compared
### Advantages of hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells
- **Water is the only product.** No carbon dioxide is released when the vehicle runs, so it does not add to global warming at the point of use.
- **No pollutants** such as carbon monoxide, oxides of nitrogen or particulates are produced.
- They are **more efficient**: more of the energy of the fuel is transferred usefully, because there are fewer stages and less energy is lost as heat.
- They are **quiet** and have fewer moving parts.
- Hydrogen can be made from water, a renewable resource. Petrol comes from petroleum, which is finite.

### Disadvantages
- **Hydrogen is difficult to store.** It is a gas, so it must be compressed into strong, heavy, expensive pressurised tanks, or liquefied at a very low temperature.
- **Hydrogen is flammable and forms explosive mixtures with air**, so there is a safety risk.
- **Hydrogen is expensive to produce**, and making it uses energy. Most is made from methane or by electrolysis using electricity from fossil fuels, which releases carbon dioxide.
- There are **few hydrogen filling stations**.
- Fuel cells are **expensive**: the electrodes contain a platinum catalyst.

| | Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell | Petrol engine |
| Products | water only | carbon dioxide, water, carbon monoxide, oxides of nitrogen |
| Efficiency | higher | lower |
| Storing the fuel | difficult: a gas | easy: a liquid |
| Refuelling | few stations | stations everywhere |
| Source of fuel | water or methane | petroleum (finite) |
