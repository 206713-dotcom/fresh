### Cracking
Cracking breaks large alkane molecules from the heavier fractions of crude oil into **smaller, more useful alkanes and alkenes of lower $M_r$**.

- The shorter alkanes are in greater demand, as fuels such as petrol.
- The alkenes are feedstock for polymers and other chemicals.

## Why alkanes are unreactive
- The **C–C and C–H bonds are strong**, so a lot of energy is needed to break them.
- The bonds are **non-polar** (carbon and hydrogen have similar electronegativities), so alkanes are **not attacked by polar reagents**, nucleophiles or electrophiles.

## Free-radical substitution
Alkanes react with **Cl₂ or Br₂** in **ultraviolet light**. A hydrogen atom is replaced by a halogen atom.

$$\mathrm{C_2H_6 + Cl_2 \rightarrow C_2H_5Cl + HCl}$$

ethane + chlorine → chloroethane + hydrogen chloride

### The mechanism
**Initiation.** UV light breaks the Cl–Cl bond by **homolytic fission**, forming two chlorine free radicals.

$$\mathrm{Cl_2 \rightarrow 2Cl^\bullet}$$

**Propagation.** Two steps, which repeat thousands of times. A radical is used and another is made.

$$\mathrm{Cl^\bullet + C_2H_6 \rightarrow {}^\bullet C_2H_5 + HCl}$$

$$\mathrm{{}^\bullet C_2H_5 + Cl_2 \rightarrow C_2H_5Cl + Cl^\bullet}$$

**Termination.** Two radicals combine. Any pair is possible.

$$\mathrm{Cl^\bullet + Cl^\bullet \rightarrow Cl_2}$$

$$\mathrm{{}^\bullet C_2H_5 + Cl^\bullet \rightarrow C_2H_5Cl}$$

$$\mathrm{{}^\bullet C_2H_5 + {}^\bullet C_2H_5 \rightarrow C_4H_{10}}$$

### A mixture of products
- **Further substitution** gives C₂H₄Cl₂, C₂H₃Cl₃ and so on, as chlorine radicals attack the product. Using **excess alkane** reduces this.
- **Termination** gives butane.
- In longer alkanes, substitution can happen at **different positions**, giving isomers.

So the method gives a poor yield of any one halogenoalkane.

## Test for a C=C bond
Shake with **aqueous bromine**. The colour changes from **orange to colourless**.

## Mechanism: bromine and ethene
- The C=C bond is a region of **high electron density**. As a bromine molecule approaches, the π electrons **repel** the electrons in the Br–Br bond, **inducing a dipole**: Br^δ+–Br^δ−.
- A **pair of π electrons** forms a bond to the **δ+ bromine** atom. The Br–Br bond breaks by **heterolytic fission**, giving a **carbocation** and a **bromide ion**.
- The **lone pair** on the bromide ion forms a bond to the positive carbon.

!mol Electrophilic addition of bromine to ethene
 H2C = CH2        H2C - CH2+        H2C - CH2
     |      ->      |         ->      |   |
  d+ Br             Br   :Br-         Br  Br
     |
  d- Br
 arrows: C=C bond to Br(d+); Br-Br bond to
 Br(d-); then lone pair of Br- to C+
!end

Bromine acts as an **electrophile**: it accepts a pair of electrons.

## Mechanism: hydrogen bromide and propene
H–Br has a permanent dipole, H^δ+–Br^δ−. The π electrons attack the δ+ hydrogen; the H–Br bond breaks heterolytically; then Br⁻ attacks the carbocation.

Propene is unsymmetrical, so the hydrogen can add to either carbon of the double bond, giving two possible carbocations.

| Hydrogen adds to | Carbocation | Product |
| the CH₂ carbon (C1) | CH₃C⁺HCH₃, **secondary** | **2-bromopropane: major product** |
| the CH carbon (C2) | CH₃CH₂C⁺H₂, **primary** | 1-bromopropane: minor product |

## Stability of carbocations
$$\text{tertiary} > \text{secondary} > \text{primary}$$

**Explanation:** alkyl groups are **electron-donating**: they have a **positive inductive effect**, pushing electron density towards the positive carbon and spreading out (stabilising) its charge. The **more alkyl groups** attached to the C⁺, the **more stable** the carbocation.

!key **Markovnikov addition:** when HX adds to an unsymmetrical alkene, the major product is formed through the **more stable carbocation**. The hydrogen adds to the carbon of the double bond that already has more hydrogens.

## Nucleophilic substitution
The halogen is more electronegative than carbon, so the bond is polar: C^δ+–X^δ−. A **nucleophile** attacks the **δ+ carbon** and replaces the halogen.

| Reagent | Conditions | Product | Equation for bromoethane |

## The SN2 mechanism: primary halogenoalkanes
**One step.** S = substitution, N = nucleophilic, 2 = two species in the rate-determining step.

- The nucleophile uses its **lone pair** to attack the **δ+ carbon**, from the side **opposite** the halogen.
- **At the same time**, the C–X bond breaks, and the halogen leaves as X⁻ with the bonding pair.
- The reaction passes through a **transition state** in which the carbon is partly bonded to both the nucleophile and the halogen.

!mol SN2: hydroxide ion with bromoethane
          CH3                  CH3
           |                    |
 HO:-  +   C - Br    ->    HO - C   + :Br-
          / \                  / \
         H   H                H   H
 arrows: lone pair of OH- to C(d+);
 C-Br bond to Br
!end

## The SN1 mechanism: tertiary halogenoalkanes
**Two steps.** 1 = one species in the rate-determining step.

- **Step 1 (slow):** the C–X bond breaks by **heterolytic fission**, forming a **carbocation** and a halide ion.

$$\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CBr \rightarrow (CH_3)_3C^+ + Br^-}$$

- **Step 2 (fast):** the nucleophile attacks the carbocation.

$$\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C^+ + OH^- \rightarrow (CH_3)_3COH}$$

## Which mechanism?
| Halogenoalkane | Mechanism | Reason |
| **primary** | **SN2** | a primary carbocation would be unstable; the δ+ carbon is easy for the nucleophile to reach |
| **tertiary** | **SN1** | the tertiary carbocation is **stabilised by the inductive effect of three electron-donating alkyl groups**; the bulky groups also block attack on the carbon |
| **secondary** | **a mixture** of SN1 and SN2, depending on structure | |

## Reactivity of different halogenoalkanes

Q: [3] Describe the SN2 mechanism for the reaction of bromoethane with aqueous hydroxide ions.
+ the lone pair of the hydroxide ion attacks the δ+ carbon atom :: lone pair|attack
+ the C–Br bond breaks at the same time (one step; a transition state) :: same time|one step|single step|simultaneous|transition
+ bromide ion leaves; ethanol is formed :: bromide|Br-|leaves|ethanol
A: The lone pair of the hydroxide ion attacks the delta-positive carbon atom. At the same time the C–Br bond breaks, in a single step through a transition state. A bromide ion leaves and ethanol is formed.
Q: [3] Explain why 2-bromo-2-methylpropane reacts by the SN1 mechanism but 1-bromobutane reacts by the SN2 mechanism.
+ 2-bromo-2-methylpropane is tertiary and forms a tertiary carbocation :: tertiary
+ which is stabilised by the inductive effect of three electron-donating alkyl groups :: alkyl|inductive|donat
+ 1-bromobutane is primary; a primary carbocation is too unstable to form :: primary
A: 2-bromo-2-methylpropane is a tertiary halogenoalkane. It forms a tertiary carbocation, which is stabilised by the inductive effect of three electron-donating alkyl groups. 1-bromobutane is primary, and a primary carbocation would be too unstable, so the nucleophile attacks in a single step.
Q: [3] Describe and explain an experiment that compares the rates of hydrolysis of 1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane and 1-iodobutane.
+ warm each with aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol and time the appearance of the precipitate :: silver nitrate
+ 1-iodobutane gives a precipitate fastest (then bromo, then chloro) :: fastest|quickest|first
+ because the C–I bond is the weakest :: weak|bond energy|bond strength
A: Warm each halogenoalkane with aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol and time how long the precipitate takes to appear. 1-iodobutane gives its yellow precipitate fastest, then 1-bromobutane, then 1-chlorobutane, because the C–I bond is the weakest and breaks most easily.
