## Hydrocarbons and functional groups
!def Hydrocarbon | A compound made up of carbon and hydrogen atoms only.

!def Functional group | An atom or group of atoms that dictates the physical and chemical properties of a compound.

### Skeletal formulae
- Each **end of a line** and each **corner** is a **carbon atom**.
- **Hydrogen atoms on carbon are not shown.** Each carbon has enough hydrogens to make four bonds.
- Atoms other than C and H are written in, with any hydrogen attached to them: OH, NH₂, Cl, O.
- A double line is a double bond.

!mol Skeletal formulae: a carbon at each line end and corner

              CH3CH2CH2CH2OH
   |
  /\/        2-methylbutane
              CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3
!end

To get a molecular formula from a skeletal formula: count the corners and line ends for carbon, then give each carbon enough hydrogens for four bonds.

## Naming organic compounds
- **Stem:** the longest carbon chain that contains the functional group.

| Carbons | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| Stem | meth- | eth- | prop- | but- | pent- | hex- |

- **Ending:** the functional group (-ane, -ene, -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid, -nitrile, -amine).
- **Number** the chain from the end that gives the functional group the **lowest number**.
- **Side chains** (methyl, ethyl) and **halogens** go at the front, with their positions, in **alphabetical order**.
- Use **di-, tri-** for two or three of the same group.
- Numbers are separated from each other by commas, and from letters by hyphens.

## Families of compounds
| Term | Meaning |
| **homologous series** | a family of compounds with the same functional group and general formula, in which each member differs from the next by CH₂ |
| **saturated** | contains only single carbon–carbon bonds |
| **unsaturated** | contains at least one carbon–carbon double or triple bond |

## Breaking a covalent bond
| | Homolytic fission | Heterolytic fission |
| What happens | the bond breaks **evenly**: **each atom takes one electron** | the bond breaks **unevenly**: **one atom takes both electrons** |
| Products | two **free radicals** | a **cation and an anion** |
| Example | $\mathrm{Cl{-}Cl \rightarrow Cl^\bullet + Cl^\bullet}$ | $\mathrm{H{-}Cl \rightarrow H^+ + Cl^-}$ |

!def Free radical | A species with one or more unpaired electrons.

A radical is shown with a dot: Cl•, •CH₃. Radicals are very reactive.

### Steps in a radical chain reaction
| Step | What happens |
| **initiation** | radicals are **formed** from a molecule (homolytic fission) |
| **propagation** | a radical reacts with a molecule to give a **new radical** and a molecule: the chain continues |
| **termination** | **two radicals combine** to form a molecule: the chain stops |

## Attacking species
!def Nucleophile | An electron-pair donor.

A nucleophile is attracted to a region of **low electron density** (a δ+ carbon). It has a **lone pair**, and often a negative charge: OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, H₂O.

!def Electrophile | An electron-pair acceptor.

An electrophile is attracted to a region of **high electron density**, such as a C=C bond. It is positive or δ+: H⁺, Br^δ+ in Br₂, H^δ+ in HBr.

"Nucleophilic" and "electrophilic" describe the reactions in which these species attack.

## Types of reaction
| Type | Meaning | Example |
| **addition** | two molecules join to form **one product** | ethene + bromine → 1,2-dibromoethane |
| **substitution** | one atom or group is **replaced** by another | bromoethane + OH⁻ → ethanol + Br⁻ |
| **elimination** | a small molecule is **removed** from a larger one, forming a double bond | ethanol → ethene + water |
| **hydrolysis** | a compound is **broken down by water** (often with acid or alkali) | ester + water → acid + alcohol |
| **condensation** | two molecules join, with the **loss of a small molecule** such as water | acid + alcohol → ester + water |
| **oxidation** | gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen | ethanol → ethanal |
| **reduction** | gain of hydrogen or loss of oxygen | ethanal → ethanol |

### [O] and [H]
In organic redox equations, **[O]** stands for one atom of oxygen from an oxidising agent and **[H]** for one atom of hydrogen from a reducing agent.

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH + [O] \rightarrow CH_3CHO + H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_3CHO + 2[H] \rightarrow CH_3CH_2OH}$$

The equation must still balance.

## The four AS mechanisms
| Mechanism | Who reacts | Example |
| **free-radical substitution** | alkanes with halogens in UV light | CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl |
| **electrophilic addition** | alkenes with Br₂, HBr | CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br |
| **nucleophilic substitution** | halogenoalkanes with OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃ | CH₃CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂OH + Br⁻ |
| **nucleophilic addition** | aldehydes and ketones with HCN | CH₃CHO + HCN → CH₃CH(OH)CN |

The name has two parts: the **kind of attacking species**, then the **type of reaction**.

<<<
## Carbon skeletons
| Type | Meaning | Example |
| **straight-chained** | the carbon atoms are joined in one continuous chain | butane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ |
| **branched** | one or more carbon side chains are attached to the main chain | methylpropane, CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃ |
| **cyclic** | the carbon atoms are joined in a ring | cyclohexane, C₆H₁₂ |

Cyclohexane has the same molecular formula as hexene, C₆H₁₂, but has no double bond.

## Hybridisation, shape and bond angle
| Carbon atom | Hybridisation | σ bonds | π bonds | Shape around the carbon | Bond angle |
| four single bonds: alkanes | **sp³** | 4 | 0 | **tetrahedral** | **109.5°** |
| one double bond: alkenes, C=O | **sp²** | 3 | 1 | **trigonal planar** | **120°** |
| one triple bond (or two double bonds): –C≡N, CO₂ | **sp** | 2 | 2 | **linear** | **180°** |

**Explanation of the shapes:** the regions of electron density (σ bonds; the π bond lies with its σ bond) repel each other and get as far apart as possible. Four regions: tetrahedral. Three regions: trigonal planar. Two regions: linear.

## σ and π bonds
- A **σ bond** is formed by the **direct overlap** of orbitals along the line between the two nuclei. Atoms can **rotate** about a σ bond.
- A **π bond** is formed by the **sideways overlap of p orbitals**, giving electron density **above and below** the plane of the σ bond.
- A double bond is one σ bond and one π bond. A triple bond is one σ bond and two π bonds.

### Ethane
Each carbon is sp³ hybridised and forms four σ bonds: three to hydrogen (sp³ with 1s) and one to the other carbon (sp³ with sp³). All bond angles are 109.5°. The molecule is not flat, and the two ends can rotate.

### Ethene
- Each carbon is **sp² hybridised**: three sp² orbitals in a plane at 120°, and one **p orbital** at right angles to the plane.
- The sp² orbitals form **three σ bonds**: two C–H and one C–C.
- The two p orbitals **overlap sideways** to form the **π bond**, above and below the plane.

!mol Ethene: all six atoms in one plane
  H           H
    \       /
     C  =  C        all bond angles 120
    /       \
  H           H
!end

!def Planar | All the atoms lie in the same plane.

Ethene is **planar**: both carbons and all four hydrogens lie in one plane. The π bond **prevents rotation** about the C=C bond, because rotating would break the sideways overlap.

### Triple bonds
In HCN, or in the –C≡N group of a nitrile, the carbon is sp hybridised. It forms two σ bonds at 180°, and its two remaining p orbitals form two π bonds at right angles to each other.

S: Isomers share a molecular formula but differ in how their atoms are joined or arranged in space. Structural isomers have different connections. Stereoisomers have the same connections but a different 3D arrangement: cis/trans around a double bond, or mirror images around a chiral carbon.
<<<
## Structural isomerism
!def Structural isomers | Compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae.

| Type | Difference | Example |
| **chain** | the carbon skeleton is arranged differently (straight or branched) | butane and methylpropane, C₄H₁₀ |
| **positional** | the functional group is at a different position on the same skeleton | propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol, C₃H₈O |
| **functional group** | the atoms form a different functional group | propanal and propanone, C₃H₆O; ethanol and methoxymethane, C₂H₆O |

Other functional group pairs: an alkene and a cycloalkane ($\mathrm{C_nH_{2n}}$); a carboxylic acid and an ester.

## Stereoisomerism
!def Stereoisomers | Compounds with the same structural formula but a different arrangement of atoms in space.

There are two kinds: **geometrical (cis/trans)** and **optical**.

## Geometrical (cis/trans) isomerism
It arises in alkenes because there is **restricted rotation about the C=C bond**, due to the **π bond**.

**Two conditions:**

- a C=C double bond (restricted rotation), and
- **each carbon of the double bond has two different groups** attached.

!mol cis- and trans-but-2-ene
 H3C       CH3        H3C       H
    \     /              \     /
     C = C                C = C
    /     \              /     \
   H       H            H       CH3

  cis-but-2-ene        trans-but-2-ene
!end

- **cis:** the two identical groups are on the **same side** of the double bond.
- **trans:** they are on **opposite sides**.

But-1-ene, CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃, has no cis/trans isomers: one carbon of the double bond has two identical hydrogens.

Cis/trans isomers have different physical properties. Restricted rotation in **rings** also gives cis/trans isomers, such as cis- and trans-1,2-dichlorocyclohexane. The E/Z system of naming may be used but is not required.

## Optical isomerism
!def Chiral centre | A carbon atom with four different atoms or groups attached to it.

A molecule with one chiral centre exists as **two optical isomers**, called **enantiomers**, which are **non-superimposable mirror images** of each other.

!mol The two enantiomers of 2-hydroxypropanoic acid
      COOH      |      COOH
       |        |        |
       C        |        C
     / | \      |      / | \
  H3C  H  OH    |   HO   H  CH3
             mirror
!end

When you draw them, show the **tetrahedral shape in 3D**: two bonds in the plane of the paper, one **wedge** (coming forward) and one **dashed** bond (going back). Draw the second isomer as the **mirror image** of the first.

Enantiomers:

- have **identical chemical properties** (with reagents that are not chiral) and identical physical properties
- **rotate the plane of plane-polarised light** in **opposite directions**.

A chiral centre is marked with an asterisk, C*. A molecule can have **more than one** chiral centre.

### Finding chiral centres
Check each carbon in turn. It is a chiral centre only if all **four** groups are **different**. So it cannot be:

- a CH₃ or CH₂ carbon (two or more identical hydrogens)
- a carbon in a C=C or C=O double bond (only three groups).

Compare whole groups, not just the first atom: –CH₃ and –CH₂CH₃ are different.

| Molecule | Chiral centre? |
| butan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ | **yes**: C2 has H, OH, CH₃ and C₂H₅ |
| propan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ | no: two CH₃ groups |
| 2-bromobutane, CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃ | **yes**: C2 |
| 2-methylbutanoic acid | **yes**: C2 |

In a **ring**, a carbon is a chiral centre if it has two different groups attached and the two routes round the ring from it are different.

## Finding all the isomers of a formula
Work systematically.

- Draw the **straight chain**; move the functional group along it (positional isomers).
- **Shorten the chain** by one carbon and add a methyl branch in each possible place (chain isomers).
- Consider a **different functional group** (functional group isomers).
- Then check each structure for **cis/trans** and **optical** isomers.
- Name each one to make sure none is repeated.

**Example: C₄H₉Br** has four structural isomers.

| Name | Structural formula | |
| 1-bromobutane | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Br | primary |
| 2-bromobutane | CH₃CH₂CHBrCH₃ | secondary; has a chiral centre |
| 1-bromo-2-methylpropane | (CH₃)₂CHCH₂Br | primary |
| 2-bromo-2-methylpropane | (CH₃)₃CBr | tertiary |

Q: [2] Explain what is meant by a chiral centre, and state what it gives rise to.
+ a carbon atom with four different atoms or groups attached :: four different|4 different
+ it gives two optical isomers (enantiomers: non-superimposable mirror images) :: optical|enantiomer|mirror
A: A chiral centre is a carbon atom with four different atoms or groups attached. It gives rise to two optical isomers, which are non-superimposable mirror images.
Q: [3] Explain why but-2-ene shows cis/trans isomerism but but-1-ene does not.
+ there is restricted rotation about the C=C bond (because of the π bond) :: rotat
+ in but-2-ene each carbon of the double bond has two different groups (H and CH₃) :: two different|different groups
+ in but-1-ene one carbon of the double bond has two identical hydrogen atoms :: identical|two hydrogen|same|two H
A: There is restricted rotation about the C=C bond. In but-2-ene each carbon of the double bond has two different groups attached, a hydrogen atom and a methyl group. In but-1-ene one carbon of the double bond has two identical hydrogen atoms, so there is only one arrangement.
