## Inside the atom
- An atom is **mostly empty space** surrounding a **very small, dense nucleus**.
- The nucleus contains the **protons** and **neutrons** (the nucleons).
- The **electrons** are found in **shells** in the empty space around the nucleus.

| Particle | Relative charge | Relative mass | Where |
| **proton** | +1 | 1 | nucleus |
| **neutron** | 0 | 1 | nucleus |
| **electron** | −1 | $\frac{1}{1836}$ (negligible) | shells |

**Distribution of mass and charge:** nearly all the **mass** is in the nucleus. The nucleus carries all the **positive charge**; the negative charge is spread through the shells around it. An atom is neutral because it has equal numbers of protons and electrons.

!def Atomic (proton) number | The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.

!def Mass (nucleon) number | The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.

## Counting particles
For $\mathrm{^{A}_{Z}X}$:

- protons = Z
- neutrons = A − Z
- electrons = Z in an atom; **Z − charge** in an ion.

| Species | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
| $\mathrm{^{23}_{11}Na}$ | 11 | 12 | 11 |
| $\mathrm{^{23}_{11}Na^+}$ | 11 | 12 | 10 |
| $\mathrm{^{37}_{17}Cl^-}$ | 17 | 20 | 18 |
| $\mathrm{^{27}_{13}Al^{3+}}$ | 13 | 14 | 10 |

A positive ion has lost electrons; a negative ion has gained them. The nucleus does not change.

## Beams in an electric field
Beams of protons, neutrons and electrons, moving at the **same velocity**, pass between two charged plates.

| Beam | Behaviour | Reason |
| **protons** | deflected towards the **negative** plate, by a small amount | positive charge; large mass |
| **electrons** | deflected towards the **positive** plate, by a **much larger** amount | negative charge; very small mass |
| **neutrons** | **not deflected** | no charge |

The size of the deflection depends on the **charge-to-mass ratio**. Protons and electrons have charges of equal size, but an electron has only $\frac{1}{1836}$ of the mass, so it is deflected much more.

## Atomic radius and ionic radius
### Across a period: atomic radius decreases
- The **nuclear charge increases** (one more proton each time).
- The extra electrons go into the **same shell**, so **shielding stays about the same**.
- The outer electrons are **attracted more strongly** and pulled closer.

### Down a group: atomic radius increases
- Each element has **one more shell** of electrons.
- The outer electrons are **further from the nucleus** and **more shielded** by inner shells. This outweighs the increase in nuclear charge.

### Ionic radius
| Ion | Compared with its atom | Reason |
| **cation** (positive) | **smaller** | it has lost its outer shell; the remaining electrons are attracted by the same nuclear charge |
| **anion** (negative) | **larger** | extra electrons increase the repulsion between electrons, with the same nuclear charge |

Across Period 3:

- Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ (all 2,8): radius **decreases**, because the same number of electrons is attracted by an increasing nuclear charge.
- P³⁻, S²⁻, Cl⁻ (all 2,8,8): much larger than the cations, as they have one more shell; radius again **decreases** from P³⁻ to Cl⁻.

Down a group, ionic radius increases, as the number of shells increases.

Ions with the same number of electrons are called **isoelectronic**.

## What isotopes are
!def Isotopes | Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

### Notation
$$\mathrm{^{x}_{y}A}$$

- **x** is the **mass (nucleon) number**
- **y** is the **atomic (proton) number**
- A is the symbol of the element.

| Isotope | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
| $\mathrm{^{1}_{1}H}$ | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| $\mathrm{^{2}_{1}H}$ | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| $\mathrm{^{35}_{17}Cl}$ | 17 | 18 | 17 |
| $\mathrm{^{37}_{17}Cl}$ | 17 | 20 | 17 |

An isotope can also be written with its name and mass number: carbon-12, chlorine-37.

## Properties of isotopes
### Same chemical properties
Isotopes of the same element have the **same chemical properties**, because they have the **same number of electrons** and the **same electronic configuration**. Chemical reactions involve only the outer electrons.

### Different physical properties
Isotopes of the same element have **different physical properties**, limited to **mass** and **density**, because they have **different numbers of neutrons** and so different masses.

A property that depends on mass differs slightly between isotopes: a sample of $\mathrm{^{2}H_2O}$ ("heavy water") has a density of about 1.11 g cm⁻³, compared with 1.00 g cm⁻³ for ordinary water. Rates of diffusion also differ.

## Isotopes and relative atomic mass
Most elements occur as a mixture of isotopes, so the relative atomic mass is a **weighted mean**.

Chlorine is 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37.

$$A_r = \frac{(75 \times 35) + (25 \times 37)}{100} = 35.5$$

S: Electrons occupy orbitals, grouped into sub-shells (s, p, d) within shells. Each orbital holds two electrons with opposite spins. Electrons fill the lowest-energy sub-shells first, which gives the configuration 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p.

## Shells, sub-shells and orbitals
| Term | Meaning |
| **shell** | a main energy level, numbered by the **principal quantum number, n** (1, 2, 3 …) |
| **sub-shell** | a division of a shell: **s, p, d** |
| **orbital** | a region of space around the nucleus that can hold **up to two electrons**, with opposite spins |
| **ground state** | the **most stable (lowest energy) electronic configuration** of an atom |

| Sub-shell | Number of orbitals | Maximum electrons |
| **s** | 1 | 2 |
| **p** | 3 | 6 |
| **d** | 5 | 10 |

| Shell (n) | Sub-shells | Maximum electrons |
| 1 | 1s | 2 |
| 2 | 2s 2p | 8 |
| 3 | 3s 3p 3d | 18 |
| 4 | 4s 4p 4d 4f | 32 |

## Order of energy
$$1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p$$

The **4s sub-shell is lower in energy than 3d**, so it fills first.

## Rules for filling
- Electrons occupy the **lowest energy** sub-shell available.
- An orbital holds a maximum of **two electrons**, which must have **opposite spins**.
- In a sub-shell, electrons occupy orbitals **singly first**, with parallel spins, before any pair up. This minimises **inter-electron repulsion**: two electrons in the same orbital repel each other (spin-pair repulsion).

## Writing configurations
For iron, Z = 26:

- full: **1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s²**
- shorthand: **[Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²**

## Shapes of orbitals
- An **s orbital** is **spherical**. A 2s orbital is larger than a 1s orbital.
- A **p orbital** is a **dumb-bell** shape, with two lobes, one each side of the nucleus. The three p orbitals in a sub-shell (pₓ, p_y, p_z) lie at **right angles** to each other.

S: Ionisation energy is the energy needed to pull an electron out of a gaseous atom. It depends on nuclear charge, distance, shielding and spin-pair repulsion. Big jumps in successive ionisation energies reveal how many electrons are in the outer shell, and so the group.
<<<
## Definition and equations
!def First ionisation energy, IE | The energy required to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms, to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.

$$\mathrm{Na(g) \rightarrow Na^+(g) + e^-} \qquad \text{first}$$

$$\mathrm{Na^+(g) \rightarrow Na^{2+}(g) + e^-} \qquad \text{second}$$

$$\mathrm{Na^{2+}(g) \rightarrow Na^{3+}(g) + e^-} \qquad \text{third}$$

Every species is **gaseous**, and each equation removes **one** electron. The n-th ionisation energy starts from the (n−1)+ ion. Ionisation energies are always **endothermic** (positive), with units kJ mol⁻¹.

## What decides the size of an ionisation energy
Ionisation energy is due to the **attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron**. Four factors affect it.

| Factor | Effect |
| **nuclear charge** | more protons: stronger attraction: **higher** IE |
| **atomic / ionic radius** | outer electron further from the nucleus: weaker attraction: **lower** IE |
| **shielding** by inner shells and sub-shells | more inner electrons repel the outer electron: **lower** IE |
| **spin-pair repulsion** | two electrons in the same orbital repel: the electron is easier to remove: **lower** IE |

## Trends
### Down a group: first IE decreases
- The outer electron is in a shell **further from the nucleus**.
- There is **more shielding** from the extra inner shells.
- These outweigh the increased nuclear charge, so the attraction is weaker.

### Across a period: first IE increases (in general)
- The **nuclear charge increases**.
- The electrons are in the **same shell**, so the **shielding is similar**, and the radius decreases.
- The attraction for the outer electron is stronger.

### The two dips across Period 2 and Period 3
| Dip | Reason |
| **Be → B** and **Mg → Al** | the outer electron of B (or Al) is in a **p sub-shell**, which is **higher in energy** than the s sub-shell and is **shielded by the s electrons**; it is easier to remove |
| **N → O** and **P → S** | in O (or S) the electron removed is one of a **pair in the same p orbital**; **spin-pair repulsion** makes it easier to remove |

There is a large fall from the end of one period to the start of the next (Ne → Na), because the electron removed is in a new shell, further from the nucleus and more shielded.

## Successive ionisation energies
The successive ionisation energies of an element always **increase**: each electron is removed from an ion with a greater positive charge, and the remaining electrons are held more tightly, as there is less repulsion between them.

- A **large jump** shows that the next electron is being removed from a **shell closer to the nucleus**, with much less shielding.
- The number of electrons removed **before the first large jump** is the number of **outer-shell electrons**, which gives the **group**.

**Example.** Successive ionisation energies in kJ mol⁻¹: 578, 1817, 2745, 11 577, 14 842.

The large jump is between the third and fourth. Three electrons are in the outer shell: the element is in **Group 13** (it is aluminium).

### Reading a full set
For sodium, the pattern is 1 electron (easy), then 8 (harder), then 2 (very hard): this shows the configuration 2,8,1. Smaller steps within a shell show the sub-shells.

## The standard
!def Unified atomic mass unit | One twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

## Four relative masses
!def Relative atomic mass, Ar | The weighted mean mass of the atoms of an element, relative to one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom (the unified atomic mass unit).

!def Relative isotopic mass | The mass of an atom of one isotope, relative to one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

!def Relative molecular mass, Mr | The weighted mean mass of a molecule, relative to one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

## Calculating relative atomic mass
$$A_r = \frac{\sum (\text{isotopic mass} \times \text{percentage abundance})}{100}$$

**Example.** Magnesium: 79.0% ²⁴Mg, 10.0% ²⁵Mg, 11.0% ²⁶Mg.

$$A_r = \frac{(79.0 \times 24) + (10.0 \times 25) + (11.0 \times 26)}{100} = \frac{2432}{100} = 24.3$$

If relative abundances are given instead of percentages, divide by the total abundance.

### Finding an abundance
Boron has two isotopes, ¹⁰B and ¹¹B, and $A_r$ = 10.8. Let the percentage of ¹¹B be x.

$$\frac{11x + 10(100 - x)}{100} = 10.8$$

$$x + 1000 = 1080 \qquad x = 80$$

Boron is 80% ¹¹B and 20% ¹⁰B.

## Molar mass
The **molar mass, M**, is the mass of one mole of a substance, in **g mol⁻¹**. It has the same number as the relative atomic, molecular or formula mass.

$$n = \frac{m}{M}$$

## Equations
- A **balanced equation** has the same number of each kind of atom on both sides.

### Ionic equations
An ionic equation shows only the species that change. **Spectator ions**, which are unchanged, are left out.

- Write the full balanced equation.
- Split every **aqueous ionic** substance into its ions. Keep solids, liquids, gases and covalent molecules whole.
- Cancel the ions that appear unchanged on both sides.
- Check that atoms **and charges** balance.

## Empirical and molecular formulae
!def Empirical formula | The simplest whole number ratio of the atoms of each element in a compound.

!def Molecular formula | The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule of a compound.

### Finding an empirical formula
- Write the **mass or percentage** of each element.
- **Divide by $A_r$** to get moles.
- **Divide by the smallest** number.
- If needed, multiply to get **whole numbers** (×2 for .5, ×3 for .33 or .67).

**Example.** A compound contains 40.0% C, 6.7% H and 53.3% O.

| | C | H | O |
| % | 40.0 | 6.7 | 53.3 |
| ÷ $A_r$ | 3.33 | 6.7 | 3.33 |
| ÷ smallest | 1 | 2 | 1 |

Empirical formula: **CH₂O**.

### From empirical to molecular formula
$$\text{multiplier} = \frac{M_r}{\text{empirical formula mass}}$$

If $M_r$ = 180: 180 ÷ 30 = 6, so the molecular formula is **C₆H₁₂O₆**.

### From combustion data
When a hydrocarbon burns, all its carbon goes to CO₂ and all its hydrogen to H₂O.

- moles of C = moles of CO₂
- moles of H = 2 × moles of H₂O

## The three conversions
| Quantity | Equation | Units |
| mass | $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$ | g, g mol⁻¹ |
| gas volume at room conditions | $n = \dfrac{V}{24.0}$ | V in dm³ |

- The molar gas volume is **24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹** at room conditions.
- Concentration in g dm⁻³ = concentration in mol dm⁻³ × M.

## The route
- Write the **balanced equation**.
- Change the known quantity into **moles**.
- Use the **mole ratio** from the equation.
- Change the moles of the wanted substance into mass, volume or concentration.

## Reacting masses
**Example.** What mass of calcium oxide is formed when 25.0 g of calcium carbonate decomposes?

$$\mathrm{CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2}$$

- ratio 1 : 1, so n(CaO) = 0.250 mol
- m(CaO) = 0.250 × 56.1 = **14.0 g**

## Percentage yield
$$\text{percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100$$

If only 11.2 g of CaO is collected above: 11.2 ÷ 14.0 × 100 = **80.0%**.

Yields are below 100% because of incomplete or reversible reactions, side reactions, and losses on transferring and purifying.

## Volumes of gases
**Avogadro's law:** equal volumes of gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules. So for gases, the **mole ratio is also the volume ratio**.

**Example.** 20 cm³ of propane burns completely.

$$\mathrm{C_3H_8(g) + 5O_2(g) \rightarrow 3CO_2(g) + 4H_2O(l)}$$

- volume of O₂ needed = 5 × 20 = 100 cm³
- volume of CO₂ formed = 3 × 20 = 60 cm³

### Finding a hydrocarbon formula
$$\mathrm{C_xH_y + \left(x + \tfrac{y}{4}\right)O_2 \rightarrow xCO_2 + \tfrac{y}{2}H_2O}$$

10 cm³ of a hydrocarbon needs 50 cm³ of oxygen and gives 30 cm³ of CO₂ (water is liquid).

- CO₂ : hydrocarbon = 3 : 1, so x = 3
- O₂ : hydrocarbon = 5 : 1, so x + y/4 = 5 and y = 8
- The hydrocarbon is **C₃H₈**.

- c(NaOH) = 4.30 × 10⁻³ ÷ (25.0 ÷ 1000) = **0.172 mol dm⁻³**

## Limiting and excess reagents
!def Limiting reagent | The reactant that is completely used up, and so determines the amount of product formed.

**Example.** 2.43 g of magnesium is added to 50.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid.

$$\mathrm{Mg + 2HCl \rightarrow MgCl_2 + H_2}$$

- n(HCl) = 2.00 × 0.0500 = 0.100 mol: not enough
- **HCl is limiting**; magnesium is in excess
- n(H₂) = 0.100 ÷ 2 = 0.0500 mol; volume = 0.0500 × 24.0 = **1.20 dm³**

Work out the moles of each substance that reacted and find their simplest ratio. That ratio gives the balancing numbers in the equation.

If 0.010 mol of a metal carbonate reacts with exactly 0.020 mol of HCl, the ratio is 1 : 2, so the carbonate is of the type MCO₃.

Q: [2] Explain what is meant by the term limiting reagent.
+ the reactant that is completely used up (not in excess) :: used up|runs|consumed|finish
+ it determines (limits) the amount of product formed :: product|determin|limit
A: The limiting reagent is the reactant that is completely used up in the reaction. It determines the amount of product that is formed.

+ the reaction is incomplete or reversible, or there are side reactions :: incomplete|reversible|side|equilibrium
+ product is lost during transfer, separation or purification :: lost|loss|transfer|purif|filter
A: The reaction may be reversible or incomplete, or side reactions may occur. Some product is also lost when it is transferred and purified.
