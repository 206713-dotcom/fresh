## Predicting the products
### Molten electrolytes
Only the ions of the compound are present.

- **Cathode (−):** the metal. $\mathrm{Pb^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Pb}$
- **Anode (+):** the non-metal. $\mathrm{2Br^- \rightarrow Br_2 + 2e^-}$

### Aqueous electrolytes
Water also provides H⁺ and OH⁻ ions (or water molecules themselves are oxidised or reduced), so there is competition. Three things decide the product: the **state** of the electrolyte, the **position of the ions in the redox series** (their E⦵ values), and the **concentration**.

**At the cathode: reduction.** The species with the **more positive E⦵** is reduced more easily.

| Cation | E⦵ compared with H⁺/H₂ (0.00 V) | Product |
| Cu²⁺, Ag⁺ | more positive | the **metal** |
| Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, Zn²⁺ | much more negative | **hydrogen**: $\mathrm{2H^+ + 2e^- \rightarrow H_2}$ |

**At the anode: oxidation.**

| Anion | Product |
| SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻ | **oxygen**: $\mathrm{4OH^- \rightarrow O_2 + 2H_2O + 4e^-}$ |
| Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ in **concentrated** solution | the **halogen**: $\mathrm{2Cl^- \rightarrow Cl_2 + 2e^-}$ |
| Cl⁻ in **dilute** solution | mainly **oxygen** |

On E⦵ values alone, hydroxide ions (water) should be oxidised in preference to chloride. But in a concentrated solution the high concentration of Cl⁻ favours its discharge: **concentration can override the redox series**.

| Electrolyte (inert electrodes) | Cathode | Anode |
| molten NaCl | sodium | chlorine |
| concentrated NaCl(aq) | hydrogen | chlorine |
| dilute NaCl(aq) | hydrogen | oxygen |
| CuSO₄(aq) | copper | oxygen |
| dilute H₂SO₄ | hydrogen | oxygen |

## Charge
$$Q = It$$

Q is charge in coulombs, I is current in amperes, t is time in **seconds**.

## The Faraday constant
!def Faraday constant, F | The charge carried by one mole of electrons: 96 500 C mol⁻¹.

$$F = Le$$

L is the Avogadro constant (6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹) and e is the charge on one electron (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C).

## Calculating the mass or volume released
- Find the charge: **Q = It**.
- Find the moles of electrons: **n(e⁻) = Q ÷ F**.
- Use the **half-equation** to find the moles of product.
- Convert to mass (× M) or to gas volume (× 24.0 dm³ at room conditions).

**Example 1.** A current of 0.500 A passes through aqueous copper(II) sulfate for 30.0 minutes. Find the mass of copper deposited.

- Q = 0.500 × (30.0 × 60) = 900 C
- n(e⁻) = 900 ÷ 96 500 = 9.33 × 10⁻³ mol
- Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu, so n(Cu) = 4.66 × 10⁻³ mol
- mass = 4.66 × 10⁻³ × 63.5 = **0.296 g**

**Example 2.** Find the volume of oxygen formed at the anode in the same experiment.

- 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻, so n(O₂) = 9.33 × 10⁻³ ÷ 4 = 2.33 × 10⁻³ mol
- volume = 2.33 × 10⁻³ × 24.0 = **0.0560 dm³** (56.0 cm³)

Working backwards, the same steps give the time or current needed for a given mass.

## Finding the Avogadro constant by electrolysis
- Electrolyse aqueous copper(II) sulfate with **copper electrodes**.
- Weigh the cathode before and after (rinse and dry it first). Measure the **current** with an ammeter, keep it constant with a variable resistor, and measure the **time**.
- Calculate the charge passed: Q = It.
- From the **mass of copper deposited**, find the moles of copper, and so the moles of electrons (× 2).
- **F = Q ÷ moles of electrons.**
- **L = F ÷ e**, using the charge on the electron, 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

**Example.** 0.200 A for 2400 s deposits 0.158 g of copper.

- Q = 480 C
- n(Cu) = 0.158 ÷ 63.5 = 2.49 × 10⁻³ mol; n(e⁻) = 4.98 × 10⁻³ mol
- F = 480 ÷ 4.98 × 10⁻³ = 96 400 C mol⁻¹
- L = 96 400 ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **6.03 × 10²³ mol⁻¹**

S: Every half-cell has an electrode potential that measures how readily it gains electrons, compared with the standard hydrogen electrode. Join two half-cells and the difference is the cell voltage. A positive cell potential means the reaction is feasible, and the Nernst equation corrects for non-standard concentrations.

## Definitions
!def Standard electrode potential, E⦵ | The voltage produced when a standard half-cell is connected to a standard hydrogen electrode under standard conditions.

!def Standard cell potential, E⦵cell | The difference in standard electrode potential between two half-cells.

**Standard conditions:** 298 K; 101 kPa for gases; 1.00 mol dm⁻³ for ions in solution.

Electrode potentials are written as **reductions**: oxidised form + electrons ⇌ reduced form.

$$\mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons Cu(s)} \qquad E^\ominus = +0.34\ \text{V}$$

## The standard hydrogen electrode
- **Hydrogen gas at 101 kPa** bubbles over
- a **platinum electrode** (coated with platinum black), dipping into
- a solution containing **1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺(aq)**, at **298 K**.

$$\mathrm{2H^+(aq) + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons H_2(g)} \qquad E^\ominus = 0.00\ \text{V (by definition)}$$

Platinum is inert and conducts, and it provides a surface on which the equilibrium is set up.

## Measuring E⦵
Connect the half-cell to a standard hydrogen electrode through a **high-resistance voltmeter** and a **salt bridge**.

| Half-cell | How it is built |
| **metal / metal ion**, e.g. Cu²⁺/Cu | a rod of the **metal** in a **1.00 mol dm⁻³** solution of its ions |
| **non-metal / non-metal ion**, e.g. Cl₂/Cl⁻ | the **gas at 101 kPa** bubbling over a **platinum** electrode in a 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solution of its ions |
| **ions of the same element in different oxidation states**, e.g. Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ | a **platinum** electrode in a solution containing **both ions, each at 1.00 mol dm⁻³** |

- The **salt bridge** (filter paper soaked in saturated potassium nitrate) **completes the circuit** by letting ions move between the solutions, keeping the charges balanced. It must not react with either solution.
- The **high-resistance voltmeter** means that almost no current flows, so the maximum potential difference is measured.

## Cell potential
$$E^\ominus_{cell} = E^\ominus(\text{more positive half-cell}) - E^\ominus(\text{more negative half-cell})$$

| Half-equation | E⦵ / V |
| $\mathrm{Zn^{2+} + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons Zn}$ | −0.76 |
| $\mathrm{Fe^{2+} + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons Fe}$ | −0.44 |
| $\mathrm{2H^+ + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons H_2}$ | 0.00 |
| $\mathrm{Cu^{2+} + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons Cu}$ | +0.34 |
| $\mathrm{I_2 + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons 2I^-}$ | +0.54 |
| $\mathrm{Fe^{3+} + e^- \rightleftharpoons Fe^{2+}}$ | +0.77 |
| $\mathrm{Ag^+ + e^- \rightleftharpoons Ag}$ | +0.80 |
| $\mathrm{Br_2 + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons 2Br^-}$ | +1.07 |
| $\mathrm{Cl_2 + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons 2Cl^-}$ | +1.36 |
| $\mathrm{MnO_4^- + 8H^+ + 5e^- \rightleftharpoons Mn^{2+} + 4H_2O}$ | +1.52 |

**Zinc–copper cell:** E⦵cell = +0.34 − (−0.76) = **+1.10 V**.

### Polarity and electron flow
- The half-cell with the **more positive E⦵** is the **positive electrode**: **reduction** happens there.
- The half-cell with the **more negative E⦵** is the **negative electrode**: **oxidation** happens there (its half-equation runs backwards).
- **Electrons flow** through the external circuit **from the negative electrode to the positive electrode**: from zinc to copper.

$$\mathrm{Zn + Cu^{2+} \rightarrow Zn^{2+} + Cu}$$

## Oxidising and reducing agents
- The **more positive** the E⦵, the **stronger the oxidising agent** on the **left** of the half-equation. MnO₄⁻ (in acid) and Cl₂ are strong oxidising agents.
- The **more negative** the E⦵, the **stronger the reducing agent** on the **right**. Zinc is a strong reducing agent.

## Predicting feasibility
A reaction is **feasible if E⦵cell is positive**.

Quick rule: the species on the **left** of the half-equation with the **more positive E⦵** will oxidise the species on the **right** of the half-equation with the more negative E⦵.

**Example.** Will chlorine oxidise Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺?

- Cl₂/Cl⁻ is +1.36 V; Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ is +0.77 V.
- E⦵cell = +1.36 − (+0.77) = **+0.59 V**: feasible.

$$\mathrm{Cl_2 + 2Fe^{2+} \rightarrow 2Cl^- + 2Fe^{3+}}$$

**Will iodine oxidise Fe²⁺?** E⦵cell = +0.54 − 0.77 = −0.23 V: not feasible. (The reverse is: Fe³⁺ oxidises I⁻.)

### Building the equation
- Write the half-equation with the more positive E⦵ as a **reduction**.
- **Reverse** the other half-equation.
- **Multiply** so the electrons are equal, then add. Do **not** multiply the E⦵ values.

### Limits of the prediction
- A feasible reaction may be **too slow** to see: a high activation energy.
- The conditions may **not be standard**: concentrations and temperature change the potentials.

## The effect of concentration
Apply Le Chatelier's principle to the half-equation.

$$\mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons Cu(s)}$$

- **Increasing [Cu²⁺]** moves the equilibrium to the right: electrons are removed, so E becomes **more positive**.
- **Decreasing [Cu²⁺]** makes E **less positive** (more negative).

In general, raising the concentration of the oxidised form (left side) makes E more positive; raising the concentration of the reduced form makes it more negative.

## The Nernst equation
$$E = E^\ominus + \frac{0.059}{z}\log\frac{[\text{oxidised species}]}{[\text{reduced species}]}$$

z is the number of electrons transferred. Solids are not included.

**Copper:**

$$E = 0.34 + \frac{0.059}{2}\log[\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}]$$

If [Cu²⁺] = 0.010 mol dm⁻³: E = 0.34 + 0.0295 × (−2) = **+0.28 V**.

**Iron(III) / iron(II):**

$$E = 0.77 + 0.059\log\frac{[\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}]}{[\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}]}$$

If [Fe³⁺] is ten times [Fe²⁺]: E = 0.77 + 0.059 = +0.83 V.

## Cell potential and Gibbs free energy
$$\Delta G^\ominus = -nE^\ominus_{cell}F$$

n is the number of moles of electrons transferred in the cell reaction; F = 96 500 C mol⁻¹. The answer is in **joules** per mole.

**Zinc–copper cell:** ΔG⦵ = −2 × 1.10 × 96 500 = −212 300 J mol⁻¹ = **−212 kJ mol⁻¹**.

A **positive E⦵cell gives a negative ΔG⦵**: the two tests for feasibility agree.

S: pH is a logarithmic measure of hydrogen ion concentration. Strong acids give up all their protons; weak acids only a fraction, set by Ka. A buffer holds pH steady by pairing a weak acid with its conjugate base, and the solubility product Ksp sets the limit for sparingly soluble salts.
<<<
## Conjugate acid–base pairs
When an acid donates a proton, what is left is its **conjugate base**. When a base accepts a proton, it becomes its **conjugate acid**. A conjugate pair differs by **one H⁺**.

$$\mathrm{CH_3COOH + H_2O \rightleftharpoons CH_3COO^- + H_3O^+}$$

| Acid | Conjugate base |
| CH₃COOH | CH₃COO⁻ |
| H₃O⁺ | H₂O |
| NH₄⁺ | NH₃ |
| H₂O | OH⁻ |

Each equation has two pairs: acid 1 / base 1 and acid 2 / base 2.

## pH, Kw, Ka and pKa
$$\mathrm{pH} = -\log[\mathrm{H^+}] \qquad [\mathrm{H^+}] = 10^{-\mathrm{pH}}$$

$$K_w = [\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}] = 1.00 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{mol}^2\,\text{dm}^{-6}\ \text{at 298 K}$$

For a weak acid, $\mathrm{HA \rightleftharpoons H^+ + A^-}$:

$$K_a = \frac{[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]} \qquad \mathrm{p}K_a = -\log K_a$$

- $K_a$ has units of mol dm⁻³.
- A **larger $K_a$** (a **smaller p$K_a$**) means a **stronger** acid.
- $K_w$ is the ionic product of water. In pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³, so pH = 7.

## Calculating pH
### Strong acids
Fully dissociated: for a monoprotic acid, **[H⁺] = concentration of the acid**.

0.0200 mol dm⁻³ HCl: pH = −log(0.0200) = **1.70**.

### Strong alkalis
Fully dissociated: find [OH⁻], then use $K_w$.

$$[\mathrm{H^+}] = \frac{K_w}{[\mathrm{OH^-}]}$$

0.0500 mol dm⁻³ NaOH: [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 0.0500 = 2.00 × 10⁻¹³; pH = **12.70**.

### Weak acids
Two approximations: [H⁺] = [A⁻], and so little acid dissociates that [HA] at equilibrium equals its original concentration, c.

$$K_a = \frac{[\mathrm{H^+}]^2}{c} \qquad [\mathrm{H^+}] = \sqrt{K_a \times c}$$

0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid, $K_a$ = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³:

$$[\mathrm{H^+}] = \sqrt{1.74 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.100} = 1.32 \times 10^{-3} \qquad \mathrm{pH} = 2.88$$

To find $K_a$ from a pH: [H⁺] = 10^−pH, then $K_a$ = [H⁺]² ÷ c.

## Buffer solutions
!def Buffer solution | A solution that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or alkali are added.

### How to make one
- Mix a **weak acid with its salt** (its conjugate base): ethanoic acid and sodium ethanoate. This gives an acidic buffer.
- Or **partly neutralise** a weak acid with a strong alkali, leaving a mixture of the acid and its salt.
- A weak base with its salt, such as ammonia and ammonium chloride, gives an alkaline buffer.

### How it works
The buffer contains **large amounts (reservoirs) of both** the weak acid, CH₃COOH, and its conjugate base, CH₃COO⁻.

$$\mathrm{CH_3COOH \rightleftharpoons CH_3COO^- + H^+}$$

**Adding acid:** the extra H⁺ ions are removed by the conjugate base.

$$\mathrm{CH_3COO^- + H^+ \rightarrow CH_3COOH}$$

**Adding alkali:** the OH⁻ ions are removed by the weak acid.

$$\mathrm{CH_3COOH + OH^- \rightarrow CH_3COO^- + H_2O}$$

In each case the ratio [CH₃COOH] : [CH₃COO⁻] changes very little, so [H⁺] and the pH hardly change.

### Buffers in blood
Blood is kept at pH 7.35 to 7.45, mainly by **hydrogencarbonate ions, HCO₃⁻**.

$$\mathrm{H^+ + HCO_3^- \rightleftharpoons H_2CO_3 \rightleftharpoons CO_2 + H_2O}$$

Extra H⁺ is removed by HCO₃⁻; extra OH⁻ is removed by H₂CO₃ (or reacts with H⁺, and more H₂CO₃ dissociates). Buffers are also used in shampoos, food and medicines, and wherever enzymes need a constant pH.

### The pH of a buffer
$$[\mathrm{H^+}] = K_a \times \frac{[\mathrm{HA}]}{[\mathrm{A^-}]} \qquad \mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}$$

Moles can be used in place of concentrations, as both are in the same volume.

**Example.** A buffer contains 0.100 mol of ethanoic acid and 0.200 mol of sodium ethanoate. $K_a$ = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵.

$$[\mathrm{H^+}] = 1.74 \times 10^{-5} \times \frac{0.100}{0.200} = 8.70 \times 10^{-6} \qquad \mathrm{pH} = 5.06$$

When [HA] = [A⁻], pH = p$K_a$.

## Solubility product
!def Solubility product, Ksp | The product of the concentrations of the ions in a saturated solution of a sparingly soluble salt, each raised to the power of its number in the formula, at a given temperature.

| Salt | Equilibrium | $K_{sp}$ | Units |
| AgCl | $\mathrm{AgCl(s) \rightleftharpoons Ag^+ + Cl^-}$ | $[\mathrm{Ag^+}][\mathrm{Cl^-}]$ | mol² dm⁻⁶ |
| PbI₂ | $\mathrm{PbI_2(s) \rightleftharpoons Pb^{2+} + 2I^-}$ | $[\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}][\mathrm{I^-}]^2$ | mol³ dm⁻⁹ |
| Ag₂CrO₄ | $\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4(s) \rightleftharpoons 2Ag^+ + CrO_4^{2-}}$ | $[\mathrm{Ag^+}]^2[\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}}]$ | mol³ dm⁻⁹ |

The solid is not included.

### Calculations
Let the solubility be s mol dm⁻³.

- **AgCl:** [Ag⁺] = [Cl⁻] = s, so $K_{sp}$ = s². With $K_{sp}$ = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰: s = 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.
- **PbI₂:** [Pb²⁺] = s and [I⁻] = 2s, so $K_{sp}$ = s × (2s)² = **4s³**. With s = 1.5 × 10⁻³: $K_{sp}$ = 4 × (1.5 × 10⁻³)³ = 1.4 × 10⁻⁸ mol³ dm⁻⁹.

A precipitate forms when the ionic product of the mixed solutions is **greater than $K_{sp}$**.

## The common ion effect
!def Common ion effect | The reduction in the solubility of a sparingly soluble salt in a solution that already contains one of its ions.

Adding Cl⁻ ions (from NaCl) to a saturated solution of AgCl increases [Cl⁻]. The equilibrium $\mathrm{AgCl(s) \rightleftharpoons Ag^+ + Cl^-}$ moves to the **left**, so more AgCl precipitates: [Ag⁺] falls until [Ag⁺][Cl⁻] equals $K_{sp}$ again.

**Example.** Solubility of AgCl in 0.10 mol dm⁻³ NaCl:

$$[\mathrm{Ag^+}] = \frac{K_{sp}}{[\mathrm{Cl^-}]} = \frac{1.8 \times 10^{-10}}{0.10} = 1.8 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$$

This is far less than its solubility in pure water, 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.

## Partition
Two **immiscible** solvents, such as water and an organic solvent, form two layers. A solute that dissolves in both distributes itself between them. Molecules of solute move across the boundary in both directions until the two rates are equal: a **dynamic equilibrium**.

!def Partition coefficient, Kpc | The ratio of the concentrations of a solute in two immiscible solvents at equilibrium.

$$K_{pc} = \frac{[\text{solute in organic solvent}]}{[\text{solute in water}]}$$

- $K_{pc}$ has **no units**: the concentration units cancel.
- It is constant at a given **temperature**.
- It applies when the solute is in the **same physical state** (the same molecular form) in both solvents.
- The question states which solvent is on top of the ratio. Check it.

## Calculating Kpc
**Example 1.** 100 cm³ of water containing 0.400 g of iodine is shaken with 20.0 cm³ of an organic solvent. Afterwards 0.060 g of iodine remains in the water.

- in the organic layer: 0.400 − 0.060 = 0.340 g in 20.0 cm³: 0.0170 g cm⁻³
- in the water: 0.060 g in 100 cm³: 6.0 × 10⁻⁴ g cm⁻³

$$K_{pc} = \frac{0.0170}{6.0 \times 10^{-4}} = 28$$

Any concentration units can be used, as long as they are the same for both layers.

## Using Kpc
**Example 2.** 2.00 g of X is dissolved in 100 cm³ of water. It is shaken with 50.0 cm³ of ether. $K_{pc}$ (ether/water) = 4.00. What mass of X is extracted?

Let the mass extracted be m g.

$$4.00 = \frac{m / 50.0}{(2.00 - m) / 100}$$

$$4.00 = \frac{2m}{2.00 - m} \qquad 8.00 - 4.00m = 2m \qquad m = 1.33\ \text{g}$$

### Several small extractions are better than one large one
Using the 50 cm³ of ether as two separate 25 cm³ portions removes more X in total than using it all at once: each fresh portion sets up a new equilibrium with what is left in the water.

## Polarity
"Like dissolves like." The value of $K_{pc}$ depends on the **polarities of the solute and of the two solvents**.

| Solute | More soluble in | $K_{pc}$ (organic/water) |
| **non-polar**, e.g. iodine, I₂ | the **non-polar** organic solvent: only id–id forces are needed, and iodine cannot hydrogen-bond with water | **large** (much greater than 1) |
| **polar, hydrogen-bonding**, e.g. ammonia, ethanoic acid | **water**: it forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules | **small** (less than 1) |

A solute dissolves when the forces it can form with the solvent are similar in strength to the forces that had to be broken in the solute and in the solvent.

## Where partition is used
- **Solvent extraction:** removing an organic product from an aqueous reaction mixture with a separating funnel.
- **Chromatography:** components separate because they partition differently between the mobile and stationary phases.
- Predicting how a drug or pesticide distributes between water and fatty tissue.
