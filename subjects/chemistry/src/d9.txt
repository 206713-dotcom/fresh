## Primary amines
A **primary amine** has the functional group **–NH₂**: one alkyl group attached to the nitrogen atom.

| Name | Structural formula |
| methylamine | CH₃NH₂ |
| ethylamine | CH₃CH₂NH₂ |
| propylamine | CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ |
| butylamine | CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂NH₂ |

!mol Ethylamine
    H   H
    |   |       H
H - C - C - N <
    |   |       H
    H   H
!end

The nitrogen atom has a **lone pair**, so amines are bases and nucleophiles, like ammonia. The shape around the nitrogen is pyramidal.

## Making a primary amine
**Reagents and conditions:** a **halogenoalkane** with **NH₃ in ethanol**, **heated under pressure** (in a sealed tube).

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2Br + NH_3 \rightarrow CH_3CH_2NH_2 + HBr}$$

The hydrogen bromide reacts at once with more ammonia, so the overall equation is:

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2Br + 2NH_3 \rightarrow CH_3CH_2NH_2 + NH_4Br}$$

### Why these conditions
| Condition | Reason |
| **ethanol** as the solvent | it dissolves both the halogenoalkane and the ammonia; water would give an alcohol by hydrolysis |
| **heated under pressure** in a sealed tube | ammonia is a gas and would escape on heating in an open vessel |
| **excess ammonia** | to reduce further substitution |

### The mechanism
This is **nucleophilic substitution**. The **lone pair on the nitrogen** of ammonia attacks the **δ+ carbon** of the C–Br bond, and a bromide ion leaves. The intermediate, CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺, then loses a proton to a second ammonia molecule.

### Further substitution
The amine formed also has a lone pair on nitrogen, so it is a nucleophile too. It can attack another molecule of the halogenoalkane, giving a secondary amine, (CH₃CH₂)₂NH, and then a tertiary amine. Using a **large excess of ammonia** makes it much more likely that a halogenoalkane molecule meets ammonia than an amine, so the primary amine is the main product.

(Classifying amines as primary, secondary or tertiary is not tested at AS Level.)

## Where this fits
| Starting material | Reagent | Product |
| halogenoalkane | NH₃ in ethanol, heat under pressure | primary amine |
| halogenoalkane | KCN in ethanol, heat | nitrile |
| halogenoalkane | NaOH(aq), heat | alcohol |

All three are nucleophilic substitutions.

## Nitriles
The functional group is **–C≡N**. The carbon of the nitrile group counts in the name.

| Name | Structural formula | Carbons |
| ethanenitrile | CH₃CN | 2 |
| propanenitrile | CH₃CH₂CN | 3 |
| butanenitrile | CH₃CH₂CH₂CN | 4 |

## Making nitriles
**A halogenoalkane with KCN in ethanol, and heat.**

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2Br + KCN \rightarrow CH_3CH_2CN + KBr}$$

- The mechanism is **nucleophilic substitution**: the cyanide ion, :CN⁻, attacks the δ+ carbon.
- Ethanol is the solvent. In water, hydroxide ions would be present and an alcohol would form.
- The carbon chain becomes **one carbon longer**: bromoethane (2 C) gives propanenitrile (3 C).

## Making hydroxynitriles
**An aldehyde or ketone with HCN, KCN as catalyst, and heat.**

$$\mathrm{CH_3CHO + HCN \rightarrow CH_3CH(OH)CN}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3 + HCN \rightarrow (CH_3)_2C(OH)CN}$$

- The mechanism is **nucleophilic addition**.
- A hydroxynitrile has an **–OH group and a –CN group on the same carbon atom**.
- Again the chain is one carbon longer.

| Carbonyl compound | Hydroxynitrile |
| ethanal | 2-hydroxypropanenitrile |
| propanal | 2-hydroxybutanenitrile |
| propanone | 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile |

!mol 2-hydroxypropanenitrile
    H   H
    |   |
H - C - C - C = N     (C = N is a triple bond)
    |   |
    H   O - H
!end

## Hydrolysis of nitriles
The –CN group is converted to **–COOH**. The number of carbon atoms does not change.

### With dilute acid, and heat
$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CN + 2H_2O + HCl \rightarrow CH_3CH_2COOH + NH_4Cl}$$

The carboxylic acid forms directly, with an ammonium salt.

### With dilute alkali and heat, then acidification
$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CN + NaOH + H_2O \rightarrow CH_3CH_2COONa + NH_3}$$

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2COONa + HCl \rightarrow CH_3CH_2COOH + NaCl}$$

The salt of the acid forms first, and ammonia gas is given off. Adding a strong acid then gives the carboxylic acid.

### Hydroxynitriles
A hydroxynitrile is hydrolysed in the same way, to a **hydroxycarboxylic acid**.

$$\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH)CN + 2H_2O + HCl \rightarrow CH_3CH(OH)COOH + NH_4Cl}$$

2-hydroxypropanenitrile gives 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid).

## Why nitriles matter in synthesis
They provide a way to **lengthen a carbon chain by one carbon**.

| propanoic acid → propan-1-ol | LiAlH₄ | CH₃CH₂CH₂OH |

## Addition polymerisation
- The monomers are **alkenes** (they contain C=C).
- The **π bond** in each monomer breaks, and the monomers join through new **σ bonds** to form a long chain.
- **The polymer is the only product:** no small molecule is lost.
- The polymer is **saturated**, and its empirical formula is the same as that of the monomer.

### Poly(ethene)
$$n\,\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{-(CH_2{-}CH_2)_n-}$$

### Poly(chloroethene), PVC
$$n\,\mathrm{CH_2{=}CHCl} \rightarrow \mathrm{-(CH_2{-}CHCl)_n-}$$

!mol Chloroethene and the repeat unit of PVC
  H       Cl        /  H   Cl \
   \     /          |  |   |  |
n   C = C     ->   -+- C - C -+-
   /     \          |  |   |  |
  H       H         \  H   H  / n
!end

## Deducing the repeat unit from the monomer
- Draw the monomer with the C=C in the middle and the four groups above and below.
- Change the **double bond to a single bond**.
- Add a bond at **each end**, passing through the brackets.
- Write **n** after the bracket.

| Monomer | Repeat unit |
| ethene, CH₂=CH₂ | –CH₂–CH₂– |
| propene, CH₂=CHCH₃ | –CH₂–CH(CH₃)– |
| chloroethene, CH₂=CHCl | –CH₂–CHCl– |
| phenylethene, CH₂=CHC₆H₅ | –CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)– |
| tetrafluoroethene, CF₂=CF₂ | –CF₂–CF₂– |
| but-2-ene, CH₃CH=CHCH₃ | –CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)– |

The repeat unit of an addition polymer always has **two carbon atoms in the chain**. Anything else attached to the double-bond carbons becomes a side group.

## Identifying the monomer from a section of polymer
- Find the **repeating pattern**: take **two adjacent carbon atoms** of the backbone, with their side groups.
- Put a **double bond** between them and remove the bonds at the ends.

A chain –CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH(CH₃)– has the repeat unit –CH₂–CH(CH₃)–: the monomer is propene.

If the side groups alternate in a pattern of four backbone carbons, such as –CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CHCl–, two different monomers have been used: here ethene and chloroethene.

## Disposal of poly(alkene)s
Poly(alkene)s are very unreactive: they have strong, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, like alkanes. This makes them useful, but hard to dispose of.

| Problem | Detail |
| **non-biodegradable** | microorganisms cannot break them down, so they persist for hundreds of years in **landfill** sites and as litter, on land and in the oceans |
| **harmful combustion products** | burning releases **carbon monoxide** when combustion is incomplete, and carbon dioxide; **PVC** also gives toxic, acidic **hydrogen chloride** |

Ways to reduce the problem: recycling, burning in incinerators that capture the harmful gases and use the energy released, and developing degradable polymers.

## Devising a route
- Compare the **start** and the **target**: which functional groups change? Does the **number of carbons** change?
- Work **backwards** from the target: what could it be made from in one step?
- Look for a common intermediate. **Halogenoalkanes and alcohols** are the hubs: most routes go through one of them.
- To **add a carbon**, go through a **nitrile** (from a halogenoalkane) or a **hydroxynitrile** (from a carbonyl compound).

**Example 1.** Ethene to ethanoic acid.

- ethene → ethanol: steam, H₃PO₄ catalyst
- ethanol → ethanoic acid: acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, reflux

**Example 2.** Ethanol to propanoic acid (one more carbon).

- ethanol → bromoethane: HBr (or KBr with concentrated H₂SO₄)
- bromoethane → propanenitrile: KCN in ethanol, heat
- propanenitrile → propanoic acid: dilute HCl, heat

**Example 3.** Propene to propanone.

- propene → propan-2-ol: steam, H₃PO₄ catalyst (major product, through the secondary carbocation)
- propan-2-ol → propanone: acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, heat

**Example 4.** 1-bromopropane to propyl propanoate.

- 1-bromopropane → propan-1-ol: NaOH(aq), heat
- part of the propan-1-ol → propanoic acid: acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, reflux
- propan-1-ol + propanoic acid → propyl propanoate: concentrated H₂SO₄, heat

## Analysing a given route
For each step, state:

- the **type of reaction** (and mechanism, if asked)
- the **reagents and conditions**
- any **by-products**, the other substances formed alongside the organic product.

| Step | By-product |
| alcohol + PCl₅ | POCl₃, HCl |
| alcohol + SOCl₂ | SO₂, HCl |
| halogenoalkane + NH₃ | NH₄X |
| esterification | H₂O |
| acid hydrolysis of a nitrile | ammonium salt |
| free-radical substitution | HX, and further-substituted products |
| HBr + unsymmetrical alkene | the minor isomer |

A route with fewer steps, higher yields and harmless by-products is the better route.

S: Bonds vibrate and absorb infrared radiation at characteristic wavenumbers, so an infrared spectrum is a quick check on which functional groups a molecule contains. The key regions are the broad O–H bands above 2500 cm⁻¹ and the strong C=O peak near 1700 cm⁻¹.

## How it works
- Covalent bonds **vibrate**: they stretch and bend.
- A bond **absorbs infrared radiation** of the frequency that matches its vibration.
- Different bonds absorb at different frequencies, which depend on the **strength of the bond** and the **masses of the atoms**.
- The spectrum plots **transmittance (%)** against **wavenumber (cm⁻¹)**. Absorptions appear as **dips** (troughs), which are called peaks.

## Absorptions to recognise
The exact ranges are in the Data Booklet. Use it in every question.

| Bond | Found in | Wavenumber / cm⁻¹ | Appearance |
| **O–H** | alcohols | **3200–3650** | **broad**, strong |
| **O–H** | carboxylic acids | **2500–3000** | **very broad** |
| **N–H** | amines | 3300–3500 | medium; sharper than O–H |
| **C–H** | alkanes, most organic compounds | 2850–2950 | medium |
| **C≡N** | nitriles | **2200–2250** | sharp |
| **C=O** | aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, esters | **1670–1750** | **strong, sharp** |
| **C=C** | alkenes | 1500–1680 | weak to medium |
| **C–O** | alcohols, esters, carboxylic acids | 1040–1300 | strong |

The O–H bands are broad because of **hydrogen bonding**.

Below about 1500 cm⁻¹ is the **fingerprint region**: a complicated pattern that is unique to each compound and is used to identify it by comparison with known spectra.

## Reading a spectrum
- Look first near **1700 cm⁻¹**: is there a strong, sharp **C=O** peak?
- Look above **2500 cm⁻¹**: is there a **broad O–H** band? Where exactly?
- Check for C≡N near 2250 cm⁻¹ and N–H near 3400 cm⁻¹.
- Ignore the C–H peaks near 2900 cm⁻¹: nearly every organic compound has them.
- Combine what you find.

| O–H band | C=O peak | Class of compound |
| broad, 3200–3650 | absent | **alcohol** |
| absent | present | **aldehyde, ketone or ester** (an ester also has a strong C–O peak) |
| very broad, 2500–3000 | present | **carboxylic acid** |
| absent | absent | alkane, alkene or halogenoalkane |

Always quote the **bond**, the **wavenumber** and the **functional group**: "the strong peak at 1715 cm⁻¹ is due to C=O in a ketone".

## Following a reaction
Infrared spectra show whether a reaction has worked.

| Reaction | Change in the spectrum |
| oxidising propan-2-ol to propanone | the broad O–H band at 3200–3650 cm⁻¹ **disappears**; a strong C=O peak near 1700 cm⁻¹ **appears** |
| oxidising ethanol to ethanoic acid | C=O appears; the O–H band shifts and broadens to 2500–3000 cm⁻¹ |
| making an ester from an acid and an alcohol | the O–H bands disappear; C=O remains |
| reducing a ketone to an alcohol | C=O disappears; broad O–H appears |

## Limits
Infrared spectroscopy shows which bonds are present, not how the atoms are arranged. Propanal and propanone both show C=O, so other evidence, such as a chemical test or a mass spectrum, is needed to tell them apart.

## What a mass spectrum shows
- Molecules are ionised to **positive ions**, usually with a 1+ charge.
- The ions are separated by their **mass-to-charge ratio, m/e**.
- The spectrum plots **relative abundance** against **m/e**. For a 1+ ion, m/e equals the mass.

(How the instrument works is not required.)

## Relative atomic mass of an element
For an element, each peak is one **isotope**. The height of the peak is its relative abundance.

$$A_r = \frac{\sum (m/e \times \text{abundance})}{\sum \text{abundances}}$$

**Example.** Neon gives peaks at m/e 20 (90.9%), 21 (0.3%) and 22 (8.8%).

$$A_r = \frac{(20 \times 90.9) + (21 \times 0.3) + (22 \times 8.8)}{100} = 20.2$$

## The molecular ion
!def Molecular ion, M⁺ | The ion formed when a molecule loses one electron.

$$\mathrm{C_2H_5OH + e^- \rightarrow [C_2H_5OH]^+ + 2e^-}$$

The **molecular ion peak** is the peak with the **highest m/e** (apart from the small isotope peaks). Its m/e value is the **relative molecular mass**.

## Fragmentation
The molecular ion can break into a smaller **positive ion**, which is detected, and a neutral radical, which is not.

$$\mathrm{[CH_3CH_2OH]^+ \rightarrow CH_2OH^+ + {}^\bullet CH_3}$$

| m/e | Fragment ion |
| 15 | CH₃⁺ |
| 17 | OH⁺ |
| 28 | CO⁺ or C₂H₄⁺ |
| 29 | C₂H₅⁺ or CHO⁺ |
| 31 | CH₂OH⁺ |
| 43 | C₃H₇⁺ or CH₃CO⁺ |
| 45 | COOH⁺ |
| 57 | C₄H₉⁺ or C₂H₅CO⁺ |

A **loss** from the molecular ion is also informative.

| Peak at | Lost | Suggests |
| M − 15 | CH₃ | a methyl group |
| M − 17 | OH | an alcohol or acid |
| M − 29 | C₂H₅ or CHO | an ethyl group or an aldehyde |
| M − 45 | COOH | a carboxylic acid |

Always write a fragment ion **with its positive charge**.

**Example.** Propanone, CH₃COCH₃ ($M_r$ = 58): peaks at 58 (M⁺), 43 (CH₃CO⁺, loss of CH₃) and 15 (CH₃⁺). Its isomer propanal, CH₃CH₂CHO, gives 29 (C₂H₅⁺ and CHO⁺) instead: fragmentation distinguishes isomers.

## The M+1 peak: counting carbon atoms
About 1.1% of carbon atoms are carbon-13. A molecule with one ¹³C atom is one unit heavier, giving a small **M+1 peak**. The more carbon atoms, the bigger this peak.

$$n = \frac{100 \times \text{abundance of M+1 ion}}{1.1 \times \text{abundance of M}^+ \text{ ion}}$$

n is the number of carbon atoms.

**Example.** The M⁺ peak has an abundance of 40.0% and the M+1 peak 1.76%.

$$n = \frac{100 \times 1.76}{1.1 \times 40.0} = 4$$

The molecule has four carbon atoms.

## The M+2 peak: chlorine and bromine
Chlorine and bromine each have two common isotopes, two mass units apart.

| Element | Isotopes | Natural ratio | Pattern for one atom in the molecule |
| **chlorine** | ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl | 3 : 1 | **M : M+2 = 3 : 1** |
| **bromine** | ⁷⁹Br and ⁸¹Br | 1 : 1 | **M : M+2 = 1 : 1** |

Chloroethane, C₂H₅Cl, gives peaks at m/e 64 (with ³⁵Cl) and 66 (with ³⁷Cl) with heights 3 : 1. Bromoethane gives peaks at 108 and 110 of equal height.

### Two halogen atoms
There are M, M+2 and M+4 peaks.

| Atoms | M : M+2 : M+4 |
| two chlorine | 9 : 6 : 1 |
| two bromine | 1 : 2 : 1 |

Fragments that still contain the halogen show the same pattern.

## Putting it together
- **M⁺ peak:** the relative molecular mass.
- **M+1 peak:** the number of carbon atoms.
- **M+2 peak:** chlorine (3 : 1) or bromine (1 : 1).
- **Fragments:** pieces of the structure.
- Use the infrared spectrum for the functional groups, and combine everything.
