## Electronegativity
!def Electronegativity | The power of an atom to attract electrons to itself.

It is measured on the **Pauling scale**. Fluorine, the most electronegative element, has a value of 4.0.

| H 2.1 | | | | | | |
| Li 1.0 | Be 1.5 | B 2.0 | C 2.5 | N 3.0 | O 3.5 | F 4.0 |
| Na 0.9 | Mg 1.2 | Al 1.5 | Si 1.8 | P 2.1 | S 2.5 | Cl 3.0 |

Values are given in the question when they are needed.

## Factors affecting electronegativity
| Factor | Effect |
| **nuclear charge** | more protons attract the bonding electrons more strongly: **higher** |
| **atomic radius** | the bonding electrons are further from the nucleus: **lower** |
| **shielding** by inner shells and sub-shells | inner electrons reduce the attraction for the bonding electrons: **lower** |

## Trends
### Across a period: increases
- **Nuclear charge increases.**
- **Atomic radius decreases**, and the **shielding is similar** because the electrons are in the same shell.
- The nucleus attracts the bonding pair more strongly.

### Down a group: decreases
- **Atomic radius increases**: the bonding pair is further from the nucleus.
- **Shielding increases**, as there are more inner shells.
- This outweighs the greater nuclear charge.

The most electronegative elements are at the top right of the Periodic Table: F, O, N and Cl.

## Predicting the type of bond
| Difference in electronegativity | Type of bond | Example |
| **0** (or very small) | **non-polar covalent**: the electrons are shared equally | Cl–Cl (0), C–H (0.4) |
| **small to medium** (up to about 1.7) | **polar covalent**: the electrons are shared unequally | H–Cl (0.9), O–H (1.4) |
| **large** (about 1.8 or more) | **ionic**: an electron is transferred | Na and Cl (2.1), Mg and O (2.3) |

- A **metal with a non-metal** usually has a large difference: ionic.
- **Two non-metals** have a small difference: covalent.

The change from covalent to ionic is gradual: the bigger the difference, the more polar the bond.

## Ionic bonding
!def Ionic bonding | The electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions (positively charged cations and negatively charged anions).

- A **metal** atom **loses** electrons to form a **cation**.
- A **non-metal** atom **gains** electrons to form an **anion**.
- The ions usually have the electronic configuration of a noble gas.
- The attraction acts in **all directions**, so the ions form a **giant ionic lattice**.

## Three examples
| Compound | Electron transfer | Ions formed |
| **sodium chloride, NaCl** | one electron from Na to Cl | Na⁺ (2,8) and Cl⁻ (2,8,8) |
| **magnesium oxide, MgO** | two electrons from Mg to O | Mg²⁺ (2,8) and O²⁻ (2,8) |
| **calcium fluoride, CaF₂** | one electron from Ca to each of two F atoms | Ca²⁺ (2,8,8) and two F⁻ (2,8) |

$$\mathrm{Na \rightarrow Na^+ + e^-} \qquad \mathrm{Cl + e^- \rightarrow Cl^-}$$

In a dot-and-cross diagram for an ionic compound, show each ion in square brackets with its charge outside, and show the transferred electron with the other atom's symbol (a dot among crosses).

## Strength of ionic bonding
The attraction is stronger when:

- the ions have a **greater charge**
- the ions have a **smaller radius**, so that they are closer together.

MgO (2+ and 2− ions, both small) melts at 2852 °C. NaCl (1+ and 1− ions) melts at 801 °C.

## Properties that follow
| Property | Explanation |
| **high melting and boiling points** | strong electrostatic attraction between the ions, throughout the lattice, needs a lot of energy to overcome |
| **conduct when molten or in solution, not when solid** | the ions are free to move when molten or dissolved; in the solid they are held in fixed positions |
| **brittle** | a force moves layers of ions so that like charges are next to each other; they repel and the crystal splits |
| **often soluble in water** | polar water molecules are attracted to the ions and surround them |

## Metallic bonding
!def Metallic bonding | The electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons.

- Each metal atom loses its **outer-shell electrons**, which become **delocalised**: free to move throughout the structure.
- The atoms become **positive ions**, arranged in a regular **giant metallic lattice**.
- The attraction between the ions and the delocalised electrons acts in all directions.

## Strength of metallic bonding
The bonding is stronger when:

- there are **more delocalised electrons** per atom
- the ion has a **greater charge**
- the ion has a **smaller radius**.

| Metal | Ion | Delocalised electrons per atom | Melting point / °C |
| Na | Na⁺ | 1 | 98 |
| Mg | Mg²⁺ | 2 | 650 |
| Al | Al³⁺ | 3 | 660 |

## Covalent bonding
!def Covalent bonding | The electrostatic attraction between the nuclei of two atoms and a shared pair of electrons.

| Molecule | Bonds |
| H₂ | one single bond, H–H |
| O₂ | one double bond, O=O |
| N₂ | one triple bond, N≡N |
| Cl₂ | one single bond |
| HCl | one single bond |
| CO₂ | two double bonds, O=C=O |
| NH₃ | three single bonds; one lone pair on N |
| CH₄ | four single bonds |
| C₂H₆ | one C–C and six C–H single bonds |
| C₂H₄ | one C=C double bond and four C–H single bonds |

### Expanding the octet
Elements in **Period 3** can have more than eight electrons in their outer shell, because they can use their 3d orbitals.

| Molecule | Electrons around the central atom |
| **SO₂** | 10 (two S=O double bonds and one lone pair) |
| **PCl₅** | 10 (five single bonds) |
| **SF₆** | 12 (six single bonds) |

Period 2 elements cannot do this: nitrogen forms NCl₃ but not NCl₅.

## Coordinate (dative covalent) bonding
!def Coordinate bond | A covalent bond in which both electrons of the shared pair come from the same atom.

It needs one atom with a **lone pair** and another with a **vacant orbital**. It is drawn as an arrow from the donor atom. Once formed, it is the same as any other covalent bond.

### The ammonium ion
Ammonia reacts with hydrogen chloride gas: the lone pair on the nitrogen forms a coordinate bond to an H⁺ ion.

$$\mathrm{NH_3 + HCl \rightarrow NH_4^+Cl^-}$$

!mol Coordinate bond in the ammonium ion
     H                    [     H     ] +
     |                    [     |     ]
 H - N :   +  H+   ->     [ H - N -> H ]
     |                    [     |     ]
     H                    [     H     ]
!end

All four N–H bonds in NH₄⁺ are identical.

### Aluminium chloride, Al₂Cl₆
In AlCl₃ the aluminium atom has only six outer electrons. Two AlCl₃ molecules join: a lone pair on a chlorine atom of each molecule forms a coordinate bond to the aluminium atom of the other.

!mol The Al₂Cl₆ molecule: two coordinate bonds
 Cl        Cl         Cl
   \     /    \      /
     Al         Al
   /     \    /      \
 Cl        Cl         Cl
!end

The two bridging chlorine atoms each form one ordinary covalent bond and one coordinate bond (Cl → Al).

## σ and π bonds
| Bond | How it forms | Electron density |
| **σ (sigma)** | **direct (end-on) overlap** of orbitals between the bonding atoms | along the line between the two nuclei |
| **π (pi)** | **sideways overlap of adjacent p orbitals** | **above and below** the σ bond |

- A single bond is one σ bond.
- A double bond is one σ and one π bond.
- A triple bond is one σ and two π bonds.
- A π bond is weaker than a σ bond, because the sideways overlap is less effective.

| Molecule | σ bonds | π bonds |
| H₂ | 1 (overlap of two 1s orbitals) | 0 |
| C₂H₆ | 7 | 0 |
| C₂H₄ | 5 | 1 |
| HCN (H–C≡N) | 2 | 2 |
| N₂ (N≡N) | 1 | 2 |

## Hybridisation
Atomic orbitals mix to form new **hybrid orbitals** of equal energy before bonding.

| Hybridisation | Orbitals mixed | Hybrid orbitals | Arrangement | Bond angle | Example |
| **sp³** | one s + three p | 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | C in CH₄, C₂H₆ |
| **sp²** | one s + two p | 3 (one p orbital left over) | trigonal planar | 120° | C in C₂H₄ |
| **sp** | one s + one p | 2 (two p orbitals left over) | linear | 180° | C in HCN, CO₂ |

The hybrid orbitals form σ bonds. The p orbitals left over form π bonds.

In ethene, each carbon is sp² hybridised. The sp² orbitals form σ bonds to two hydrogens and the other carbon; the remaining p orbitals overlap sideways to form the π bond.

## Bond energy and bond length
!def Bond energy | The energy required to break one mole of a particular covalent bond in the gaseous state.

!def Bond length | The internuclear distance of two covalently bonded atoms.

| Bond | Bond energy / kJ mol⁻¹ | Bond length / nm |
| C–C | 350 | 0.154 |
| C=C | 610 | 0.134 |
| C≡C | 840 | 0.120 |
| N≡N | 945 | 0.110 |

- A **shorter bond is a stronger bond**: the nuclei attract the shared electrons more strongly.
- A molecule with **strong bonds is less reactive**: more energy is needed to break them. Nitrogen is unreactive because of its very strong N≡N bond.
- Down Group 17, the hydrogen halides become more reactive (HCl < HBr < HI) as the H–X bond gets longer and weaker.

## VSEPR theory
Valence shell electron-pair repulsion theory:

- the **pairs of electrons** around the central atom **repel** each other
- they arrange themselves **as far apart as possible**, to minimise repulsion
- **lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs**.

$$\text{lone pair–lone pair} > \text{lone pair–bonding pair} > \text{bonding pair–bonding pair}$$

Each lone pair reduces the bond angle by about 2.5°. A double or triple bond counts as **one** region of electrons.

## The seven shapes to know
| Molecule | Bonding pairs | Lone pairs | Shape | Bond angle |
| **CO₂** | 2 (double bonds) | 0 | **linear** | **180°** |
| **BF₃** | 3 | 0 | **trigonal planar** | **120°** |
| **CH₄** | 4 | 0 | **tetrahedral** | **109.5°** |
| **NH₃** | 3 | 1 | **pyramidal** | **107°** |
| **H₂O** | 2 | 2 | **non-linear** (bent) | **104.5°** |
| **PF₅** | 5 | 0 | **trigonal bipyramidal** | **120° and 90°** |
| **SF₆** | 6 | 0 | **octahedral** | **90°** |

!mol Tetrahedral methane and non-linear water
      H                      ..

      C                    /   \
    / | \                 H     H
   H  H  H               104.5 degrees
  109.5 degrees

## Working out a shape
- Find the number of **outer electrons** on the central atom (its group number, minus 10 for Groups 13 to 18).
- Add **one for each bonded atom** (one electron per single bond).
- For an ion: **add one** for each negative charge, **subtract one** for each positive charge.
- **Divide by two** to get the number of electron pairs.
- Pairs that are not bonding pairs are **lone pairs**.
- Choose the arrangement, then name the shape from the positions of the **atoms** only.

## Explaining a bond angle
For ammonia: "There are **three bonding pairs and one lone pair** around the nitrogen atom. The four pairs repel each other and are arranged tetrahedrally. The **lone pair repels more strongly than the bonding pairs**, so the bond angle is reduced from 109.5° to 107°."

For water: two lone pairs give even more repulsion, so the angle is 104.5°.

## Van der Waals' forces
!def Van der Waals' forces | The intermolecular forces between molecular entities, other than those due to bond formation. The term covers all intermolecular forces.

### Instantaneous dipole – induced dipole (id–id) forces
Also called **London dispersion forces**. They exist between **all** molecules and atoms.

- Electrons move constantly, so at any instant the electron cloud may be unevenly spread: an **instantaneous dipole**.

- The two dipoles **attract** each other.

- the molecule has **more electrons** (a larger, more polarisable electron cloud)
- there are **more points of contact** between molecules. Straight-chain molecules pack closer than branched ones, so they have higher boiling points.

Between **polar** molecules. The δ+ end of one molecule attracts the δ− end of the next. For molecules with a similar number of electrons, pd–pd forces make the boiling point higher.

## The origin of gas pressure
- Gas molecules move **randomly** and at high speed.
- They **collide with the walls** of the container.
- Each collision exerts a force on the wall. **Pressure is the force per unit area** from all these collisions.

More frequent or harder collisions give a higher pressure:

| Change | Effect on pressure | Reason |
| higher temperature (fixed volume) | increases | the molecules have more kinetic energy: they hit the walls **more often** and **harder** |
| smaller volume (fixed temperature) | increases | the molecules are closer together: they hit the walls **more often** |
| more gas (fixed volume and temperature) | increases | more molecules: more collisions |

## Ideal gases
An ideal gas is one that obeys pV = nRT under all conditions. Its molecules:

- have **zero particle volume**
- have **no intermolecular forces of attraction**
- move randomly, in straight lines between collisions
- collide **elastically**: no kinetic energy is lost.

### Real gases
Real molecules do have a volume and do attract each other. A real gas behaves most like an ideal gas at **high temperature** and **low pressure**:

- at high temperature the molecules have enough kinetic energy for the forces between them to be negligible
- at low pressure the molecules are far apart, so their own volume is negligible compared with the volume of the container, and the forces between them are very small.

Real gases deviate most at **low temperature and high pressure**. Small, non-polar molecules with weak intermolecular forces, such as helium and hydrogen, are the closest to ideal.

## The ideal gas equation
$$pV = nRT$$

| Symbol | Quantity | Unit that must be used |
| p | pressure | **Pa** |
| V | volume | **m³** |
| n | amount of gas | **mol** |
| R | molar gas constant | 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ |
| T | temperature | **K** |

### Conversions
| From | To | Do this |
| kPa | Pa | × 1000 |
| dm³ | m³ | ÷ 1000 (× 10⁻³) |
| cm³ | m³ | ÷ 1 000 000 (× 10⁻⁶) |
| °C | K | + 273 |

**Example.** Calculate the volume of 0.500 mol of gas at 25 °C and 101 kPa.

$$V = \frac{nRT}{p} = \frac{0.500 \times 8.31 \times 298}{101\,000} = 0.0123\ \text{m}^3 = 12.3\ \text{dm}^3$$

## Finding a relative molecular mass
Since $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$:

$$pV = \frac{mRT}{M} \qquad M = \frac{mRT}{pV}$$

**Example.** 0.320 g of a gas occupies 120 cm³ at 300 K and 100 kPa.

- V = 120 × 10⁻⁶ = 1.20 × 10⁻⁴ m³; p = 1.00 × 10⁵ Pa
- $n = \dfrac{pV}{RT} = \dfrac{1.00 \times 10^5 \times 1.20 \times 10^{-4}}{8.31 \times 300} = 4.81 \times 10^{-3}$ mol
- $M = \dfrac{0.320}{4.81 \times 10^{-3}} = 66.5$ g mol⁻¹, so $M_r$ = **66.5**

This method is used for gases and for volatile liquids: a weighed sample is vaporised in a gas syringe and its volume, temperature and pressure are measured.

| Lattice | Particles | Held together by | Examples |

## Giant ionic lattices
**Sodium chloride:** a cubic lattice. Each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by **six** Cl⁻ ions, and each Cl⁻ by six Na⁺ ions. **Magnesium oxide** has the same structure, with Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions.

## Simple molecular lattices
- **Iodine:** I₂ molecules in a regular arrangement, held by weak **id–id forces**. It sublimes on gentle heating.
- **Buckminsterfullerene, C₆₀:** each molecule is a hollow sphere of 60 carbon atoms, in hexagons and pentagons, like a football. Each carbon is bonded to **three** others. The molecules are held together by weak id–id forces, so it has a low sublimation point and is soft. The delocalised electrons cannot move from one molecule to the next, so it is a poor conductor.
- **Ice:** each water molecule is **hydrogen-bonded** to **four** others, in an open, tetrahedral arrangement.

| Substance | Structure | Properties |
| **diamond** | each carbon covalently bonded to **four** others, **tetrahedrally** | very hard; very high melting point; does not conduct: no free electrons |
| **graphite** | each carbon bonded to **three** others, in **layers of hexagons**; weak id–id forces between the layers; **one delocalised electron per carbon** | soft and slippery: the layers slide; **conducts electricity** along the layers; high melting point |
| **silicon(IV) oxide, SiO₂** | each silicon bonded to **four** oxygens, each oxygen to **two** silicons, tetrahedrally | hard; high melting point; does not conduct |

## Giant metallic lattices
**Copper:** a regular, close-packed arrangement of Cu²⁺ ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. It conducts well, is malleable and ductile, and has a high melting point.

Q: [3] Explain why diamond has a much higher melting point than iodine.
+ diamond is giant molecular: strong covalent bonds throughout :: covalent
+ iodine is simple molecular: weak intermolecular (id–id) forces between I₂ molecules :: intermolecular|van der waals|id-id|dispersion|weak forces
+ much more energy is needed to break the covalent bonds than to overcome the intermolecular forces :: energy
A: Diamond has a giant molecular structure, in which every atom is held by strong covalent bonds. Iodine is simple molecular, with only weak intermolecular forces between the I₂ molecules. Much more energy is needed to break the covalent bonds than to overcome the intermolecular forces.
Q: [3] Describe the structure of graphite and use it to explain why graphite is soft and conducts electricity.
+ layers (of hexagons), each carbon bonded to three others :: layer
+ weak forces between the layers, so the layers can slide :: slide|weak
+ delocalised electrons are free to move :: delocal|free electron
A: Graphite consists of layers of carbon atoms, each bonded to three others. There are only weak forces between the layers, so they can slide over each other. Each carbon has one delocalised electron, which is free to move and carry charge.

+ simple molecular :: simple molecular|simple covalent|molecular

A: It is simple molecular. The low melting point shows that only weak intermolecular forces hold the molecules together, and it does not conduct because it has no mobile ions or electrons.
